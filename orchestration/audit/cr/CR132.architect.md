## 3. The distinction the track is built on

Lesson 366 separates two numbers that are routinely conflated:

- **18.6%** of the actively-trading Taiwan subgroup profit in a given year (luck
  included) — 81.4% lose.
- **Under 1%** are reliably profitable: ranked on the prior year, re-tested out of
  sample, net of fees.

Its quizzes teach the difference explicitly, because collapsing them is precisely how
one good year becomes "I have an edge". Worth verifying that the lesson never lets the
stricter number do the weaker number's work, or vice versa.

Note for your own cross-check: other papers in this literature report ~3% "predictably
profitable" on a *different* definition. The lesson is quoting the persistence cut of
the Cross-Section of Speculator Skill paper. If you think the attribution is loose,
say so.

# Round 2 — the BLOCKER is confirmed, and my round-1 clearance of it was wrong

`VERDICT: AWAITING_FIXES (round 1)` — 1 BLOCKER on lesson 368's return-gap figures.

**The auditor is right and my own §2 defence of this number was wrong.** I flagged
the figure as suspicious, "verified" it, and cleared it. What I actually verified was a
**search-engine summary of the SSRN working-paper abstract**, which does carry 1.4 and
2.3. The lesson's `sources:` cites the **published QJE 116(1), 2001** version. The
auditor fetched that PDF from the author's own faculty page and read §III.A, §III.B and
Table IV directly. Reading the primary source beats reading a summary of a different
version of it, and that is the whole reason this lane exists.

Two things I got wrong, stated plainly:

1. `2.3` appears nowhere in the cited paper under any of its three measures.
2. `1.4` is real but is the **singles** own-benchmark figure, not the full-sample one —
   so the lesson attached the widened number to the un-widened comparison.

The auditor also caught something neither my draft nor my review noticed and which is
worse than the magnitude error: the paper states raw net-return differences between men
and women are **not statistically significant** (16.3% vs 16.9%, reversed in sign), so
"men earned lower returns" was wrong in *direction*, not just size.

## The fix

Now uses the cited version's own headline measure — the cost trading imposes on net
return — with the paper's own numbers:

- full sample: 2.65 pp/yr for men vs 1.72 for women → a **0.93 pp** gap
- singles: the gap widens to **1.44 pp**

The lesson's argument is unchanged and is now better supported: the gap still widens
when household division-of-labour is ruled out (0.93 → 1.44). Body text, the hypothesis
sentence ("earn less on a risk-adjusted basis" → "pay more for it", which was left
dangling once the measure changed) and **Quiz 1's marked-correct answer and explanation**
are all updated together — the quiz was the serious part, since it graded the wrong
pairing as correct.

The explanation now also states the not-statistically-significant point, so the lesson
teaches the distinction rather than silently avoiding the trap.

## Guard against recurrence

`sources:` for this lesson now carries an explicit **do-not-use** note naming the
1.4/2.3 pair, saying they are the working-paper abstract's, and stating that raw returns
must never be written as "men earn less". Provenance is internal-only, so this costs the
reader nothing and puts the warning where the next author looks. It is the cheapest
available guard for a class — *a widely-circulated figure from a superseded version of
the cited paper* — that no test can catch.

## Round-2 addendum — the second auditor pass sharpened the BLOCKER, and it vindicates the fix

A second track-U pass landed on the same lane and refined the finding: the full-sample
**`1.4` is real** — footnote 21's three-factor Fama-French model gives "1.4 percent
annually" for the full sample — so only the **singles `2.3`** was unsupported. Narrower
than round 1 stated.

That refinement **confirms the one-measure fix rather than undercutting it.** The same
pass reports there is **no published three-factor singles breakdown**. So keeping `1.4`
would have left the singles half with no counterpart under the same measure, and the
only way to state both numbers would have been to mix measures across the sentence —
which is precisely the error that produced the bug. Using the abstract's trading-cost
measure end to end (2.65 / 1.72 → 0.93, widening to 1.44) is the version where both
halves come from one measure and both are quotable from the paper's own abstract.

Also worth recording, because it is the mechanism and not just this instance: that pass
found **WebSearch echoed the wrong "1.4 / 2.3" pairing verbatim across two independent
queries with no citation**, directly contradicting the abstract text it claims to
summarise. That is exactly how I cleared it in round 1. **Search results are not a
primary source, and a figure that a search engine repeats confidently is not
corroborated — it is the same uncited claim twice.** The `sources:` do-not-use note now
names that pair specifically, which is the only guard available for a class no test can
catch.

## MINOR (round 2, second pass) — lesson 369 overstated Huang & Chan

The lesson said institutions "showed no such pattern". The paper shows proprietary
futures firms **do** exhibit a break-even effect; what differs is that theirs does not
move market volatility and liquidity the way individual traders' does. Fixed — the
lesson now states the difference is in market impact, not immunity. It reads as a
better lesson for it: "professionals are not immune, their mistakes just cost the market
less" is truer and more useful to this audience than "professionals don't do this".
