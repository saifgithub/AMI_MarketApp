## The MAJOR — "so the safety floor permits it" reads as reassurance, not fact

The full sentence: *"(Concentration: this is 50.0% of the portfolio in one
name. Your mandate's single-name cap is 100.0%, so the safety floor
permits it.)"*

The first clause is unambiguous fact. The second is what you asked me to
weigh, and I think it crosses. "Permits" is an authorization word — it
carries the same connotation in "the safety floor permits it" that it
does in "the law permits it" or "your license permits it": not just
*this does not violate a rule*, but *this is sanctioned*. A user who just
watched a 12-analyst team argue for putting half their simulated
portfolio in one name, then reads "so the safety floor permits it,"
has a ready, natural inference available: *the system checked this and
says it's fine*. That inference is a recommendation-shaped conclusion the
sentence itself did not have to hand the user — it's exactly the "caution
one comma away" you flagged, just arrived at through authorization
language rather than a caution word.

**Compare it against its own siblings.** You wrote in §2 that this is
"same idiom, same voice" as the existing parenthetical disclosures. It
isn't, and the difference is instructive: `"(sized down to 40.0% —
mandate risk-tier ceiling.)"` and `"(stop/target not stated by the PM…)"`
both state **what happened**, with no evaluative word attached. `"so the
safety floor permits it"` states **an evaluative outcome** — permitted vs.
not — which is a different rhetorical move even though it's still,
technically, a true fact about what the deterministic check returned.

**The banned-word test can't see this, and that's exactly the gap you
flagged going in.** None of `should / recommend / advise / consider
reducing / too much / risky / dangerous / warning / careful / we suggest`
appears, and "permits" was never a candidate for that list because it
isn't advice-shaped in isolation — it only reads as reassurance in this
specific sentence, sitting right after the concentration figure, as the
sentence's rhetorical conclusion.

**Not prescribing the exact rewrite, but offering the shape of one** since
it costs little to consider: keep the same two facts (the cap value, and
that this is why the verdict wasn't a PASS) without the authorization
verb — e.g. *"Your mandate's single-name cap is 100.0%; this position
does not exceed it."* That preserves the information a user needs (why
did this APPROVE rather than get blocked) without supplying the
inference that it's therefore a good idea.
