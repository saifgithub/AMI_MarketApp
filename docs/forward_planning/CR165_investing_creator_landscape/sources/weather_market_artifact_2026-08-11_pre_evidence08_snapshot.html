If we can predict the weather, why can’t we predict the market better than a coin toss?

A review of roughly twenty studies on how well professional firms, sell-side analysts, individual investors and investment groups actually forecast security prices — what the measured record shows, why it is this poor, and why the field cannot easily tell skill from luck even when it exists.

Opening

A question worth asking precisely

Weather forecasting works. The usable range of a forecast has extended by roughly one day per decade — today’s one-week forecast is about as good as a three-day forecast was in 1980.S Ask a meteorologist how accurate they are and you get a number, because the field has graded itself daily since the 1970s.

Ask the same question of the people paid to forecast security prices and the answer is much harder to obtain. This report is about that answer: what the measured record actually shows for four populations that forecast markets for a living or for money, and what explains it.

The short version is that the record is poor almost everywhere it has been measured, that the reasons are structural rather than a matter of effort or intelligence, and that the most interesting finding is not about accuracy at all — it is about how little of this is knowable in the first place.

Evidence 01

The firms: professional asset managers

This is the best-measured population in finance. S&P’s SPIVA scorecards have tracked active managers against their benchmarks for twenty-five years, with survivorship correction, published by the index provider itself.

Over ten years, 84.3 % of active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500. Over fifteen, 89.5 %.S Roughly one professional team in ten beats a passive index over fifteen years, and about one in twelve over twenty.

The year-by-year series is rarely shown, and it is the more interesting object.

Does the past predict the future here?

Barely, and possibly less than chance. S&P’s Persistence Scorecard found that of the top-quartile domestic equity funds as of December 2020, not one remained top-quartile over the following four years; earlier editions found zero large-, mid- or multi-cap funds holding top-quartile status across five.S

For scale: pure chance would leave about 0.39 % of them top-quartile four years running — roughly one fund in a cohort of 250. So observing zero is consistent with chance, not worse than it, and it would take a top-quartile cohort of some 766 funds before zero survivors became statistically surprising at all. The honest reading is not that persistence is unusually bad. It is that past quartile rank carries no detectable information about future quartile rank — which is the more useful finding anyway.

Is there skill underneath the fees?

Yes — and it is almost exactly consumed by cost. Fama and French bootstrapped the whole cross-section of US mutual fund returns and found that on net returns few funds cover their costs, while on gross returns there is genuine non-zero alpha in the extreme tails, both good and bad.S Their own summary: a portfolio of low-cost index funds performs about as well as the top 3 % of active funds, and better than the rest.

Two supporting results explain the mechanism. Berk and Green model skill as real but captured by fund flows — assets chase the skilled manager until net alpha to the investor approaches zero, so the skill accrues to the manager rather than the client. Carhart showed that most apparent persistence disappears once momentum is controlled for: the hot hand was factor exposure.S

Skill exists. It is roughly the size of the fee charged to access it.

The strongest case against everything above

A fair review has to put the other side, and there is a serious one. Berk and van Binsbergen argue that measuring skill by net alpha is measuring the wrong thing entirely — because, as Berk and Green showed, net alpha is set in equilibrium by competition between investors chasing the manager, not by the manager’s ability. A skilled manager attracts money until the edge to the next investor is gone. Net alpha near zero is therefore what you should expect whether or not skill exists.

Measuring instead the dollar value a fund extracts from markets, they find the average mutual fund generates about $3.2 million a year, and that cross-sectional differences in that value persist for as long as ten years — their conclusion being that they find it hard to reconcile the results with anything other than the existence of genuine skill.P

How to hold both

These findings are not actually in conflict; they answer different questions. SPIVA asks did an investor beat the index after fees — overwhelmingly no. Berk and van Binsbergen ask did the manager extract value from the market — often yes, persistently. Both can be true, and together they say something sharper than either alone: skill is real, and it is captured by the manager and the fund rather than delivered to the client.

It also means the persistence result above is narrower than it looks. Quartile rank of net returns does not persist. Dollar value added, on this evidence, does.

Evidence 02

The analysts: sell-side research

Sell-side research teams publish the closest thing markets have to a weather forecast: a named prediction with a number and a deadline — a rating and a twelve-month price target.

Across 2000–2009, roughly 38 % of those targets were met at the end of the twelve-month horizon and 64 % at some point during it. Absolute forecast errors averaged about 45 %, and target-implied returns exceeded actual returns by about 15 %.S

The authors’ explanation is the part worth carrying. Earnings-forecast accuracy is scrutinised by the market and feeds analyst compensation. Target-price accuracy is neither scrutinised nor compensated. So nobody optimises it, and the numbers look like the numbers of a metric nobody optimises.

Do the recommendations move anything?

Mostly not. Across I/B/E/S data from 1993–2006, only about 12 % of analyst recommendation changes are “influential” enough to visibly move the affected stock.S Seven in eight published calls are read, distributed, and economically inert.

What separated the influential ones is instructive: they came from star analysts, they departed from consensus, and they carried a specific numeric earnings forecast. Being differentiated and being concrete are what made a call matter.

Is there money in following them?

Gross, yes; net, no. The best-documented result found that from 1986–1996 the most highly recommended stocks returned 18.8 % annualised against 5.78 % for the least favoured, and a long-short strategy returned about 75 basis points per month after adjusting for size, book-to-market and momentum.

The same paper reports that capturing it requires daily rebalancing and immediate reaction to rating changes, and that net of transaction costs the abnormal returns are “not reliably greater than zero.” A later re-examination found the effect inverted in 2000–2001, with the least-favoured stocks outperforming.

A structural bias worth naming

Analyst ratings are heavily long-skewed. In the 1986–1996 sample above, 54 % of all recommendations were buys and only 7 % were sells — a distribution shaped by banking relationships and management access rather than by the distribution of investment merit.P Ratings also drifted more bullish across the decade, the average falling from 2.37 in 1986 to 2.04 in 1996 on a five-point scale where 1 is a strong buy. Notably, brokers issuing the fewest buy ratings produced the most profitable upgrades: selectivity correlated with signal.S

Evidence 03

The individuals

Barber and Odean studied 66,465 households at a large discount broker from 1991 to 1996. The market returned 17.9 % annualised; the average household earned 16.4 %; the most active quintile earned 11.4 %.S Average annual portfolio turnover was 75 %.

The scale replicates internationally. Using the complete trading history of every investor in Taiwan, the same group measured an aggregate annual penalty of −3.8 percentage points for individuals against +1.5 for institutions — losses equivalent to about 2.2 % of Taiwan’s GDP.S Virtually all of it traced to aggressive, marketable orders; passive limit orders were profitable at short horizons.

Among the day-trading subset, fewer than 1 % of the population is able to “predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees.”P That is the authors’ own wording, from a fifteen-year study of every day trader on the Taiwan Stock Exchange, 1992–2006.

The counterintuitive part

None of this is a stock-picking failure. As the next section shows directly, individual investors’ gross selection beat a passive index fund. The deficit is created entirely by the act of trading — frequency, spread, and the disposition to sell winners and hold losers. That is a behavioural finding, not an analytical one.

Evidence 04

The groups: investment clubs

This is the finding that most deserves attention, and the one least often quoted.

Barber and Odean examined 166 investment clubs at the same brokerage between February 1991 and January 1997 — groups that met, researched collectively, debated, and voted before buying. Exactly the structure that is supposed to improve on the lone amateur.

60 % of the clubs underperformed the index. The average club returned 14.1 % net against the market’s 17.9 % — and, critically, against the average individual investor’s 16.4 %.P

The group did worse than the lone amateur it was made of.

The group also picked worse, not just traded more

It would be comforting to attribute the club shortfall entirely to turnover. The data does not allow it. Clubs’ own-benchmark gross abnormal return — performance measured against the portfolio they held at the start of the year, which isolates the effect of their trading decisions — was −0.106 percentage points per month, significant at 5 %. In the authors’ words, the stocks clubs chose to buy performed worse than the stocks they chose to sell.

Net of costs, clubs underperformed their own beginning-of-year portfolio by 3.5 points a year, with Jensen’s alpha at −4.8 and the Fama-French intercept at −4.4.P Clubs also tilted toward small-cap growth stocks with high market risk, and turned over 65 % of their portfolios annually.

Why collective research made things worse

The group decision-making literature offers three well-replicated mechanisms, and the club data exhibits all three.

Shared-information bias. Groups spend discussion time on what members already hold in common and systematically under-surface information held by only one person. The distinctive evidence — the entire reason to convene several people — is the least likely thing to reach the table.

Premature consensus. Groups converge quickly, and dissent is socially expensive to voice. The objection that would have caught the error goes unsaid.

Manufactured activity. A club that meets monthly needs something to decide. Given that trading volume is the documented cause of individual underperformance, a meeting cadence is a machine for producing the exact behaviour that destroys returns.

Analysis 05

Why accuracy and profit come apart

A forecaster who calls rain correctly 60 % of the time is straightforwardly better than one who manages 50 %. In markets that inference fails, because the outcome distribution is extraordinarily skewed.

Hendrik Bessembinder examined every US common stock in the CRSP database since 1926. The majority had lifetime buy-and-hold returns below one-month Treasury bills. Only 47.8 % of all monthly stock returns exceeded the Treasury-bill rate in the same month, and fewer than half were positive at all. The best-performing 4 % of listed companies account for the entire net gain of the US stock market since 1926; the other 96 % collectively matched Treasury bills.P

His own conclusion is the operative one: this “helps to explain why poorly diversified active strategies most often underperform market averages.” A concentrated portfolio has a median outcome below the index even with no difference in skill, because the index captures the rare enormous winners and a short list probably does not.

You can be right more often than not and still lose — the losses are ordinary, the wins are rare and extreme.

This is why a bare hit rate is close to meaningless as a measure of forecasting quality in equities, and why any honest scorecard has to report the distribution of outcomes alongside the frequency of being right.

Analysis 06

The deeper problem: almost none of this is knowable

Suppose a manager or an analyst genuinely does have an edge. How long before anyone could demonstrate it? This turns out to be the binding constraint on the entire field, and it is pure arithmetic.

To establish that a hit rate of 55 % differs from a 50 % base rate — at conventional 5 % significance and 80 % power — takes about 783 independent observations. A three-point edge takes over two thousand.

The word independent is doing heavy lifting, and it makes the real requirement worse rather than better. Picks made in the same period are correlated with each other through shared exposure to the market: when it falls, most of them fall together. The effective sample size is therefore materially below the nominal count, so the figures below are a floor, not a target.

For risk-adjusted returns it is dramatically worse. The t-statistic on a Sharpe ratio grows only with the square root of elapsed time, so the track record required to show that a manager’s Sharpe differs from zero is measured in decades.

Read that against the persistence data. A manager with genuine but ordinary skill could work an entire career and retire before the evidence of that skill became statistically distinguishable from luck. The profession cannot reliably identify its own best practitioners within a working lifetime — which means it cannot select for skill, which means skill does not compound the way it does in fields with fast feedback.

And the coin is not fair

One correction to the framing in the title, because it matters more than it first appears. “Better than a coin toss” implies a stable 50 % baseline. There is no such thing.

S&P’s own measurement found that 62 % of S&P 500 constituents beat the index in the first quarter of 2025 — and 29 % in the second.P Consecutive quarters, thirty-three points apart. The base rate for “a randomly chosen stock beats the benchmark” is not a constant; it moves violently, and it is skewed within each window.

So a forecaster evaluated in one quarter can look brilliant and in the next look broken, with no change whatsoever in method. Any honest evaluation has to measure the base rate over the same window from the same universe rather than importing a number.

Findings 07

What does work

The literature is not uniformly bleak. Two findings point at something real, and both are about process rather than prediction.

Structured disagreement beats consensus. Schweiger, Sandberg and Ragan had managers make strategic decisions under three protocols: dialectical inquiry (two opposed positions, argued in full), devil’s advocacy (one assigned attacker), and consensus. Both structured-conflict methods produced significantly higher-quality decisions and surfaced significantly better assumptions than consensus.S

There is a sting in the tail, and it explains the club result precisely. Members of the structured-conflict groups reported more re-evaluation of their own assumptions but lower satisfaction, lower acceptance of the group’s decision, and less desire to keep working together. Consensus feels best and performs worst. A voluntary group of humans drifts toward the thing that feels good — agreement — which is exactly what an investment club does, and exactly why sixty percent of them lost to the index.

Calibration is measurable, and separable from accuracy. The forecasting-tournament literature finds that top forecasters achieve Brier scores roughly 20–30 % below the median, that teams outperform individuals, and that statistical aggregation helps ordinary forecasters most and top performers least.S Note what is being scored: not whether the forecast made money, but whether the stated confidence was honest.

The measurable skill in forecasting is not being right. It is knowing how sure you are.
Conclusion

Back to the weather

The comparison in the title is not quite fair, and the ways it is unfair are the findings of this report.

A hurricane does not read the forecast; a stock does, and moves toward it, destroying the opportunity being described. Weather payoffs are roughly symmetric, while equity payoffs are so skewed that being right and making money come apart. A weather forecast resolves in days, unambiguously, hundreds of times a year, while a manager’s edge may take four decades to distinguish from luck.

But one difference is not structural at all, and it is the one worth ending on. Meteorology built an entire subdiscipline whose only job is to score forecasters against outcomes and publish the result. Financial forecasting largely did not: price-target accuracy is neither tracked nor compensated, seven in eight recommendation changes move nothing, and top-quartile status predicts almost nothing about the next four years.

It would be tidy to say that verification caused the improvement, and that is more than the evidence supports: weather forecasting got better largely on the back of satellites, numerical models and several orders of magnitude more computing power — none of which finance lacks. What verification did was make improvement legible. It let the field tell which changes helped and discard the ones that did not. A discipline that cannot separate a good forecast from a lucky one has no way to choose between competing methods, so whatever skill it has does not compound.

That part is fixable, and it does not require anyone to become a better forecaster first. It requires publishing the forecast, fixing the horizon in advance, scoring it against what happened, and reporting the sample size next to the result. It is not a sophisticated idea. It is an uncomfortable one, because most of the early scores would be bad.

Method

How this was assembled, and how far to trust it

This is secondary research: a review of published studies, with no new data collected. Given that the subject is partly the discipline of grading forecasts, it would be poor form not to grade the sourcing.

P marks a figure taken from a primary document opened and read. S marks one verified across multiple independent secondary reports, where the primary was paywalled, auth-gated, or returned an error to automated retrieval.

Known weaknesses

Two of the most-quoted figures here are S. The SPIVA ten- and fifteen-year numbers (84.3 % and 89.5 %) come from year-end scorecards whose PDFs could not be retrieved; the annual series charted above is primary. The 38 %/64 % price-target figures come from a paywalled 2013 paper — an earlier, readable working-paper version by two of the same authors reports a range of 24–45 % met for its own sample, which contains 38 % but is not the same claim.

Publication bias applies throughout: in this literature, studies finding no forecasting skill are easier to publish than studies finding it, and genuinely profitable private evidence would not appear here by construction. The strongest results reported above are also among the oldest — several of the individual-investor datasets are from the 1990s, and market structure has changed since.

This review was also assembled by searching, which surfaces famous results, and the famous results in this field are the pessimistic ones. That is a selection bias in the bibliography, not just in the underlying literature. The counter-case in “The strongest case against everything above” was added specifically to correct for it; readers should assume more counter-evidence exists than is represented here.

Nothing here is investment advice, and none of it should be read as a prediction about any market or security. It is a review of how a forecasting discipline evaluates itself.
