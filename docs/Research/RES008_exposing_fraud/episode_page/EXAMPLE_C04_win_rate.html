A 90% win rate does not prove a strategy works

The claim, as it is usually made: “My strategy wins 90% of its trades — that is how you know it works.”

DISPROVED A win rate is set by where you put the target and the stop, not by whether the entries know anything — entries carrying no information at all reached 89.2% wins while losing money.
89.2%win rate from entries carrying no information (0.5 : 5 bracket)
−0.33%expectancy per trade at that same setting, after costs (interval −0.39 … −0.28)
2.6 ptslargest gap between the measured win rate and stop ÷ (target + stop), across seven settings

What we wrote down before testing

We wrote the test down before any test code existed, including what would have counted as support for the claim: if any bracket setting reached 85% wins and made money after costs on entries chosen at random, then a high win rate would carry information on its own, and we would have said so. We also wrote down three predictions about what we expected to see. The pre-registration is public and dated: commit 3ac320eb, 2026-09-17.

What happened

Win rate against bracket geometry, from out/results.json (part1_by_geometry). 27 instruments × 7 geometries × 2,000 random trades = 378,000 trades.

  • The win rate is a dial. Across seven target-and-stop settings the measured win rate never sat more than 2.6 points from stop ÷ (target + stop) — a number you can compute before placing a single trade. Entries carried no information: random bars, random side.
  • The 89% setting loses money. A small target with a wide stop (0.5 : 5) won 89.2% of its trades and returned −0.33% of price per trade after costs, with the interval entirely below zero.
  • Reverse the bracket and the same entries win 18.9%. Nothing about the entries changed. Only where the target and the stop were placed.
  • A few dozen trades is not a measurement. 22 wins in 25 trades — a convincing-looking “88%” — has a 95% interval of 70% to 96%. And a strategy whose true win rate is 70% shows at least 22 wins in 25 about half the time, if you are allowed twenty attempts and report the best one.

What is true, and what we cannot say

  • A win rate is not useless — it is half of a calculation. With the average win, the average loss and the number of trades, it tells you something. On its own it tells you almost nothing.
  • This does not say any particular 90% strategy loses money. It says a 90% win rate cannot be the evidence that it works. Those are different claims, and we tested only the second.
  • Our fill rules are deliberately conservative — when one bar spans both target and stop we take the stop, and a gap through the stop fills at the worse price. That pushes zero-information entries slightly below zero even before costs. The test asks whether any geometry comes out above zero, and none did.
  • One honest oddity we did not set out to find. In the two cryptocurrencies, the low win-rate settings made money on random entries: the 5 : 1 bracket won 25.3% of trades and returned +1.70% per trade. Two coins and one history — not a pre-registered test, and not something to trade on. It is the claim upside down.

Check the next one yourself

  1. Where is the target, and where is the stop? A high win rate with a wide stop is arithmetic, not evidence.
  2. What is the average win against the average loss? Without it, a win rate is half a sentence.
  3. How many trades is that measured on? Twenty-five trades cannot separate 70% from 96%.
  4. How many variants were tried before this one was shown? The best of twenty attempts looks remarkable even when nothing is.

The same five questions apply to any trading claim: how to read a trading claim.
