# Futures & forwards

Calls and puts give one side a right and the other an obligation. Futures and forwards skip the right entirely — both sides are obligated from the start, with no premium paid for the choice to walk away. A futures contract is the standardized, exchange-traded, daily-settled version of that obligation; a forward is its private, customized, over-the-counter cousin. After this lesson you'll be able to explain why an obligation-based contract behaves fundamentally differently from an option, especially when a position moves hard against you.

## Example

A wheat farmer and a food company might enter a forward contract to lock in a delivery price six months out — customized, privately negotiated, off-exchange, and only as reliable as the counterparty's ability to honor it. Compare that to CME wheat futures: standardized contract size, standardized expiry, and a clearinghouse in the middle marking positions to market every single day, so gains and losses settle in cash daily instead of accumulating unseen until expiry. Both sides in a futures contract have accepted an obligation — unlike an option, nobody paid a premium for the right to walk away.

Bursa Malaysia Derivatives lists its own standardized futures, like FCPO (crude palm oil) and FKLI (FTSE Bursa Malaysia KLCI futures) — but retail access to *single-stock options*, the kind this module has focused on, is far more limited in Malaysia than in the US, where listed single-stock options trade on nearly every liquid large-cap ticker.

## The trap

Beginners treat futures as "just leveraged stock" and don't grasp the mandatory daily cash settlement — mark-to-market. A losing futures position can trigger a margin call demanding more cash well before any "expiry," unlike a long option, where the maximum loss is fixed the moment the premium is paid. The correct mental model: an option's worst case is capped by contract design; a futures position's worst case is only capped by how far the market is willing to move against you before you're forced to close.

## The steelman

A skeptic might argue: since both futures and options can deliver leveraged directional exposure, the distinction between "right" and "obligation" is academic — in practice both get closed out before expiry and the real-world payoff looks similar. The falsifier: compare margin-call behavior directly. A long option's downside is contractually capped at the premium paid, full stop, no matter how violently the market moves against it overnight. A futures position has no such floor — daily mark-to-market can demand additional margin repeatedly, and a large enough adverse move can force liquidation for far more than any comparable option premium would have cost. The clearest real-world falsifier: on April 20, 2020, front-month WTI crude futures traded below $0 per barrel intraday — an obligation-based contract produced a loss with no floor at zero, something no long option position can ever do.

## Takeaway

A future is an obligation with no built-in floor — mark-to-market can call for cash before you ever reach expiry — while a long option's worst case was fixed the moment you paid the premium; know which one you're looking at before you judge the risk.

*Where this comes from: CME Group's standard futures contract specifications — the daily mark-to-market mechanism that defines how a futures obligation differs from an option's right.*
