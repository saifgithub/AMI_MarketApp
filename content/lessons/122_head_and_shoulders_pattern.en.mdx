# Head and shoulders pattern

This assumes you've completed 023 (support and resistance) and 024 (breakouts). Here we go deeper on the most-cited multi-bar chart pattern: the head and shoulders. It's a three-peak reversal structure that forms after an uptrend — a left shoulder (a swing high), a higher head (the highest peak), and a right shoulder (a lower high than the head, roughly level with the left shoulder). The lows between the peaks form a "neckline". A daily close below the neckline triggers the pattern. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify the structure, draw the neckline correctly, compute the measured-move target, and state the exact invalidation rule.

## Example

Look at TSLA on the daily chart from late January to mid-April 2026. TSLA topped at $278.40 on 28 January (left shoulder), pulled back to $254.60 by 12 February (first neckline low), rallied to a higher high of $289.20 on 4 March (head), pulled back to $258.80 by 19 March (second neckline low), then rallied weakly to $277.10 on 1 April (right shoulder — roughly the same level as the left shoulder, distinctly lower than the head).

Neckline: draw a line between the two pullback lows — $254.60 on 12 February and $258.80 on 19 March. That's a slight upward slope; at the time of the right shoulder peak on 1 April, the neckline projects to roughly $262.

On 8 April TSLA closed at $258.40 — a daily close below the projected neckline. Pattern triggered.

**Measured move target.** The height of the pattern from head to neckline = $289.20 minus ~$257 (neckline midpoint) ≈ $32. Subtract that height from the neckline-break price: $258.40 minus $32 ≈ $226. That's the textbook measured-move target.

By 22 April TSLA was at $224.60 — within $1.40 of the textbook target. A trader entering short on the neckline-break close at $258.40, with a stop above the right shoulder at $279, risked $20.60 to a target of $226 — a $32.40 reward, roughly 1:1.6 R/R, plus the option to scale out at $240 for a higher partial-R/R first take.

The pattern invalidates the moment a daily candle closes back above the right shoulder ($277.10 for TSLA). At that close, the structural reversal is voided and any short thesis dies.

## The trap

The trap is calling every "three peaks" structure a head and shoulders. Most aren't. Real head-and-shoulders patterns have (1) a clear prior uptrend to reverse, (2) a head that's *distinctly* higher than both shoulders — typically 3–8% — and (3) shoulders that are roughly level with each other, within about 2% in price terms. A "head" that's only 0.5% above the shoulders isn't a head, it's a range top. Worse trap: shorting on the right-shoulder peak before the neckline breaks. Until the neckline closes through, the pattern is *potential*, not triggered. Many would-be H&S patterns evaporate without ever breaking the neckline. The correct mental model: a head and shoulders has two events — the visual shape (necessary but not sufficient) and the neckline break (the actual trigger). Wait for the close.

## Try it

Open a chart for any stock that topped out in the past six months. Look for a three-peak structure where the middle peak is distinctly higher. If you find one, ask the Market Analyst in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "Is this a valid head-and-shoulders? Draw the neckline, compute the measured-move target, and state the right-shoulder invalidation price." Save the trigger, target, and invalidation to your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> — even if the pattern didn't trigger, document why so you can recognise the shape sooner next time.

## Takeaway

Head and shoulders is a two-event pattern — visible shape plus neckline-break close — with a measured-move target equal to the head-to-neckline height; the structural invalidation is always a close back above the right shoulder.
