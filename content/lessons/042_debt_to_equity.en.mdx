# Debt-to-equity

Debt-to-equity captures how much of a company's financing comes from creditors versus owners. Formula: D/E = total debt / total shareholders' equity. It's a leverage gauge. Higher D/E means more amplification — better returns when things work, faster ruin when they don't. By the end of this lesson you should be able to read D/E in the right sector context, pair it with interest-coverage and free-cash-flow checks, and name the specific debt-structure red flag that turns a "manageable" balance sheet into a crisis.

## How this plays out in real markets

AAPL in fiscal 2024 carried roughly $100B in total debt against ~$65B in shareholders' equity, giving D/E ≈ 1.5x. That number alone screams "highly levered" — but AAPL also generated over $110B in annual free cash flow, meaning the entire debt stack could be retired in under a year if needed. The ratio in isolation lied; coverage made the picture safe. Compare TSLA in fiscal 2024: total debt around $13B, equity around $66B, D/E ≈ 0.2x — much "lower leverage" by the ratio, but TSLA's cash generation is more cyclical and the equity base includes substantial recent capital raises. The headline ratio favours TSLA; the durability of the underlying cash favours AAPL.

Banks live in a different universe. JPM carries D/E above 10x because banks fund loans with deposits and wholesale debt — leverage is the business model, not a flaw. Comparing JPM's D/E to AAPL's is meaningless; banks should be evaluated by capital ratios (CET1, tier-1) rather than corporate D/E.

Now an example of how D/E hides debt structure: a company with D/E of 1.0x where 80% of the debt matures in the next 18 months is in real trouble if credit markets tighten — even though the ratio looks moderate. Two companies with identical D/E can have radically different risk profiles based on (a) maturity ladder, (b) fixed vs. floating rate, (c) currency mismatch with revenue.

On Bursa Malaysia: TENAGA, a regulated utility, carries D/E in the 0.8–1.0x range — high for a non-financial but normal for a capital-intensive regulated business with predictable cash flow. AIRASIA's D/E ballooned to multiples of equity during COVID as losses eroded equity and debt rose; that combination — equity shrinking while debt grows — is the textbook distress signature.

Falsifiable claim: a non-financial company with D/E above 2.0x AND interest-coverage (EBIT / interest expense) below 3.0x is a credit risk that should be flagged regardless of the headline P/E or ROE. Either condition alone might be benign; both together is the pattern that precedes most non-cyclical bankruptcies.

## The trap

The trap is reading D/E as a single safety score and stopping. A beginner sees D/E of 0.5x and concludes "low debt, safe." But 0.5x can hide three landmines: (1) most of the debt comes due within 24 months and refinancing isn't guaranteed, (2) the debt is in a currency the company doesn't earn revenue in (a quiet killer for emerging-market issuers), (3) operating leases and off-balance-sheet obligations were excluded from the ratio under older accounting standards. The correct mental model: D/E is the headline; debt structure is the substance. Always check the maturity ladder (when does it come due?), interest coverage (can current EBIT pay the interest?), and free-cash-flow trend (is the cushion growing or shrinking?). What invalidates the signal: a "low" D/E achieved by *equity issuance* rather than debt paydown means shareholders bought their way to a better ratio — accretive only if the new capital earned a return above the cost of equity.

## Takeaway

Debt-to-equity is the headline; debt structure, interest coverage, and cash flow are the substance. A "low" ratio can hide refinancing risk; a "high" ratio can be safe if the cash flow covers it many times over.
