# The Greeks — delta, theta, vega intuition

An option's price isn't one number moving for one reason — it's three forces pulling at once. Delta is how much the option's price shifts when the underlying moves. Theta is how much value the option bleeds just from time passing, with no price move at all. Vega is how much the option's price shifts when the market's expectation of future volatility changes. You don't need the formulas to use this lesson — you need the intuition that all three are always active on any position you hold, not just the one you happen to be watching. After this lesson you'll be able to explain, in plain language, why an option can lose value even when you called the stock's direction correctly.

## Example

Ahead of AAPL's earnings report, the market bids up the price of calls and puts alike — even ones far from the current price — because implied volatility (vega's target) rises when an outsized move looks more possible. That's vega at work: pure anticipation, no price move required. Meanwhile, every option in that chain is also losing a little value every single day just from the calendar ticking forward — that's theta, and it never pauses, earnings or not. After the earnings print, even if AAPL moves in the anticipated direction, options can lose value overnight if the volatility crush (vega snapping back down once the uncertainty resolves) outweighs the price movement (delta) — especially if the move wasn't as large as what was priced in.

## The trap

Beginners treat the Greeks as three unrelated stats an option happens to have, rather than three forces actively fighting each other in real time. The common mistake: buying a short-dated option ahead of a known event, expecting delta to pay off if the direction call is right — and then losing money even when the direction call *is* right, because theta was decaying the position every hour and vega gave back more in the crush than delta gained from the move. The correct mental model: every option position is delta, theta, and vega happening simultaneously, and a "correct" read on one of the three doesn't protect you from being wrong, or just outpaced, on the other two.

## The steelman

The strongest case against learning Greeks intuition: "I don't need decay curves — I just need to be right about direction, and the payoff diagram already tells me what happens at expiry." The falsifier: watch what happens to that same option's price *before* expiry, not just at it. Two identical-strike options on the same stock, with different days left to expiry, the near-dated one moves a far larger percentage on the same stock move, because its smaller premium levers the same delta gain — and their prices diverge further as theta and vega act. If direction were the whole story, both would move the same way by the same amount. They don't.

## Try it

Open the option chain view (or ask the Technical Strategist) for a ticker with earnings coming up in the next two weeks. Compare the premium on a near-dated option versus a longer-dated option at the same strike — the gap between them is the market pricing theta and vega, not just delta.

## Takeaway

Delta tells you how an option reacts to the stock moving, theta tells you what it costs you just to wait, and vega tells you how much the market's fear of a big move is baked into the price — all three are working on your position at once, not just the one you happen to be watching.
