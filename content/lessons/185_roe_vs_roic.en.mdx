# ROE vs ROIC — why ROIC matters more for capital-heavy businesses

Lesson 041 set ROE as "the closest single number to what this business is compounding shareholder capital at." This lesson sharpens that claim. ROE measures return on the equity slice of the capital stack; ROIC (return on invested capital) measures return on the *whole* capital stack — equity plus debt. For capital-heavy businesses, the gap between the two tells you whether ROE is real operating excellence or financial-engineering noise. By the end you should be able to compute ROIC, recognise which businesses demand it, and name the falsifiable condition under which a high ROE with a low ROIC is a sell signal.

## How this plays out in real markets

ROIC = NOPAT (net operating profit after tax) / invested capital, where invested capital = equity + interest-bearing debt − cash. The intuition: how much pre-financing operating profit does each dollar of total capital generate, before management decides how to split it between debtholders and shareholders?

**XOM (Exxon) at a moderate-oil-price moment**: ROE around 16%, ROIC around 12%. The 4-point gap reflects modest debt financing. Both numbers are above the cost of capital; the business produces real economic return regardless of capital structure.

**F (Ford)**: ROE often reported around 12–15%, ROIC closer to 4–6%. The gap is wide because Ford carries massive finance-arm debt — billions of dollars of vehicle loans that lift ROE through the equity multiplier but don't move ROIC much. The honest read of "business quality" is the 4–6% ROIC, against an 8–10% cost of capital. Ford has, in many years, been a value-destroying business at the *operating* level, supported by financing-arm spread.

**MSFT**: ROE ~36%, ROIC ~28%. Modest gap (mostly because MSFT carries some debt and significant cash). Both numbers far above cost of capital — genuine operating excellence regardless of how you measure.

**NFLX in 2018–2020**: reported ROE 25%+, ROIC roughly 8–10% — because Netflix was funding content with debt that inflated equity-side returns. The gap signalled what wasn't yet visible in headline ROE: the underlying capital productivity was modest until streaming scale arrived.

Falsifiable claim: for capital-intensive businesses (utilities, telecoms, autos, airlines, miners, integrated oil), ROIC is the load-bearing return metric — not ROE. A high ROE with low ROIC means the difference is financing leverage, not operational productivity, and the long-term return to shareholders converges toward ROIC over a full cycle. On Bursa: TENAGA reports ROE around 7–9%; ROIC closer to 5–6% on a heavily-leveraged utility balance sheet. The narrow gap and the absolute level (close to cost of capital) explain why utilities trade at modest multiples — the underlying capital productivity is by design just-above-cost.

## The trap

The trap is using ROE alone for capital-heavy businesses and concluding "good ROE = good business." Auto OEMs, telecoms, airlines, capital-intensive industrials — each can report 15–20% ROE on operations that earn 4–8% ROIC, simply by stacking interest-bearing debt against equity. The 15% ROE compounds shareholder capital *only as long as the leverage sticks*; the moment a credit shock arrives or refinancing terms harden, the ROE collapses faster than the headline ever suggested. The correct mental model: for any business with debt-to-equity above 1.0x, compute ROIC alongside ROE. If ROIC is at or below cost of capital while ROE is high, the business is creating value for *bondholders and management*, not for shareholders. What invalidates the signal: financial companies (banks, insurers) are special — the debt-like funding (deposits, premiums) is the raw material of the business, not balance-sheet engineering, and ROIC as commonly defined doesn't apply. For banks, use ROE with cost of equity (lesson 181) instead.

## Takeaway

ROE is a financing-aware return; ROIC is the operating truth. For capital-heavy businesses, ROIC vs cost of capital is the load-bearing read — high ROE on low ROIC is borrowed glory.
