# Recovery scams: the second-victim pattern

After the initial loss, a second wave arrives. "Asset recovery specialists", "crypto recovery consultants", "blockchain forensic investigators" reach out — often referencing the specific platform that the victim lost on, sometimes with surprisingly detailed knowledge of the victim's case. Most are operated by the same networks that ran the original scam or by adjacent networks who buy victim lists. They charge an upfront retainer to "trace the funds" and disappear with it. This lesson teaches the recovery-scam architecture, why the contact detail is sometimes accurate (you are on a list), and the rule for legitimate post-loss action: regulators and law enforcement do not contact victims by DM and do not require upfront retainers.

## How this plays out in real markets

The FBI's IC3 and FTC have both warned publicly about recovery-scam patterns. The FCA's ScamSmart resources and SC Malaysia have issued parallel warnings. The architecture documented across cases:

- **Victim-list trafficking.** When the original scam collapses, the operator's database of victims — names, deposits, platform identifiers — is sold or shared to other scam operators. New approaches arrive within days to weeks. The contact often references the specific platform, deposit amount, or losses, which manufactures the impression of inside knowledge.
- **Authority impersonation.** The recovery operator presents as a government agent, regulator, law-enforcement officer, or law firm. Sometimes the impersonated entity is real (a named SEC investigator, a UK police unit). Verification fails the same contact-detail match as in clone-firm scams — the email is from a free service, the phone does not match the published agency line.
- **Upfront retainer.** A "retainer", "investigation fee", "legal filing fee", or "court-bond payment" is requested in advance. Sums range from USD 500 to USD 50,000. Once paid, the operator either disappears or escalates with further fee requests in the same withdrawal-block escalation pattern.
- **Quasi-legitimate cover.** Some operators run a hybrid model — they perform genuine but useless surface activity (a basic blockchain trace anyone can do on a public explorer) and present a flashy report to justify the fee. The recovery prospect is zero.

The structural facts that defeat the architecture: regulators (SEC, FCA, ASIC, SC Malaysia, BNM, MAS) do not contact victims by Telegram DM, WhatsApp, or unsolicited email demanding fees. Real law-enforcement investigations do not bill victims for participation. Real lawyers operate under regulated bar associations and can be verified against those bodies' public registers; they may charge fees, but they are accountable through the bar and do not solicit victims by cold DM.

## The trap

The trap is hope amplified by sunk cost. The victim has lost a significant sum, the emotional pain is acute, and a stranger arrives claiming a path to recovery. The mistake is treating the recovery offer with less scepticism than the original scam — when it should be treated with *more*, because the recovery space is structurally more saturated with fraud than the average investment space. The fix is to apply the verification rules from the rest of the module, plus one additional rule specific to post-loss: legitimate routes for recovery are *reported into* (regulator complaints, police reports, court action), they are not solicited *out of* via DM. Anyone who reaches out to you offering recovery — and who you did not formally contact through an institutional channel — should be treated as the second wave.

## Takeaway

Real regulators and law enforcement do not solicit victims by DM and do not charge upfront retainers. Any unsolicited recovery offer should be treated as the second wave of the same scam network — file it, do not pay it.
