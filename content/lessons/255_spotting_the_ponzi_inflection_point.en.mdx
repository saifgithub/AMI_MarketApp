# Spotting the Ponzi inflection point

The previous lesson explained why a Ponzi must collapse. This lesson teaches the observable signals of the inflection point — the weeks or months when redemptions begin to outpace deposits and the operator starts buying time. The signals are public to anyone in the scheme who is paying attention: friction added to withdrawals, new "premium" tiers, payment delays, a sudden pivot toward a new product. If you are already in a scheme and any two of these signals appear together, the door is closing and the rational move is to withdraw whatever the system still allows.

## How this plays out in real markets

SEC enforcement filings and SC Malaysia investor-alert post-mortems describe the same playbook in the months before failure.

- **Withdrawal friction.** Same-day withdrawals become 3-day, then 7-day, then "queued for the next batch". A scheme that took two clicks last month now requires a verification photo, a "tax certificate", a wire-recall fee.
- **Tier inflation.** A new "VIP" or "founder's circle" tier appears, promising a higher yield to anyone who *locks in* a larger amount for a longer term. This drains marginal capital that would otherwise be redeemed.
- **Audit/tech-issue narrative.** The platform blames "blockchain congestion", "bank compliance review", or "scheduled audit" for payment delays. The same phrases recur across collapsed schemes documented by the FCA, BNM, and ASIC.
- **Pivot to a new product.** A second product launches — typically a "tokenised" version of the original, or a new fund "to diversify the strategy". Existing balances are encouraged to roll over into the new offering. Operationally this resets the obligation clock with the same investor base.
- **Public-facing celebration.** A glossy gala, a charity announcement, a celebrity endorsement event — paid PR designed to reassure jittery members. Filed enforcement cases repeatedly show large events held within 90 days of collapse.

When two or more of these signals appear within a 60-day window, the SEC and SC Malaysia case files show the median time to operational failure is short — typically under six months.

## The trap

The trap is reading these signals as growing pains. The operator explains the delay as "we are upgrading systems for the next 10x" or "regulators are slow, we're fully compliant". Members who notice the friction often double down — they roll over balances into the new tier rather than withdraw — because the alternative (admitting they are in a Ponzi) is emotionally costly. The mistake is treating the operator's explanation as the test. The falsifiable test is structural: friction on withdrawals is exactly what the math requires when redemptions outrun deposits, and it appears in every collapsed scheme on record. Once friction begins, the rational move is to withdraw whatever the system still permits and stop new deposits — not to upgrade your tier.

## Takeaway

When friction appears on withdrawals at the same time as a new lock-up tier or a glossy PR event, the door is closing. Withdraw what you can and stop depositing — the operator's explanation is not the test.
