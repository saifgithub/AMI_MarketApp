# The business cycle — expansion to recession

The economy moves in cycles — expansion, peak, contraction, trough, repeat — and different sectors lead or lag at each stage, which is why "the market" never moves as one block through a full cycle. What officially counts as a recession in the US isn't the popular rule of thumb you've probably heard; it's a judgment call by a small committee looking at several indicators at once, and knowing the real definition is the difference between reading the cycle correctly and reading a myth.

## Example

Here's the popular rule versus the real one, tested against actual 2022 data. In the first half of 2022, US real GDP was initially reported to have contracted for two consecutive quarters: **Q1 2022 at -1.6% annualized** and **Q2 2022 at -0.6% annualized** (BEA) — which satisfied the informal "two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth" definition of a recession that gets repeated constantly in financial media. (BEA's routine data revisions later revised Q2 2022 up to **+0.3%**, in its September 2024 annual update — a reminder that even the raw GDP inputs to the popular rule aren't final the moment they're released.) The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) — the body that actually dates US recessions — **did not declare a recession** for that period. Why: NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee looks at a *basket* of indicators together — nonfarm payrolls, real personal income, industrial production, real consumer spending — not GDP alone, and in the first half of 2022 the labor market was still adding jobs robustly and consumer spending stayed positive. Two negative GDP quarters, as first reported, occurred without NBER ever calling it a recession.

Contrast that with the actual NBER-dated COVID recession: **February 2020 to April 2020**, the shortest recession on record at two months, declared quickly because the collapse in employment, income, and spending was so severe and broad-based across every indicator NBER tracks — the opposite situation from 2022's narrow, GDP-only weakness.

## The trap

The trap is repeating "two consecutive quarters of negative GDP = recession" as if it's the official rule, then being confused when NBER doesn't confirm it — or worse, trading on that mistaken definition as if a recession call is imminent. The correct model: NBER's actual definition is "a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months," assessed across multiple indicators, not a single mechanical GDP threshold. The popular two-quarter rule is a decent rough proxy in many historical cases, but 2022 is the clean counter-example proving it isn't the rule NBER actually uses.

## The steelman

The strongest case for the two-quarter GDP rule despite 2022's counter-example: it's simple, transparent, calculable the moment GDP data lands, and doesn't depend on a committee's discretionary judgment that's only announced well after the fact (NBER often confirms a recession's start date many months, sometimes over a year, after it began). A rule you can compute yourself beats a black-box committee call for real-time decision-making. The falsifier: check whether the two-quarter rule and NBER's eventual call *agree* across a longer history of recessions — they usually do, which is exactly why the shortcut persists. 2022 is the standout exception precisely because the labor market decoupled from GDP so sharply; it's the case that proves you need the full basket, not proof the shortcut is worthless in general.
