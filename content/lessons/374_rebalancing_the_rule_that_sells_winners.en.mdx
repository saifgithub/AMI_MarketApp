# Rebalancing: the rule that makes you sell winners

Rebalancing is the only portfolio discipline that systematically requires you to sell what has been working and buy what has not. After this lesson you will be able to compute when your book has drifted far enough to act, decide the trade in advance rather than in the moment, and — the part that trips almost everyone — explain why a rule you will hate obeying is worth writing down before you need it.

## Example

An openly hypothetical simulated account of **$100,000** with a policy of **60% growth / 40% defensive** — no real instruments, chosen so the arithmetic is visible.

Starting book: **$60,000 growth · $40,000 defensive.**

Over the following year the growth sleeve returns **+40%** and the defensive sleeve **+2%**:

- growth: 60,000 × 1.40 = **$84,000**
- defensive: 40,000 × 1.02 = **$40,800**
- total: **$124,800**

New weights: 84,000 ÷ 124,800 = **67.3% growth**, 40,800 ÷ 124,800 = **32.7% defensive**. Nobody traded. The book simply became more aggressive, exactly as <Lesson id="373_strategic_allocation_vs_tactical_drift"/> describes.

Now the rule. A **band** says: act when a sleeve is more than 5 percentage points from its policy weight — so the trigger for growth sits at 65% or 55%. At 67.3% the band is breached, and the trade is fully determined before you look at any chart:

- target growth = 0.60 × 124,800 = **$74,880**
- sell = 84,000 − 74,880 = **$9,120** of growth, into defensive

Two things about that $9,120. First, it is a computation, not a judgement: the band decided *whether*, the policy decided *how much*, and no view about next year enters anywhere. Second, it is an order to sell the thing that just returned 40% and buy the thing that returned 2%, which is why the rule has to exist on paper before the moment arrives.

The alternative rule is **calendar** rebalancing — check on a fixed date, act on whatever the gap is. It is simpler to run and it trades on schedule whether or not anything moved, which means more trades in quiet years and slower response in violent ones. Bands trade only when something happened. Neither is obviously better; both beat "when I get round to it", which is not a rule at all.

Rebalancing is not free. Every rebalance is real trades with spreads and, outside a simulator, tax consequences — the costs in <Lesson id="105_hidden_costs_in_risk_reward"/> apply here in full. A five-point band trades rarely; a one-point band tracks the policy beautifully and pays for the privilege in costs. That trade-off is the reason to pick a band deliberately rather than take the tightest one that sounds rigorous.

## The trap

The trap is discovering, at the moment the band triggers, that you have a view. The growth sleeve is up 40% and the reasons are legible — the story is good, the momentum is visible, and selling into it feels like vandalism. So the rule is suspended, "just this once", and the suspension is retroactively justified by the very outperformance that caused the trigger.

The correct mental model: rebalancing is not a forecast and never was. It does not claim the winner will fall. It claims that your policy weights described the risk you were willing to hold, and 67.3% is not the number you chose. If you genuinely believe the growth sleeve deserves a permanent 67%, that is a legitimate decision — but it is a decision to change the *policy*, which you make deliberately, in writing, with a reason that is not "it went up". The one thing you may not do is leave the policy at 60 and quietly hold 67, because then you have neither a rule nor a view, only a drift you have agreed not to look at.

## The steelman

The strongest case against banded rebalancing: it is a systematic bet against momentum, and momentum is one of the better-documented return patterns in equities — <Lesson id="379_factors_value_size_momentum_quality_low_vol"/> covers the evidence. Selling winners and buying laggards on a fixed schedule trades against that pattern by construction, and pays costs to do it. That argument is real and is not disposed of by calling rebalancing "disciplined". The reply is that the two claims are about different objects: momentum is an argument about expected *returns*, and rebalancing is a control on *risk* — it exists to stop the book drifting into a shape you did not choose, and it would be worth running even if it cost a little return. The falsifier is measurable on your own record: log the weights at each trigger and what you did. If your suspensions were followed by continued outperformance often enough to cover their costs, you have a momentum overlay and should write it into the policy as one, with its own size and exit. If they were mostly followed by giving the gains back at a higher risk level, the rule was right and you were the failure mode it guards against.

## Try it

Pick your band before you need it. Write one line — "I rebalance when any sleeve is more than N points from policy" — and pick N deliberately, knowing that a smaller N means more trades. Then open your Portfolio, compute your current weights against the policy you wrote in <Lesson id="373_strategic_allocation_vs_tactical_drift"/>, and see whether you are already outside your own band. If you are, write down the exact trade the rule requires before you decide anything about whether you like it.

## Takeaway

Set the band while you have no view, because the moment it triggers you will have one — and the whole value of the rule is that it was written by someone who did not.
