# Rate decisions & how policy transmits

The Fed doesn't set the interest rate you pay on a mortgage or a car loan directly — it sets one number, the federal funds rate (the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans), and that single number ripples outward through a chain: bank lending rates, mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and ultimately asset prices, including the equity discount rate you met in the asset-classes track. Understanding that chain — not just the headline rate decision — is what separates reading a Fed announcement from understanding what it actually does to the economy and your portfolio.

## Example

The Fed's most recent hiking cycle is the cleanest illustration of transmission on record. The federal funds target range started at **0.00%-0.25% in March 2022** and reached **5.25%-5.50% by July 2023** — eleven rate hikes in sixteen months, the fastest tightening pace in over 40 years, taken directly to fight the 9.1% CPI print from two lessons ago. Trace what that did downstream: the average 30-year fixed US mortgage rate rose from roughly **3.2% in January 2022** to over **7% by October 2023** (Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey) — more than doubling the monthly payment on a given loan amount for a new homebuyer, which cooled the housing market sharply even though the Fed never touches the mortgage market directly.

That's the transmission chain working exactly as designed: fed funds rate up → bank cost of funds up → mortgage and corporate lending rates up → borrowing and spending slow → demand cools → (eventually) inflation eases. It's also why rate decisions move markets the moment they're announced, well before any real economic effect shows up — markets are pricing the entire downstream chain in advance, not just the headline number.

## The trap

The trap is treating the fed funds rate as if it's the rate that directly affects you or a company you're evaluating — "rates are at 5.5%, so borrowing costs 5.5%." The fed funds rate is the anchor at the very start of the chain; by the time it reaches a 30-year mortgage or a corporate bond issuance, it's been marked up for term length, credit risk, and market expectations about where rates go next — which is exactly why mortgage rates rose from 3.2% to over 7% (a roughly 400 basis point move) while the fed funds rate itself moved about 500 basis points over a similar window, not a 1:1 match. Always ask "how many links down the chain is this rate from the Fed's own number," not just "what did the Fed do."

## The steelman

The strongest case against overweighting the Fed-to-mortgage-rate chain as a clean, predictable mechanism: long-term rates like the 30-year mortgage are priced more off the 10-year Treasury yield and inflation expectations than off the fed funds rate directly, and the relationship can decouple — long rates sometimes fall even while the Fed hikes, if markets expect a recession and future cuts. That's a real and important qualifier. The falsifier: check whether the 10-year Treasury yield and mortgage rates moved with or against the fed funds rate during the specific window you're analyzing. During most of 2022-2023 they moved together, which is why this example holds — but a reader treating the fed-funds-to-mortgage chain as an unbreakable mechanical law, rather than a usual-but-not-guaranteed relationship, will be caught off guard in a decoupling period.

## Takeaway

The fed funds rate is the start of a chain, not the end of it — track how many links separate any rate you're evaluating from the Fed's own number before assuming they move together.
