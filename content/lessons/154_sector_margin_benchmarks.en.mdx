# Sector-specific margin benchmarks

<Lesson id="033_profit_and_margins"/> warned against comparing margins across sectors. This lesson gives you the benchmark ranges so you can compare a company to its *actual* peer set. A "good" gross margin is sector-specific — 30% is excellent in retail, mediocre in software, and structurally impossible in a low-volume distributor. By the end of this lesson you should know the typical gross, operating, and net margin ranges for the major sectors you'll encounter on US and Bursa Malaysia, and the falsifiable claim a margin outside the normal range carries.

## How this plays out in real markets

Approximate sector benchmarks (US large-cap, recent fiscal years):

- **Software / SaaS** — gross margin 70–85%, operating margin 20–40% at scale (negative during high-growth phases is normal). MSFT, ADBE, CRM all sit in this band.
- **Semiconductors (fabless)** — gross 50–65%, operating 25–40%. NVDA is currently the outlier above the band at ~75% gross / ~62% operating due to AI-cycle pricing.
- **Pharma (large)** — gross 65–80%, operating 25–35%. The high gross margin reflects patent-protected pricing; operating margin reflects heavy R&D spend.
- **Consumer staples (food, household)** — gross 30–45%, operating 15–22%. Stable, capital-efficient.
- **Retail (mass)** — gross 22–28%, operating 3–7%. Razor-thin. WMT and COST live here.
- **Banks (commercial)** — net interest margin 2–4%, ROE the more relevant metric.
- **Oil & gas (integrated)** — gross 20–35%, operating 10–20%, extreme volatility with crude prices.
- **Airlines** — gross 15–25%, operating 5–12% in good years, often negative in bad ones.

So when AAPL reports operating margin around 31% on a $391B revenue base, the right comparison is "consumer electronics / premium hardware peers" — that 31% is exceptional for hardware (~10–15% typical) and reflects AAPL's services mix and brand pricing. When AMZN reports 11% operating margin, the right comparison is "retail at scale" (3–7% typical) — and 11% is excellent for the segment mix.

A real-world misreading: in 2024, commentary occasionally compared TSLA's automotive gross margin (around 17–19%) directly to NVDA's (around 75%) and concluded TSLA was "underperforming on margins". They are not comparable. Automotive gross margins industry-wide run 10–22% — TSLA is in the upper end of car-maker margins. The comparison to NVDA is a category error.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK runs ROE in the 9–12% range, typical of a large regional commercial bank. Comparing MAYBANK's "net margin of ~25%" to a retailer's "net margin of 4%" without contextualising would conclude the bank is dramatically more profitable per dollar of revenue — which is true, but it ignores that banks have far larger asset bases supporting each dollar of revenue. The relevant ratio is ROE or ROA, not net margin.

The falsifiable claim: when a company reports a margin more than 1.5x its sector median in two consecutive years, either the company has a real durable advantage (moat, scale, mix) or the margin is borrowed from one-time items. The next four quarters will usually clarify which.

## The trap

The trap is using a single mental model of "what good margins look like" across every stock. A beginner internalises "30% net margin = great business" from looking at a software name, then dismisses a 5% net-margin retailer as second-rate without knowing the retailer is at the 95th percentile of its peer group. The mirror trap: praising a 35% gross margin at a pharma company as "high" when the sector median is 70% — the company is structurally underperforming the peer set. The correct mental model: every comparison needs a peer baseline. Before evaluating a margin, ask "what's normal for this industry, and where does this company sit in the distribution?" Above or below the median tells you the relative quality. Trend within the company tells you the direction.

## Takeaway

Margins are sector-coded. Compare a company to its peer median, not to a generic ideal — and let the duration of any premium tell you whether the company has a moat or a moment.
