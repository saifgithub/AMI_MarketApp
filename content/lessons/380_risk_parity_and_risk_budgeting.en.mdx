# Risk parity and risk budgeting

The weights you see on a portfolio screen are dollar weights, and they are a poor guide to where your risk actually sits. After this lesson you will be able to compute how much of a portfolio's risk each sleeve is responsible for, and you will find that the familiar balanced portfolio is far less balanced than its label claims.

## Example

Two openly hypothetical sleeves. **Equities:** 18% annualised volatility. **Bonds:** 6%. Correlation between them: **0.10**. Held at the classic **60/40** in dollars.

Portfolio volatility computes to **11.30%**. So far, unremarkable. Now ask a different question: *of that 11.30%, how much is each sleeve responsible for?*

The quantity that answers it is the **marginal contribution to risk** — how much portfolio risk changes for a small increase in one sleeve. It is the row of the covariance matrix multiplied by the weights, (Σw)ᵢ, and a sleeve's share of total risk is wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ divided by the portfolio's variance. Running it on the 60/40 book:

| sleeve | dollar weight | share of portfolio risk |
|---|---|---|
| Equities | 60% | **93.5%** |
| Bonds | 40% | **6.5%** |

**A 60/40 portfolio is a 93/7 portfolio.** The bond sleeve holds 40 cents of every dollar and answers for about seven percent of the risk. Nothing is wrong with the calculation and nothing is wrong with owning such a book — but anyone who described it as "balanced" was describing the money, and the money is not what moves.

**Risk parity** asks the obvious follow-up: what weights make the risk shares equal? Here, weighting each sleeve by the inverse of its volatility gives equities 1/18 ÷ (1/18 + 1/6) = **25%**, and at **25/75** the risk shares come out at exactly **50.0% / 50.0%**, with portfolio volatility of **6.67%**.

Two things follow immediately, and the second is the one people skip. First, equal *risk* requires wildly unequal *money* — three quarters of the capital in the quiet sleeve. Second, the resulting portfolio is much quieter overall, at 6.67% against 11.30%, which is a different portfolio rather than a better version of the same one. This is why risk-parity strategies in practice are so often levered: the balance is achieved at a risk level below what the holder wanted, and leverage is what brings it back — importing a completely separate set of risks in the process. Balancing risk shares and choosing a risk level are two decisions, and conflating them is the most common way the idea gets misapplied.

This is a different budget from the one in <Lesson id="109_risk_budgeting_across_positions"/>. That budgets the loss allowed if a stop is hit — a cash amount you decide. This budgets the *variance* each sleeve contributes — a quantity you compute, and which changes when volatilities and correlations change, whether or not you touch the book.

## The trap

The trap is reading dollar weights as risk weights, which almost every portfolio screen invites you to do. Two sleeves at 60 and 40 look like a deliberate, moderate split, and the arithmetic says one of them is doing nearly all the work. The error is not that dollar weights are useless — they determine what you own — but that they answer a question about capital when the question you meant was about risk.

The correct mental model: dollar weight scales with price, and risk contribution scales with volatility *and* with how a sleeve co-moves with everything else. Those come apart hardest exactly where portfolios are usually described as balanced, because the low-volatility sleeve is precisely the one that needs a large dollar weight to matter at all. Whenever someone hands you a portfolio described by its weights, one line of arithmetic tells you what it is actually made of.

## The steelman

The strongest case against risk parity: it is an allocation rule with no expected returns in it at all, so it happily concentrates capital in whatever asset has recently been quietest, and it does so with no view on whether that asset is worth owning. Applied to bonds after a long calm period it puts three quarters of the money into an asset yielding very little, and the leverage typically used to restore the risk level turns a modest adverse move into a serious one. Those are real failure modes, not caricatures. What survives the criticism is the *measurement*: computing risk shares is diagnostic, and it is right whether or not you accept the prescription that they should be equal. The falsifier separates the two cleanly — if you compute your risk shares and find they match what you intended, the diagnostic cost you one line of arithmetic and the prescription is irrelevant to you. If they are 93/7 and you believed you held something balanced, the measurement has already earned its place, and whether you respond by changing weights is a separate decision the number does not make for you.

## Try it

Take the two largest sleeves of your simulated Portfolio, write down your rough estimate of each one's annual volatility and their correlation, and compute the risk shares from wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ ÷ portfolio variance. Compare the answer to the dollar weights on your screen. Then ask the Chief Investment Officer in a 1-on-1 which sleeve it thinks carries most of your risk, and check its reasoning against your own arithmetic rather than the other way round — AMI's answers are training artifacts for you to grade.

## Takeaway

Dollar weights tell you what you own and risk shares tell you what you are exposed to; a 60/40 book is routinely a 93/7 book, and one line of arithmetic is the difference between knowing that and assuming otherwise.

*Where this comes from: the marginal-contribution decomposition of portfolio variance that follows from Markowitz's covariance framework — Portfolio Selection (1952).*
