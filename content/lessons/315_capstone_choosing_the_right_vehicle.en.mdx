# Capstone: choosing the right vehicle

You now hold the full M14 toolkit: wrapper vs strategy, fee drag compounding, the passive/active cost hurdle, leveraged-ETF decay, and the mechanical quirks of REITs, ADRs, and closed-end funds. This capstone runs them all against one saver's decision — the kind where every option "sounds reasonable" in isolation, and the skill being tested is matching the vehicle's mechanics to the goal, not to the pitch.

## One scenario, every M14 concept

A saver has $10,000 and a 30-year horizon before retirement, plus a separate, smaller amount she's set aside specifically to express a short-term view on a volatile sector she's been reading about. She's comparing four options for the core $10,000: (1) a broad-market index ETF at a 0.03% expense ratio, (2) a comparable actively-managed mutual fund at a 0.75% expense ratio, (3) a REIT paying a high current yield, and (4) — for the separate short-term money only — a 2x leveraged ETF on the sector she has a view on.

Run the toolkit against the core decision. Between the index ETF and the active fund, this module's worked number applies directly: at 7% gross return over 30 years, the fee gap between 0.03% and 0.75% costs $13,844.08 — 18.3% of the low-cost fund's terminal value — for identical gross exposure, before any judgment about whether the active fund's manager adds skill worth that cost (the Bogle cost-matters case: skill has to clear the fee hurdle every year, and most funds in this category don't, per the SPIVA pattern). The REIT's high yield is real, but it's a payout-structure feature (the 90%-distribution requirement) that comes with rate sensitivity and less capital retained for growth — appropriate as an income *sleeve*, not automatically superior core exposure, and not comparable apples-to-apples with the ETF's total-return profile. For the separate short-term sector view, the leveraged ETF is the vehicle built for that job specifically because it is not built for the 30-year core money — its daily-reset compounding means a choppy few weeks in that sector could erode value even if her long-term view on the sector eventually proves right, which is exactly why that exposure belongs in the small, short-term-labeled bucket and nowhere near the 30-year core.

## The steelman

The strongest case against this whole framework: "diversifying across vehicle types (some index, some active, some REIT, some leverage) reduces the chance that any single mistake wrecks the outcome — why not hold a little of everything instead of optimizing hard on the core?" The falsifier is the fee-drag arithmetic itself: diversification across vehicles doesn't cancel out a structural, guaranteed cost like the 0.72-percentage-point fee gap — that cost compounds against you at the same rate regardless of what else you hold alongside it. Diversification protects against uncertain, unknowable risks (which specific stock underperforms); it does nothing against a known, certain drag like an expense ratio difference. The steelman is right that vehicle mixing has real diversification value for genuinely uncertain risks — sector calls, single-manager risk — but it doesn't launder away a cost that's already known and already certain to compound.

## Try it

Convene the Room on a ticker from your watchlist and, in the simulated Verdict, identify which vehicle type (if any) each agent's argument implicitly assumes — a single-stock pick, an index-style broad bet, or something with leverage or a payout structure baked in. Log the mismatch, if any, between the agent's assumed vehicle and your own stated goal in your Decision Journal. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

There is no universally "best" fund vehicle — there is only the vehicle whose mechanics match your specific horizon, goal, and tolerance for cost and decay, checked deliberately rather than assumed.
