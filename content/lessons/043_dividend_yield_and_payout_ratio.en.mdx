# Dividend yield and payout ratio

Dividend yield is the annual cash a company pays per share, divided by the share price. Payout ratio is the share of earnings (or free cash flow) the company hands back as dividends. The two together tell you whether the income stream is generous, sustainable, both, or neither. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute both, recognise the warning sign of a "yield trap", and state the precise condition under which a high yield is durable rather than a setup for a cut.

## How this plays out in real markets

JPM in mid-2026 pays an annual dividend of roughly $5.20 per share. At a stock price near $220, dividend yield = 5.20 / 220 ≈ 2.4%. JPM's trailing EPS is around $18.50, so payout ratio on earnings = 5.20 / 18.50 ≈ 28%. That's a sustainable payout — only about a quarter of earnings goes out, leaving room for retained capital and buffers against a credit cycle. XOM pays around $3.96 annual dividend, stock near $115, yield ≈ 3.4%, payout on earnings around 45–50% depending on the oil-price cycle — high but consistent with mature-energy norms. MSFT pays around $3.32 annual, stock near $480, yield ≈ 0.7%, payout around 25% — a low-yield, low-payout profile typical of a high-growth business reinvesting most cash.

Now a yield trap. Suppose a regional telco trades at $25 with a $2.40 dividend — yield = 9.6%. The headline "almost 10%" looks irresistible. Check the payout ratio: trailing EPS of $1.80 gives a payout of 2.40 / 1.80 = 133%. The company is paying out more than it earns, funded by either debt or balance-sheet drawdown. That is unsustainable; the dividend will be cut, and the share price typically falls *before* the cut as the market anticipates it. Investors who bought for the 9.6% yield often end up holding a 4% yield on a stock that fell 50% — a worse total-return outcome than ignoring the yield entirely.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK has long been one of the highest-yielding large caps on KLCI, with a yield in the 5–6% range and payout on earnings typically 60–70%. The dividend is funded by genuine earnings, not balance-sheet erosion, which is why it has been broadly sustained through cycles. KLCC, structured as a REIT, distributes substantially all of its taxable income — its high payout is structurally mandated and shouldn't be read with the same lens as a non-REIT industrial.

Falsifiable claim: a dividend yield above 6% is sustainable only when payout ratio on free cash flow (not earnings) is below 80%, AND the trailing-three-year FCF trend is flat or growing. Either of those conditions failing flips a high yield from "income opportunity" to "pre-cut warning".

## The trap

The trap is yield-chasing — sorting a watchlist by dividend yield and buying the top of the list. The math is seductive: a 10% yield "pays you to wait". But high yield is almost always one of three things: (1) a reflection of capital-light, mature business that genuinely returns cash (acceptable), (2) a stock price that has fallen because the market expects bad news (the yield rose mechanically as the price dropped), or (3) a payout the company can't afford and will cut. Categories 2 and 3 dominate the high-yield screen. The correct mental model: a dividend is a *commitment* the company has chosen to make and chooses to maintain. Sustainability is about whether free cash flow covers the dividend with margin to spare, not about whether the yield is large. What invalidates the signal: a "special" or one-time dividend inflates the trailing yield for one period and shouldn't be annualised. Always separate regular from special dividends before computing yield.

## Takeaway

Dividend yield without payout-ratio context is just a headline number. A sustainable yield is funded by free cash flow with margin to spare; an unsustainable one is funded by debt or balance-sheet erosion and gets cut.
