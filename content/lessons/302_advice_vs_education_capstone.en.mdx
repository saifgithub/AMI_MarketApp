# Capstone: advice vs education

Everything in this module — fiduciary duty, conflicts, suitability, disclosure — converges on one line: the line between **education** and **advice**. Education transfers understanding; advice tells a specific person what to do with their money, and the moment it does, every duty you've studied attaches to the adviser. This capstone runs all four duties against a single scenario you see every day in this app, and explains from the inside why AMI is a training simulator, simulation-only, forever — not as a legal disclaimer, but as a structural consequence of M23.

## One scenario, every M23 duty

You convene the Room and the simulated Verdict lands: **BUY 5% NVDA, entry $118, stop $104, target $150, horizon 6 months** — a training artifact for you to practice evaluating. Now run the thought experiment: what would have to be true for that same output to be *advice* you could act on through a real brokerage?

**Fiduciary duty:** advice creates a duty of loyalty to your specific situation. AMI holds your training mandate, not your real balance sheet, debts, dependents, or tax position — it structurally cannot owe, or discharge, loyalty to circumstances it never measured. **Suitability and KYC:** the 5% sizing is suitable against your *simulated* mandate. Against your real finances it is unmeasured — and an unmeasured recommendation is the definition of a suitability failure, however good the analysis. **Conflicts:** an app monetised per trade would want you trading; AMI's training subscription earns the same whether you accept, modify, or reject every verdict — the conflict a real advisory relationship must manage is designed out. **Disclosure:** the Verdict arrives labelled a practice artifact. That label is the disclosure duty done honestly — it tells you exactly what the output is and is not.

Acting on the verdict off-app doesn't upgrade it to advice; it strips it of every protection advice is required to carry. The duties attach — and nobody is there to bear them.

## The steelman

The strongest counter: "If AMI's analysis is good enough to learn from, it's good enough to trade — refusing to advise protects lawyers, not users. A licensed AMI-adviser would help more people." Take it seriously: quality is real, and licensed robo-advisers exist. The falsifier is that **quality was never the line — accountability is**. Advice is a duty chain: KYC of your real finances → suitability against them → loyalty when interests collide → disclosure → recourse when it fails. Ask of any output: *if this goes wrong, who owes you what?* In education, nobody owes, because nobody who knows you told you to act. A robo-adviser accepts the whole chain for a narrow, measured slice of you. A training simulator issuing actionable calls would be the worst of both — advice's influence with none of its duties. That is the gap AMI refuses to occupy.

## Try it

Ask the Concierge exactly that question — "why won't you just tell me what to buy?" — and grade the answer against this lesson: does it name duties (measurement, loyalty, recourse), or hide behind disclaimers? Log your grading in the Decision Journal. Remember: AMI is a training simulator; every verdict you'll ever see here is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Education means nobody has to know you; advice means somebody must — and must answer for it. Never take investment direction from anything that can't know you, and notice that AMI is built to never pretend otherwise.
