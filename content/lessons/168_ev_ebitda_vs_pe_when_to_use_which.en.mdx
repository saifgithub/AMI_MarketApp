# EV/EBITDA vs P/E: when to use which

Lesson 037 introduced multiples-based valuation; lesson 039 dug into P/E. P/E is the most-quoted multiple but it's also the most-distorted, because it compares prices to earnings *after* interest and taxes — meaning two companies with identical operations but different debt loads or different tax jurisdictions will look very different on P/E even though their underlying businesses are nearly identical. EV/EBITDA solves both problems by valuing the enterprise (equity + debt − cash) against pre-interest, pre-tax cash flow. By the end of this lesson you should know which multiple to reach for in which situation, and the specific structural pattern that makes P/E systematically mislead.

## How this plays out in real markets

Compare AT&T (T) and Verizon (VZ) in 2024. Both are telecom incumbents with similar operating profiles. T trades at roughly 8x trailing P/E; VZ at roughly 10x. The headline difference suggests T is "cheaper." But T carries more debt and a different tax position. On EV/EBITDA the picture changes: T at ~6.5x, VZ at ~6.5x. Essentially identical. The P/E gap was an artifact of capital structure, not operating value — exactly the situation EV/EBITDA exists to solve.

Now compare TSLA at ~80x P/E to F (Ford) at ~7x P/E in 2024. P/E ratio gap is 11x. On EV/EBITDA: TSLA at ~50x, F at ~12x. The gap narrows but TSLA is still much more expensive — because the gap is genuine operating economics (TSLA grows faster, has higher operating margins), not just capital structure. EV/EBITDA didn't make TSLA "cheap"; it stripped out the capital-structure noise and showed the operating gap clearly.

Where P/E genuinely fails: banks, REITs, insurers, capital-intensive businesses with very different depreciation policies. For banks, P/B is the dominant multiple (lesson 040). For REITs, price-to-FFO replaces P/E. For capex-heavy industrials with widely varying depreciation schedules, EV/EBITDA tells a cleaner story than P/E because depreciation choices (straight-line vs accelerated, useful life assumptions) heavily affect reported earnings.

Where EV/EBITDA genuinely fails: companies with high working-capital needs or heavy stock-based compensation. EBITDA ignores both, which is why software companies with 15–20% of revenue going to SBC can look misleadingly cheap on EV/EBITDA (lesson 174 dives deeper). The honest multiple for those is EV/EBITDA-minus-SBC.

On Bursa Malaysia: comparing PETRONAS Chemicals to a smaller petrochemical peer on P/E can mislead if one carries different parent-company debt allocations and the other doesn't. EV/EBITDA neutralises that.

The falsifiable claim: when two operationally-similar companies show a P/E gap of more than 30% AND an EV/EBITDA gap of less than 10%, the P/E gap is capital-structure or tax-driven, not operating-economics-driven, and treating it as a "cheapness" signal is an error.

## The trap

The trap is using P/E to compare across capital structures or across countries. A beginner sees a US company at 18x P/E and a European peer at 12x and concludes the European stock is cheap — without checking that the European company has 60% more debt and pays a 6-point lower effective tax rate, both of which boost EPS for reasons that have nothing to do with operating quality. The correct mental model: P/E is the right multiple for an apples-to-apples comparison among companies with similar leverage and similar tax exposure. The moment those vary materially, switch to EV/EBITDA (or EV/EBIT for companies where depreciation is meaningfully different from real capex). Always cross-check P/E with at least one EV-based multiple before declaring something cheap or expensive across companies.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, pick two companies in the same industry and ask: "Show me P/E and EV/EBITDA side by side, with debt-to-equity and effective tax rate for each." In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write one sentence: "On P/E, A looks [cheaper/more expensive] by X%. On EV/EBITDA, the gap is Y%. The X−Y difference is the capital-structure noise I would have mistaken for an operating signal." This exercise calibrates your eye for which multiple to default to in which situation.

## Takeaway

P/E is the easiest multiple to quote and the easiest to misread — when leverage or tax jurisdictions vary, EV/EBITDA is the cleaner first read, and when capex matches depreciation, neither is enough on its own.
