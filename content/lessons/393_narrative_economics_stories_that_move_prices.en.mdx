# Narrative economics — the story is a fact about the market

A story that spreads changes what people do, and what people do changes prices and sometimes the economy itself. That makes a widely-held narrative a genuine economic variable rather than noise sitting on top of one. This is a sharper claim than "sentiment matters", and it cuts in an uncomfortable direction: a narrative does not have to be true to move a price correctly. Your job is not to grade the story. It is to work out how far it has spread, who has already acted on it, and what would have to happen for it to stop spreading.

## How this plays out in real markets

A hypothetical narrative — *"this sector is the next infrastructure"* — takes hold.

**Early.** A handful of specialists hold it. Prices reflect fundamentals plus a small premium. Acting on the narrative here means being early, and being early is indistinguishable from being wrong for an uncomfortable length of time.

**Spreading.** The story reaches general financial media. Companies begin describing themselves in its vocabulary — which is itself a measurable signal, because a firm renaming its existing business to match a narrative is telling you the narrative now pays. Capital arrives. The premium widens, and here is the part that makes this economics rather than psychology: **the capital is real.** Companies genuinely build, hire and expand because the story made funding cheap. The narrative starts producing the evidence cited in its support.

**Saturated.** The story reaches people who do not follow markets. Every listed company that can plausibly claim the label has claimed it. There is no one left to convert.

Notice the ending: saturation is not a valuation event. It arrives when the supply of people who have not yet heard the story runs out — which can happen at any price.

## The trap

The trap is arguing with the narrative instead of measuring it. "This is obviously a bubble" may be entirely correct and is not a position, because it says nothing about *when*. A story that is wrong on the fundamentals can keep being right about prices for as long as it keeps spreading, and the spreading is the variable you can actually observe.

The opposite error is treating the narrative as the analysis — buying because the story is compelling. A compelling story is evidence about how far it will spread, not evidence that it is true. Both mistakes come from the same confusion: grading the story instead of tracking it.

## Steelman

The serious objection is unfalsifiability — narrative is an explanation that fits any outcome after the fact, and "the story saturated" can be written over any top. That is a fair charge against narrative as *prediction*, and it is why the honest use is descriptive and prospective: name the specific narrative, state where you think it currently sits in its spread, and name in advance what would count as saturation for it. If you cannot name what saturation would look like before it happens, you are not using a model — you are collecting a vocabulary for describing things that already happened.

## Try it

Pick a narrative currently driving a name you follow and write it as one sentence in your Decision Journal. Underneath, write two things: where you think it sits on the spread — specialist, mainstream, or saturated — and one specific, observable event that would tell you it has saturated. Come back to it in a month and check whether you named something you could actually see.

## Takeaway

A story does not have to be true to move prices, so stop grading it and start locating it — and if you cannot say in advance what saturation would look like, you do not have a view, you have a description.

*Where this comes from: Shiller's Narrative Economics, which treats the contagion of economic stories as a driver of aggregate outcomes rather than as commentary on them.*
