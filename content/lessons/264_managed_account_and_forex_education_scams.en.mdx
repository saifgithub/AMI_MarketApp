# "Managed account" guarantees and unregulated forex education

Two related abuses sit alongside the retail-FX universe: the "managed account" pitch and the "forex education" funnel. The first promises that a third party will trade your account and deliver a fixed monthly return — structurally a Ponzi or unauthorised fund-management activity. The second sells expensive courses whose primary revenue is not education but the affiliate commission from referring students to an offshore broker. This lesson teaches how to recognise both, and the regulatory line each crosses.

## How this plays out in real markets

**Managed-account pitches** show up in WhatsApp DMs, Telegram channels, and at "investment seminars". The structure: "Open an account at broker X. Give me limited power of attorney. I will trade for you and guarantee 8% per month. You can withdraw anytime." The activity is either a Ponzi (no real trading; returns paid from new deposits) or unauthorised fund management (a real but unlicensed individual taking discretion over client funds — itself an offence in every tier-1 jurisdiction).

The CFTC's Commodity Pool Operator regime requires registration for anyone trading pooled client funds in commodities or FX. The SEC's Investment Advisers Act requires registration for discretionary management above small thresholds. The FCA, ASIC, MAS, and SC Malaysia have parallel regimes. The "managed account" operator who is not on any of these registers is operating illegally regardless of whether the underlying activity is fraudulent.

**Forex education funnels** show up as USD 2,000–8,000 courses promising "the system the prop traders use". The course content is typically generic — pin bars, support and resistance, fibonacci. The economic engine is the affiliate kickback: students are steered toward a specific offshore broker, the course provider earns a percentage of each student's deposits (and in many cases a percentage of student *losses*). The course's true product is referral flow to the broker. The FCA's 2023 review of finfluencer-led trading courses, and ASIC's parallel work, both documented the structure.

A representative example: an instructor with a polished YouTube presence sells a USD 5,000 mentorship. Students are told they must "fund a live account to apply the lessons" and given a referral link to an offshore broker offering 1:500 leverage. The course graduates lose money on the broker; the instructor earns the course fee plus the referral kickback. The instructor's own trading record is unverifiable.

Signature traits:

- Managed-account operator not registered as a CPO, investment adviser, fund manager, or equivalent in any tier-1 jurisdiction.
- Course funnel ending at a specific offshore broker with an affiliate link.
- Instructor's claimed track record exists only as screenshots; no independently audited statement.
- "Funded account" or "prop firm" challenge structures with non-refundable fees and rules calibrated to make passing statistically unlikely.

## The trap

The trap is the legitimacy of the adjacent categories. Real fund managers exist (regulated, licensed, audited). Real trading education exists (sold by registered firms or instructors with verified track records). The reader assumes "this offer is in a real category, therefore the specific offer is real". The fix is to apply the same registration check the rest of the module uses: a managed-account offer requires the operator to be registered as a fund manager or investment adviser in the relevant jurisdiction; a course paired with a broker referral is a marketing pitch for the broker, and the broker should be assessed under the rest of the module's checks before the course's content is even considered.

## Takeaway

A managed-account offer without the operator on a tier-1 fund-management or adviser register is unlawful by structure. A trading course that funnels to a specific offshore broker is a broker marketing product, not education — assess the broker first.
