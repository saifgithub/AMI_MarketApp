# Islamic contracts & instruments

Five contract structures do most of the work across Islamic finance, and each has a conventional analogue that helps place it: *murabahah* (cost-plus sale, analogue: a loan), *ijarah* (leasing, analogue: a lease or lease-to-own), *musharakah* (partnership, analogue: joint-venture equity), *mudarabah* (profit-sharing venture, analogue: a fund with a general/limited-partner split), and *takaful* (mutual risk-sharing, analogue: insurance). Each replaces a debt-and-interest relationship with an ownership, lease, or shared-risk relationship. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to match each structure to its conventional analogue and explain what actually changed.

## Example

Picture financing a $40,000 equipment purchase five different ways. **Murabahah**: the financier buys the equipment for $40,000 and immediately resells it to you for $44,000 payable over 2 years — a disclosed cost-plus markup, agreed upfront, structurally a sale rather than a loan (no compounding, no penalty interest on the balance). **Ijarah**: the financier retains ownership and leases you the equipment for a fixed monthly rental, with either an option to buy at the end (ijarah muntahia bittamlik) or a straight operating lease — your conventional-finance analogue is an equipment lease. **Musharakah**: you and the financier both contribute capital (say 60/40) and both share profit and loss in that ratio — the analogue is a joint venture, not a loan at all. **Mudarabah**: you contribute capital, a manager contributes expertise and labor, and profit is split by a pre-agreed ratio while capital loss (absent manager negligence) falls on the capital provider — the analogue is a limited-partner/general-partner fund structure. **Takaful**: participants pool contributions into a shared fund that pays out claims, with any surplus returned to participants rather than kept as insurer profit — the analogue is mutual insurance, structurally closer to a cooperative than a for-profit insurer underwriting risk for its own account.

## The trap

Beginners hear "Islamic mortgage" or "Islamic loan" and assume it's a conventional loan with an Arabic name slapped on for marketing. That's the trap this lesson exists to correct: murabahah is legally a **sale** (with a disclosed markup, no compounding), not a loan with interest relabeled; ijarah is legally a **lease**, not a loan; musharakah and mudarabah are legally **partnerships**, where the financier genuinely shares in loss, not just profit. The correct mental model: check what the contract's legal substance actually transfers — ownership, a lease, or shared risk — rather than assuming any Islamic-labeled product is a cosmetic rename of a conventional loan.

## Takeaway

Each Islamic contract structure — murabahah, ijarah, musharakah, mudarabah, takaful — replaces a debt-and-interest claim with a sale, lease, or genuinely shared-risk relationship; check the legal substance, not the label. This is screening methodology taught for training purposes, not a Sharia ruling; consult a qualified scholar when evaluating real contracts.

*Where this comes from: AAOIFI's Shariah Standards on each named contract; Usmani's An Introduction to Islamic Finance (2002); El-Gamal's Islamic Finance: Law, Economics, and Practice (2006).*
