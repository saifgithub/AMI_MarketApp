# Fiscal policy — spending, deficits & debt

Monetary policy (the Fed, rates, the balance sheet) is one lever on the economy; fiscal policy — how much the government spends and taxes — is the other, and it's run by Congress and the Treasury, entirely independent of the Fed. When the government spends more than it collects in taxes, it runs a deficit and borrows the difference by issuing Treasury bonds, adding to the national debt. This lesson is about reading fiscal policy as a second macro force alongside monetary policy — one that can amplify or fight against what the Fed is doing at the same time.

## Example

The US federal budget deficit for fiscal year 2023 ran approximately **$1.7 trillion** (US Treasury / CBO) — meaning the government spent about $1.7 trillion more than it collected in revenue that year, financed by issuing new Treasury debt. Total gross federal debt crossed **$33 trillion in September 2023** (US Treasury), a milestone widely reported at the time. Notice what was happening simultaneously: the Fed was running quantitative tightening (selling down its own bond holdings) while the Treasury was issuing large amounts of new debt to fund the deficit — two forces both adding to the supply of bonds the private market had to absorb at the same time, one reason long-term Treasury yields rose meaningfully through 2023 even after the Fed's hiking cycle was nearing its end.

This is fiscal and monetary policy interacting directly: large deficit spending can work against a Fed that's trying to cool the economy (more government spending adds to demand, partially offsetting higher rates meant to reduce it), or it can work alongside a Fed trying to stimulate a weak economy. Reading only the Fed while ignoring fiscal policy means missing half of what's actually driving demand in the economy at any given time.

## The trap

The trap is treating "deficit" and "debt" as inherently catastrophic numbers without asking what they're financing or relative to what. A trillion-dollar deficit sounds alarming in isolation, but the number that matters more for sustainability is the debt-to-GDP ratio and the interest cost relative to government revenue — a small economy running a trillion-dollar deficit is in a very different position than the ~$27 trillion US economy running the same number. The correct habit: never react to a raw deficit or debt figure without immediately asking "relative to what" — GDP, prior years, and what the spending is actually funding.

## The steelman

This is a genuinely contested debate, not a settled one, so steelman both sides. The crowding-out case: large government borrowing competes with private borrowers for the same pool of capital, pushing up interest rates for everyone (consistent with 2023's rising long-term yields alongside record Treasury issuance) and reducing private investment — the government "crowds out" private borrowing. The counter-case: in an economy with ample savings and global demand for safe US Treasury debt, government borrowing doesn't necessarily displace private investment dollar-for-dollar, and deficit spending during a genuine downturn can support growth that private demand alone wouldn't provide. The falsifier that would settle it in a specific period: check whether private borrowing rates and private investment actually fell as government issuance rose (supporting crowding-out) or whether both grew together (suggesting the economy had room for both) — the answer differs by period and cannot be assumed either way in advance.

## Takeaway

Fiscal policy is a second macro lever running independently of the Fed — always check the deficit and debt relative to GDP, and check whether fiscal and monetary policy are reinforcing or fighting each other.
