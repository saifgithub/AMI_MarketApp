# Dynamic vs static support and resistance

This assumes you've completed 023 (support and resistance). Here we go deeper on a distinction lesson 023 deliberately left flat: support and resistance come in two flavours. *Static* levels are horizontal — fixed prices the market has bounced from or rejected before. *Dynamic* levels are sloped — moving averages, trendlines, channel boundaries that drift as price moves. They behave differently. By the end of this lesson you should be able to mark both kinds on a chart, explain why dynamic levels are usually softer than static ones, and know which to lead with depending on whether the market is trending or ranging.

## Example

Look at NVDA on the daily chart from January through April 2026.

**Static levels.** $148 was a swing high in November 2025 and again the failed-breakout high in March 2026 — a horizontal line that defined the same price as resistance across four months. $108 was a major low on 14 February 2026 and previously support in late October 2025. These don't move. Mark them once, they stay marked.

**Dynamic levels.** The 50-day moving average (50DMA) carved a sloped support floor through the entire uptrend. On 18 February NVDA tested the 50DMA at $114.40 and bounced. On 5 March it tested again at $122.80 and bounced. On 19 March it tested at $130.20 and bounced. Same level — the 50DMA — but at three different prices, because moving averages slope. A trendline drawn from the $108 February low through the $114 February swing low also acts dynamically: it was at $122 in early March and $129 by late March.

So as of late April 2026 a trader's chart of NVDA shows two horizontals ($108 and $148) plus a rising 50DMA currently near $134 plus a rising trendline near $136. Static levels constrain the range; dynamic levels constrain the slope.

A bounce off the static $108 in February invalidates on a daily close below $108. A bounce off the rising 50DMA at $134 invalidates on a daily close below the 50DMA — but because the 50DMA is sloped, the actual invalidation price changes by maybe $0.30 to $0.80 each session. The level moves; the rule stays the same.

## The trap

The trap is treating dynamic levels with the same precision people grant static ones. Static levels are visible to every trader on the chart at exactly the same price — that's why they work, they're a coordination point. Dynamic levels look different on every trader's chart depending on which moving-average length or trendline endpoints they use. A 50DMA bounce on one trader's screen is a 200DMA touch on another's and a "couple of percent above the rising trendline" on a third. The correct mental model: in trending markets, lead with dynamic levels — the trend's own pace is the price-action floor. In ranging markets, lead with static levels — horizontal memory is what coordinates participants when there's no slope. And always treat dynamic invalidations as ranges, not lines: the moving-average is a fuzzy zone roughly 0.5%–1.5% wide, not a precise tick.

## Takeaway

Static levels are horizontal memory and work when the market is ranging; dynamic levels are sloped and work when it's trending — pick the tool that matches the market, and always treat the close as the invalidation, never the wick.
