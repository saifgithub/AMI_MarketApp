# Recurring vs lumpy revenue

<Lesson id="032_revenue_the_top_line"/> looked at revenue's headline rate. This one looks at its *shape*. Two companies with identical $1B annual revenue can carry wildly different fundamental risk depending on whether that $1B repeats every year automatically (subscriptions, leases, regulated utilities) or has to be re-won every quarter from project bids, hardware refresh cycles, or one-time licences. Recurring revenue earns a premium multiple for good reason: it is more predictable, cheaper to forecast, and worth more in a discounted cash flow. By the end of this lesson you should know how to estimate recurring share, why "lumpy" doesn't mean "bad", and the trap of treating a one-time spike as a new run-rate.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at MSFT and a defense contractor like LMT in fiscal 2024. MSFT reported about $245B in revenue, with the Productivity and Business Processes segment (Office 365, LinkedIn, Dynamics) and Intelligent Cloud (Azure) together representing roughly 75% of the total — almost entirely subscription or consumption-based. The remaining ~25% (Personal Computing — Windows OEM, Xbox, Surface, devices) is more transactional but heavily annuitised through Windows licensing. The market values this revenue at a higher P/S multiple (8–12x in recent years) because it shows up next quarter whether or not management does anything.

Now look at LMT. Revenue around $71B in fiscal 2024, almost entirely from long-cycle defense contracts. The contracts are recurring in the sense that programs run for decades, but recognition is lumpy — a milestone in a fighter-jet program books a chunk of revenue when achieved, not on a calendar. Quarter-to-quarter revenue can swing ±10% without anything fundamental having changed. Investors value LMT at a lower P/S (~1.7x) reflecting the lumpiness.

For a pure-lumpy contrast, look at TSLA's energy storage business — revenue grew from $3B in 2022 to $10B in 2024, with individual quarterly results swinging based on megapack delivery timing. A single quarter being "down 30% sequentially" doesn't always mean demand is down — it can mean a deployment slid two weeks.

On Bursa Malaysia: TENAGA's regulated tariff revenue is one of the most recurring on the exchange — set by a Regulatory Period framework, adjusted on a known cadence. By contrast, GAMUDA's construction segment is intensely project-based; quarterly revenue depends on which job is at which milestone.

The falsifiable claim: a company with 70%+ recurring revenue should trade at a higher EV/sales multiple than its sector peer with 30% recurring, all else equal. When the multiples invert, either the market is wrong or the "recurring" classification is overstated.

## The trap

The trap is treating a single big quarter at a lumpy business as a new baseline. A beginner sees a defense contractor or project-services company post +40% revenue and projects that forward into next year. They miss that the +40% was a milestone payment, a delivery cluster, or a contract acceptance — not a step-change in the run rate. The mirror trap exists at subscription businesses: treating a slow quarter as a structural break when it was a billing-timing artifact. The correct mental model: separate every revenue line into "would this repeat next quarter even if management did nothing" versus "did this happen because of a specific event". Build a run-rate forecast from the first bucket and treat the second as upside or noise. When 80% of growth came from the second bucket, the headline rate is borrowed.

## Takeaway

Recurring revenue is worth more than lumpy revenue per dollar — when the multiples don't reflect that, the market is either smarter than you or wronger than you. Find which.
