# Index construction — a committee's rules, not a photograph of the market

"The market was up today" almost always means an index was up, and an index is a rulebook: which companies are eligible, how their weights are set, when membership changes, and what counts as a share. Those choices are neither obvious nor neutral, and two indices covering the same market can disagree meaningfully about what it did. Owning an index fund means owning the output of a specific rulebook — which is an excellent decision, made better by knowing which rulebook.

## How this plays out in real markets

**Weighting is the biggest choice.** Most broad indices are capitalisation-weighted: each company's weight is its market value as a share of the total. A consequence follows immediately and surprises people — as a company's price rises, its weight rises, so a cap-weighted index automatically holds more of what has already gone up. That is not a flaw; it is what makes the index self-maintaining and nearly costless to track. But it does mean concentration is an outcome of the rule, not a decision anyone took.

A price-weighted index instead weights by share price, so a $400 stock moves it four times as much as a $100 stock regardless of company size — a convention that made sense when indices were computed by hand and survives by inertia.

**Float adjustment.** Most cap-weighted indices count only shares actually available to trade, excluding blocks held by founders, governments or cross-holdings. A company with a $100bn market value but only 40% free float carries roughly the weight of a $40bn company, because that is what an index fund could actually buy.

**Membership is a decision.** Additions and removals are made by a committee or by published rules on a schedule. Because index funds must hold what the index holds, an addition creates real forced buying — which is why the announcement itself moves the price, before any fund has traded.

## The trap

The trap is treating "I bought the index, so I am diversified" as the end of the analysis. A cap-weighted index of 500 companies can have a third of its value in a handful of names, because the rule that built it concentrates by design. You own 500 companies and a much smaller number of *bets*.

The second trap is comparing performance across indices without checking construction. A cap-weighted and an equal-weighted version of the identical 500 companies can differ by several percentage points in a year, and neither is wrong — they are answering different questions. If you are comparing your portfolio to "the market", know which rulebook you are being graded against.

## Steelman

None of this is an argument against index investing, and it would be a serious misreading to take it as one. The rules described here are exactly what makes a low-cost fund possible: a mechanical, published, self-rebalancing definition that requires no judgment and therefore no fee for judgment. The case for owning the market by rule rests on costs and on the difficulty of persistent selection, and nothing above touches either. The point is narrower and worth holding anyway — "the index" is a specific construction with specific consequences, and knowing them is the difference between owning it deliberately and owning it by default.

## Try it

Find out which index your main benchmark actually is, and look up two things: how many constituents it has, and what share of its value sits in its ten largest. Write both numbers in your Decision Journal next to your own portfolio's top-ten concentration. Most people are surprised in one direction or the other, and the comparison is the only honest way to say whether you are more or less concentrated than "the market".

## Takeaway

An index is a rulebook, not a photograph — so when you own one, know how it weights, what it counts as a share, and how much of it sits in its largest handful of names.
