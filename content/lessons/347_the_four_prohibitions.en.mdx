# The four prohibitions

Islamic finance screens what a business does and how it earns money against four prohibitions: *riba* (interest), *gharar* (excessive uncertainty in a contract), *maysir* (gambling), and dealing in a *haram* (forbidden) core activity. Underneath all four sits one organizing principle — Islamic finance prefers **risk-sharing over risk-transfer**: a lender who charges guaranteed interest regardless of outcome has transferred all the venture's risk onto the borrower, while a partner who shares in the actual profit or loss has shared the risk of the venture itself. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name each prohibition and explain the risk-sharing principle that ties them together — this is AMI teaching the published screening methodology, not issuing a religious ruling.

## Example

Compare two ways to fund the same $10M factory expansion. A conventional bank loan charges a fixed 7% annual interest regardless of whether the factory turns a profit — the lender is owed $700,000/year even in a year the borrower loses money. That guaranteed, outcome-independent return is *riba*. A *musharakah* (partnership) structure instead has the financier take, say, 30% of the factory's actual profit — if the factory earns $2M that year, the financier receives $600,000; if the factory loses money, the financier's return falls too. The dollar amounts can land close in a good year, but the *legal and risk structure* is opposite: one is a fixed claim regardless of outcome, the other is a shared claim on the outcome. *Gharar* shows up differently — a contract to sell "the fish I catch next month, price and quantity unknown" is void for excessive uncertainty, while a forward sale of "500kg of palm oil, delivered March, at $X/tonne" specifies enough that both sides know what they're agreeing to. *Maysir* is the clean case: a bet on Bursa Malaysia's KLCI closing above 1,600 next Friday, win-or-lose-nothing-in-between, is a wager, not an investment.

## The trap

Beginners assume Islamic finance is "no interest" and stop there, missing that *gharar* and *maysir* independently disqualify plenty of instruments that charge no interest at all. A commodity futures contract with genuinely undefined terms can fail on *gharar* even though nobody is charging riba. A lottery-style structured product can fail on *maysir* even though it's marketed as an "investment." The correct mental model: riba, gharar, maysir, and haram business activity are four *independent* filters, and a structure has to clear all four, not just the interest one. Screening only for "does it pay interest?" is exactly the shortcut that lets a genuinely gharar-heavy or maysir-heavy product slip through looking clean.

## Takeaway

Four independent filters — riba, gharar, maysir, and haram business activity — all have to clear, and the principle underneath all four is risk-sharing over risk-transfer.
