# Organic vs M&A-driven growth — separating the engine from the wrapper

<Lesson id="044_earnings_growth"/> introduced earnings growth and flagged that growth driven by acquisitions often masks declining same-store metrics. This lesson goes deep. Reported revenue or earnings growth combines two distinct sources: the existing business doing more (organic), and new businesses bolted on (acquired or inorganic). They have different durability profiles, different return profiles, and very different implications for the multiple the market is willing to pay. By the end of this lesson you should be able to decompose reported growth into organic and acquired components, recognise the "roll-up" pattern where serial acquirers hide a stagnant core, and apply a falsifiable rule for when M&A-driven growth deserves a growth multiple.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at a serial acquirer. Constellation Software (CSU.TO) has compounded revenue at over 20% annually for two decades — but the organic growth component is consistently in the low-to-mid single digits. The vast majority of the growth is acquired. The market has nonetheless paid a premium multiple because management has demonstrated decades of disciplined capital allocation: they buy small vertical-market software companies at low multiples, integrate cheaply, and earn 25%+ ROIC on the deployed capital. The organic growth is low, but the *capital deployment* is the durable engine.

Contrast that with a classic failed roll-up. Valeant Pharmaceuticals (now Bausch Health) drove revenue growth of 30–80% in the early 2010s primarily through serial acquisitions of off-patent drugs that they then re-priced. Organic growth was flat or negative; the entire reported growth was acquired. When the strategy hit limits in 2015–2016, the multiple collapsed, the debt taken on for acquisitions became unsustainable, and the stock fell over 90%. The growth was real on the income statement but the engine was a financing strategy, not an operating one.

A clean decomposition example: NSRGY (Nestle) in a typical year. Reported revenue growth might be 4–6%, decomposed roughly as: organic growth (volume + price/mix) of 3–4%, M&A net contribution of 1–2%, foreign exchange of -1 to +1%. The organic line is the operating health metric; M&A is the wrapper. Companies in this category disclose the decomposition in the quarterly report — read it.

A roll-up red flag: a company reports revenue growth of 30% with EPS growth of 25%. The press release celebrates "strong organic momentum". The acquisition footnote reveals five small acquisitions completed in the trailing 12 months, with combined acquired revenue equal to 24% of base revenue. Organic growth is 30% − 24% ≈ 6%. The headline is a lie. Companies that do this consistently usually trade at growth multiples until the market catches up, at which point the multiple compresses sharply and the buyer needs new acquisitions to maintain reported growth — a self-reinforcing trap.

On Bursa Malaysia: IHH Healthcare has grown both organically (more hospital beds, higher rates) and via acquisitions (Turkey, India). The disclosed organic same-store revenue growth is what an analyst should anchor on; the acquired growth contribution is mechanical and varies year-to-year with deal flow.

Falsifiable claim: when more than 50% of reported revenue growth comes from M&A in a given year, the reported number is wrapper-driven and should be discounted in valuation work — the multiple paid for that growth should be lower than for organic growth at the same headline rate. The signal invalidates if the acquirer's disclosed acquisition ROIC has consistently exceeded the cost of capital — Constellation Software being the canonical example — in which case the M&A is itself a value-creating activity rather than a growth wrapper.

## The trap

The trap is paying a growth multiple for acquired growth. A company growing 25% organically deserves a higher P/E than a company growing 25% via acquisitions of single-digit-organic-growth targets, because the organic grower's growth is self-funding and self-replicating, while the acquirer's growth requires continual capital deployment, integration risk, and balance-sheet expansion. The correct mental model: ask "where did each percentage point of the growth come from?" before accepting the headline. What invalidates the simple split: some acquisitions reveal latent organic growth (a strategic acquisition that unlocks distribution, for example) where the post-acquisition organic line is higher than the pre-acquisition combined organic lines would have been. Those are rare and usually disclosed by management — if they're not, assume they don't exist.

For each acquisition, give me the acquired ROIC and the cost of capital at the time of the deal." Save the decomposition. The organic line is the engine; everything else is a wrapper.

## Takeaway

Reported growth is the wrapper; organic growth is the engine. Pay a growth multiple for the engine, not the wrapper — and pay extra only if the capital allocator has a proven record of accretive deployment.
