# Backtesting a strategy

Backtesting runs your strategy rule against historical data to estimate how it would have performed. Done honestly, it tells you whether your rule has plausible edge and what its worst drawdown looked like. Done dishonestly — which is the default — it tells you a comforting story that has nothing to do with the future. The three killers are overfitting, look-ahead bias, and survivorship bias. After this lesson you can name each one, recognise when your own backtest has it, and design a test that gives you a result you can actually trust.

## How this plays out in real markets

Suppose you backtest a "buy NVDA when RSI(2) < 10, sell when RSI(2) > 80" rule across 2020–2024. The reported result: 47 trades, 78% win rate, average win 4.2%, max drawdown 6%. It looks like a money printer. Then you check three things. (1) Did you only run it on NVDA because NVDA went up 10x — survivorship bias? If you'd picked the rule blindly across the S&P 500 in 2019 you'd have applied it to dozens of names, many of which got delisted. (2) Did you use closing prices for the signal but assume execution at the same close — look-ahead bias? You can't know the close and trade at it. (3) Did you try 47 different RSI lookback/threshold combinations and keep only the best — overfitting? With 47 trials at the 5% level, several will look great by chance alone.

The honest version: define the rule BEFORE looking at the data, apply it to a universe defined ex-ante (the S&P 500 as of January 2020, including names that later got delisted), execute at the open of the day AFTER the signal, and report results from a single specification — no "I tried 30 and kept this one". When you do that on the NVDA example, the rule's edge survives but the win rate drops to 58% and max drawdown rises to 14% — a much honest answer.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is treating a backtest as proof. A backtest is a sanity check, not a forecast. The most-tempting wrong move is to keep tweaking parameters until the equity curve looks smooth — that's overfitting in slow motion. Every degree of freedom you add (a new indicator, a tighter threshold, a regime filter) is one more dimension on which to fit noise. The correct mental model: assume your backtest overstates real performance by some factor, design out-of-sample tests, and judge a rule by whether its edge is large enough to survive being half as good as the backtest claims. If a rule needs to be exactly as good as the backtest to be profitable, it's a bad rule.

## Takeaway

A backtest is a sanity check, not a forecast. If the rule needs to be exactly as good as the backtest to make money, the rule is dead — design for survival under half the in-sample edge.
