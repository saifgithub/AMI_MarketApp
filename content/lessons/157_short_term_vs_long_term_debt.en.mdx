# Short-term vs long-term debt mix

<Lesson id="034_debt_and_the_balance_sheet"/> introduced debt as a single line on the balance sheet. It is not. Debt has a tenor — short-term (due within 12 months) and long-term (due beyond 12 months) — and the mix of the two is a separate diagnostic. Short-term debt is cheaper, more flexible, and more dangerous: it must be repaid or rolled in the next 12 months, exposing the company to whatever interest-rate or credit environment exists when the roll happens. Long-term debt locks in a coupon and a date. By the end of this lesson you should know how to read the short-term debt line, the ratios that detect over-reliance on short-term funding, and the failure mode this configuration produced in the 2008 and 2023 banking stress episodes.

## How this plays out in real markets

The balance sheet typically separates: (a) "current portion of long-term debt" (long-term debt becoming due within the next 12 months); (b) "short-term borrowings" or "commercial paper" (debt issued with maturity under a year). Both count as current liabilities — the line on the balance sheet that has to be settled within the year.

Real example: AAPL in fiscal 2024 reported about $10B in commercial paper and ~$10B in current portion of long-term debt, against ~$162B in cash and marketable securities. The short-term debt is funded several times over by cash; AAPL uses commercial paper for working-capital convenience, not necessity. The ratio "short-term debt / cash" is roughly 0.1x — trivial.

Compare to a stressed scenario. Silicon Valley Bank in early 2023 held a balance sheet where uninsured deposits (effectively short-term liabilities to depositors) made up a vast majority of the funding base, while assets were locked in long-duration Treasuries that had lost market value in the rate-hike cycle. A duration mismatch — short funding, long assets — became fatal in a single news cycle when depositors moved $42B in a day. The headline solvency ratios looked healthy on a hold-to-maturity basis; the short-term funding structure made those ratios irrelevant under stress.

The diagnostic ratio is the **current ratio** (current assets / current liabilities) and its stricter cousin the **quick ratio** ((current assets – inventory) / current liabilities). A current ratio below 1.0 means the company doesn't have enough liquid assets to cover short-term obligations — it must either generate enough cash from operations within the year or roll the short-term debt forward. A current ratio below 0.7 in a non-financial company is a flashing red light.

On Bursa Malaysia: AIRASIA prior to the 2020 collapse had short-term obligations (working capital, lease payments, fuel hedging losses) that materially exceeded short-term liquid assets. The same balance sheet that had been workable in a steady-state environment became immediately distressed when cash flow vanished.

The falsifiable claim: a non-financial company with short-term debt exceeding 25% of total debt AND a current ratio below 1.0 faces material liquidity risk in any quarter where operating cash flow turns negative. The combination is the stressed configuration.

## The trap

The trap is netting short-term and long-term debt into a single "debt" number. A beginner sees "total debt $4B" and stops. They miss that $2.5B of that is due within 12 months at variable rates, while only $1.5B is long-term fixed. Two companies with identical $4B total debt — one 100% long-term fixed, the other 60% short-term variable — face profoundly different forward interest expense and liquidity risk. The correct mental model: total debt is one number; the *tenor mix* is a second number that must be read separately. Heavy short-term reliance is fine when cash flow is reliable and rates are stable. When either condition breaks, the short-term debt accelerates the stress.

## Takeaway

Total debt is one axis. Tenor is the second. Short-term debt is fine when cash flow is reliable; when cash flow turns, the short-term line is what breaks first.
