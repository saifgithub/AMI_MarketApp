# Pullback depth: Fibonacci zones, not magic numbers

This assumes you've completed <Lesson id="025_pullbacks"/> (pullbacks). Here we go deeper on a question <Lesson id="025_pullbacks"/> deferred: *how deep* should a pullback go before it stops looking healthy? Traders use the Fibonacci retracement levels — 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% — as common reference depths. These are zones, not magic numbers. They work as much because lots of participants use them (self-fulfilling coordination) as because of any mathematical mystery. By the end of this lesson you should be able to plot a Fibonacci retracement on any move, classify a pullback's depth as shallow, medium, or deep, and know what each depth typically implies about the trend's underlying strength.

## Example

Look at MSFT on the daily chart from 4 February to 26 March 2026. MSFT rallied from a swing low of $403.60 on 4 February to a swing high of $462.20 on 26 March — a $58.60 advance. Drawing a Fibonacci retracement from $403.60 to $462.20 gives:

- 38.2% retracement: $462.20 minus (0.382 × $58.60) = $439.80
- 50% retracement: $462.20 minus (0.500 × $58.60) = $432.90
- 61.8% retracement: $462.20 minus (0.618 × $58.60) = $426.00

The pullback that followed: MSFT topped at $462.20 on 26 March, then declined for nine sessions to a low of $441.20 on 8 April — a 36% retracement, just shallow of the 38.2% zone. Held there, formed a higher-low, and resumed the uptrend back to a new high of $478 by 22 April.

A shallow 36–40% retracement that held on first test is the strongest signal — trend is firm, buyers stepped in early. A medium 45–55% retracement holding suggests a trend that's tiring but intact. A deep 60–65% retracement holding is the last typical zone for a healthy pullback — beyond that, the math starts looking more like a reversal than a pullback. The 78.6% level is sometimes used as a final line, but at that depth most traders treat the trend as already broken.

The pullback thesis still invalidates per <Lesson id="025_pullbacks"/> on a daily close below the prior structural higher-low — Fibonacci zones are *guides for depth*, not invalidation rules. The actual line is still the swing low. For MSFT here the prior higher-low was $419, well below even the 61.8% level — so the structural floor and the Fibonacci floor were two different things.

## The trap

The trap is treating Fibonacci levels as precise price targets to trade against, instead of zones to set expectations. Beginners draw fibs, see "61.8% = $426.00", set a buy limit at $426.05 with a tight stop at $425.50, and get stopped out on a normal $1.50 wick that closes back at $429. The correct mental model: a Fibonacci level is a zone roughly 0.5%–1.5% wide. A pullback that bounces from anywhere in the 35–42% band is acting like a 38.2% pullback; one that bounces in the 47–53% band is acting like a 50% pullback. Trade the zone, not the tick. And remember the structural rule still rules: a pullback that respects 50% but breaks the prior higher-low has invalidated the trend regardless of what the fib level says.

## Try it

Pick a ticker that's currently in a clear uptrend and recently pulled back. In your charting app or in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Market Analyst, ask: "On [ticker], draw the Fibonacci retracement from the most recent swing low to swing high. What are the 38.2%, 50%, and 61.8% prices, and where did the pullback actually land?" Then check the structural higher-low separately. Save both the Fibonacci zones and the structural floor to your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> — note which line is closer to current price, because that's the line that will matter sooner.

## Takeaway

Fibonacci levels are zones, not magic — 38.2/50/61.8 are typical pullback depths for healthy trends, but the structural higher-low is the line that actually invalidates a trend, and depth never overrides structure.
