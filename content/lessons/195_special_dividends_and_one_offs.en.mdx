# Special dividends and one-offs — don't annualise what isn't recurring

<Lesson id="043_dividend_yield_and_payout_ratio"/> flagged that a "special" or one-time dividend inflates the trailing yield for one period and shouldn't be annualised. This lesson goes deep on why companies pay special dividends, how data feeds misreport the yield when one is in the trailing window, and the precise condition under which a special is a signal of strength versus a signal that the company has run out of better uses for the cash. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify a special dividend in trailing data, recompute the regular-dividend yield, and read what a special dividend is actually telling you about the business.

## How this plays out in real markets

Costco (COST) is the textbook serial special-dividend payer. The regular quarterly dividend is around $1.16 per share annualised, giving a regular yield near 0.5% at a stock price of ~$900. But Costco has paid five special dividends in the last decade, ranging from $5 to $15 per share. In the trailing 12 months following one of those payments, a naive yield calculation that includes the special would show a yield of 2% or higher — four times the regular yield. Most data feeds disclose the trailing 12-month total dividend; only the more sophisticated screens separate regular from special.

When does Costco pay one? Typically when the balance sheet has built up excess cash beyond what management considers needed for buybacks, capex, and operating flexibility — and when the tax environment is favourable for distributing it (special dividends in late 2012 were timed before an anticipated dividend-tax-rate increase, a classic case).

A different version: Texas Pacific Land (TPL) has paid sporadic special dividends tied to royalty windfalls from underlying oil and gas activity on its acreage. The yield in those years can be enormous (5–10%), but it isn't a recurring rate — it's the company distributing a windfall.

A failure case: a mature European industrial pays an unusually large special dividend in a year when it has just lost a major patent and earnings are expected to decline. The special is funded by drawing down cash reserves built up over years of strong margins. The market initially celebrates the special; over the next 24 months the regular dividend gets cut because the underlying earnings power has fallen and there's no more cash buffer to lean on. The special was a one-time consolation — and a tell that management didn't have a better use for the cash, including buybacks or reinvestment.

On Bursa Malaysia: some property-related names have paid special dividends after divesting non-core land at gain — the special reflects the one-time monetisation, not the going-concern earnings power. Reading the special as recurring would dramatically overstate the underlying yield.

Falsifiable claim: a company that pays a special dividend without a clear non-recurring source (M&A divestiture, regulatory windfall, accumulated excess cash with a stated buyback alternative considered and rejected) is signalling that management doesn't see a sufficiently accretive investment opportunity. That's neutral information at best, mildly negative at worst — it should not be read as a growth signal. The signal invalidates when the special is paid alongside a maintained or growing regular dividend and a healthy reinvestment program — Costco's pattern.

## The trap

The trap is annualising the trailing 12-month dividend including the special. A naive "TTM dividend yield" sorted screen will surface companies that just paid a one-time distribution, and the next 12 months won't repeat. The correct mental model: split the trailing dividend into regular and special; compute yield only on the regular portion. The special is a one-time cash event with no claim on future yield. What invalidates the simple reading: a company that has paid specials in 4 of the last 5 years on a roughly predictable cycle is effectively running a variable-rate regular dividend disguised as specials — and the trailing average is closer to the truth than the "regular only" view.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, paste a ticker that has paid any special dividend in the last 3 years and ask: "Split the trailing dividend history into regular and special. For each special, tell me the funding source (operating cash, asset sale, reserve drawdown) and whether the regular dividend was maintained afterward. Compute regular-only yield and TTM-inclusive yield separately." Save both numbers. The regular-only yield is the forward expectation; the TTM-inclusive yield is the screen's lie.

## Takeaway

A special dividend is a one-time event with no claim on future income. Compute yield on the regular dividend only — and read the special itself as information about what management couldn't find to do with the cash.
