# Capstone: reading the macro machine

You now hold the M16 toolkit: what GDP actually measures versus what the market prices, how inflation splits into demand-pull and cost-push (and why the exact real-return math matters once it runs hot), how the labor market feeds the Fed's reaction function, how to sort any indicator into leading/coincident/lagging before trusting it, and how the business cycle is actually dated versus the popular shortcut. This capstone runs one real, contradictory stretch of US macro history — 2022 to early 2023 — through all five lessons at once, because that period is the cleanest real-world case of every M16 concept disagreeing with every other one simultaneously.

## One scenario, every M16 concept

Here is the full contradiction, dated and sourced. **GDP:** US real GDP contracted in Q1 2022 (-1.6% annualized) and Q2 2022 (-0.6% annualized) — two straight negative quarters (BEA). **Inflation:** CPI year-over-year peaked at **9.1% in June 2022** (BLS), the hottest reading in over four decades. **Labor market:** despite the GDP contraction, unemployment kept falling, reaching **3.4% in January 2023** (BLS) — the lowest since 1969 — while payrolls added 517,000 jobs that same month. **Indicators:** ISM Manufacturing PMI spent over a year below the 50 expansion threshold (46.7 in November 2023, ISM) — a leading indicator flashing manufacturing-sector contraction the whole time the labor market stayed hot. **Business cycle:** despite satisfying the popular "two consecutive negative GDP quarters" recession rule in H1 2022, NBER never declared a recession, because its multi-indicator standard — payrolls, income, spending, production together — never showed the broad-based decline its definition requires.

Put all five together and you get the "no landing" narrative that dominated 2023 market commentary: an economy where GDP wobbled and manufacturing genuinely contracted, while the labor market and consumer spending stayed resilient enough that no recession was ever confirmed — even as the Fed hiked rates at the fastest pace in 40 years specifically to fight the inflation print that started this whole chain. Every M16 lesson is present in this one 18-month window: the GDP/market divergence, the real-versus-nominal stakes of 9.1% inflation, the labor market driving Fed policy in the opposite direction GDP alone suggested, a leading indicator (PMI) disagreeing with a lagging one (unemployment) for over a year, and a popular recession rule failing its most consequential real-world test.

## The steelman

The strongest case against reading 2022-2023 as one coherent "macro machine" story: "This is cherry-picked hindsight — with enough indicators, you can always find some combination that looks instructive after the fact. In real time, this period was genuinely confusing, and plenty of serious forecasters called a recession that never came." That's a fair challenge, and worth sitting with: this period is famous precisely *because* it broke the standard playbook (an inverted yield curve, historically a reliable recession signal, was flashing red the whole time too, and still no NBER recession followed as of this writing). The falsifier: the value of the M16 framework was never "predict the outcome with certainty" — it's "know which indicator is leading, which is lagging, and why they can legitimately disagree at a turning point." By that standard the framework worked exactly as designed: it explains *why* GDP, PMI, and unemployment told different stories at the same time, even though it couldn't tell you in advance which story would ultimately win.

## Takeaway

No single macro number tells the whole story — the skill is knowing which clock each indicator runs on and expecting them to disagree at turning points, not treating disagreement as noise to resolve.
