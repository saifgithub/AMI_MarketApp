# Hammer and inverted hammer

This assumes you've completed 020 (anatomy of a bar). Here we go deeper on two specific shapes: the hammer and its mirror, the inverted hammer. A hammer is a candle with a small body near the top of the range and a lower wick at least twice the body's height — sellers drove price down, buyers reclaimed almost everything. An inverted hammer flips it: small body near the bottom, long upper wick. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify both patterns, state the level-and-trend context that converts them into signals, and write down the exact bar that would invalidate either.

## Example

Look at META on the daily chart on 11 April 2026. META had been falling for eight sessions from $548 down to $498 — a 9% slide into a long-standing horizontal support zone near $495–$500 that the stock had bounced from in October and again in January. On 11 April, META opened at $501.20, sold off intraday to a low of $489.40, then ground back up to close at $503.80. Body of $2.60, lower wick of $11.80 — wick about 4.5x the body. Hammer at support, in a downtrend, with intraday volume 1.6x the 20-day average. The next session opened at $505.10 and closed at $515.20 — confirmation. Over the next eight sessions META pushed to $542.

A trader entering on the next-day open at $505.10 with a stop just below the hammer's low at $488.00 risked $17.10 to a target near the prior swing high of $548, a roughly 1:2.5 risk/reward.

The hammer invalidates the moment a subsequent daily candle closes below the hammer's low — in this case, a close below $489.40 voids the rejection read entirely. That's the falsification line you mark before you enter.

## The trap

The trap is buying a hammer anywhere on the chart, ignoring level and trend. A hammer in the middle of an uptrend with no support nearby is not a signal — it's a candle. The pattern works because it's a visible record of buyers defending a price that *mattered before*. Without a prior level to defend, there's nothing for the long wick to mean. The correct mental model: a hammer is a "rejection at a level" pattern. Required ingredients are (1) a preceding directional move, (2) the wick touching or piercing a known support zone, and (3) a body that closes back inside the prior range. Same checklist applies to the inverted hammer at resistance for short setups.

## Try it

Pick a stock that recently had a clear bounce off support. Scroll back to the bounce day in any charting view and inspect the candle. Then ask the Market Analyst in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "Was the candle on [date] a valid hammer? Confirm the body size, wick-to-body ratio, the level it defended, and the invalidation price." Save the invalidation price to your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> as the line you would have respected if you'd taken the trade.

## Takeaway

A hammer is a wick that defends a level, not just a shape — without a prior level beneath it, the long wick means nothing, and the invalidation is always a close below the hammer's low.
