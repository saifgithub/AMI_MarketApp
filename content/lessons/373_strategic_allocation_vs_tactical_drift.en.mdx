# Strategic allocation vs tactical drift

Most portfolios have no allocation policy; they have an accumulated history of individual decisions that nobody ever added up. After this lesson you will be able to separate the two decisions hiding inside every trade — the long-run shape you have chosen, and the short-run deviation you are taking from it — and to state which one you are making before you make it.

## Example

The **strategic allocation** is the shape you would hold if you had no view at all: the weights you would defend to someone who asked why your money sits where it sits. An openly hypothetical policy, chosen for illustration and not as a recommendation: **60% broad equities · 30% defensive · 10% cash**.

The **tactical deviation** is any deliberate departure from it, with a size, a reason and an end condition. Suppose you decide the equity sleeve is worth overweighting by five points for the next quarter. That is a legitimate decision, and it is a *complete* one only when it reads like this:

> Policy 60/30/10. Tactical: equities **+5 pp to 65%**, funded from cash. Reason: [the specific claim]. Reverts on: the claim resolving either way, or the end of the quarter, whichever comes first.

Three things make it a tactical deviation rather than drift. It has a **size** (five points, not "more"). It has a **funding source** — every overweight is an underweight somewhere, and naming which sleeve pays for it is what stops five separate five-point overweights adding to a portfolio that is 125% invested in conviction. And it has an **end condition**, so the deviation is a position rather than a permanent quiet change to the policy.

Drift is what you get when none of these are written down. The equity sleeve rises because equities rose, nobody decided anything, and six months later the book is at 74/20/6 — a materially more aggressive portfolio that no one chose. AMI enforces a per-trade size cap derived from your risk score and an account drawdown ceiling, and the Portfolio Manager checks each simulated trade against your Mandate — but a Mandate is a set of constraints, not an allocation policy. Nothing in the app will tell you that your book quietly became a different portfolio, because "the shape I intended" is a number only you hold.

## The trap

The trap is making tactical decisions one at a time and never seeing them as a portfolio. Each individual trade passes the test you apply to it — the thesis is decent, the size is within the cap, the Room's Verdict is worth evaluating. But those tests are per-trade, and the allocation question is per-portfolio, so a sequence of individually defensible trades assembles into a book you would never have chosen in one sitting.

The correct mental model is to write the policy *first*, on a day when you hold no view and nothing is moving, and treat every subsequent decision as a signed deviation from it. That ordering is the entire discipline: a policy written after the fact is not a policy, it is a description of what already happened, and it can never tell you that you have drifted because it moves whenever you do.

## The steelman

The strongest case against a written policy is that it is arbitrary — 60/30/10 rests on no theory that survives contact with a specific person's horizon, income and obligations, so a rigid commitment to an invented number can be worse than reacting sensibly as conditions change. That is fair: the *level* of a policy weight is much softer than it looks, and defending it as if it were derived is false precision. But the case for writing it down does not depend on the number being right. It depends on there being a fixed reference at all, because "have I drifted?" is unanswerable without one, and drift is the failure that actually shows up. The falsifier is easy to run on yourself: write your intended weights, then compute your current ones. If the gap is inside a couple of points, you were tracking your intent without needing the document and the sceptic wins for your case. If it is ten points and you cannot name the decisions that got you there, the drift was real, and it was invisible precisely because no reference existed.

## Try it

Before you open anything else, write your intended long-run weights on paper: how much in equities, how much defensive, how much in cash. Then open your Portfolio and compute what you actually hold. The gap between the two numbers is your accumulated drift, and it is almost always larger than people expect. If the gap is more than a few points, ask the Portfolio Manager in a 1-on-1 to walk you through what would have to be true for the current shape to be the right one — then judge that answer for yourself.

## Takeaway

Write the shape you would hold with no view at all, then make every departure from it a decision with a size, a funding source and an end date — anything else is drift wearing the costume of a strategy.
