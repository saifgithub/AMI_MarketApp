# When a metric doesn't apply: banks, insurers, REITs and utilities

A number can be correct on the financial statements and still tell you nothing about the business beside it. Free cash flow, net debt, EV/EBITDA, P/E and interest coverage were built for companies that make things and borrow to fund them. Banks, insurers, REITs and utilities break that assumption in four different ways, and this lesson names each one.

## Example

Start with a hypothetical bank reporting under IFRS. Its operating cash flow is not a measure of cash left after running the business, because lending is the business. IAS 7 says cash advances and loans made by financial institutions are usually operating activities, since they relate to the entity's main revenue-producing activity.

The numbers are invented and the arithmetic is simplified: operating cash flow equals net income, minus the growth in loans, plus the growth in deposits. Net income is 3.6bn in both years. Equity is 30bn and market value is 36bn, so ROE is 12% and P/B is 1.2x.

In year one, loans grow by 40bn and deposits by 34bn. Operating cash flow is 3.6 − 40 + 34 = −2.4bn. After 0.4bn of capex, free cash flow is −2.8bn, a yield of about −8% on 36bn.

In year two, earnings are identical, but loans grow by only 10bn while deposits grow by 30bn. Operating cash flow is 3.6 − 10 + 30 = +23.6bn. Free cash flow is +23.2bn, a yield of about +64%. The figure swung by 26bn while the bank earned exactly the same.

The same logic removes the other familiar tools. In Damodaran's phrase, debt is to a bank what steel is to a manufacturer: raw material. So net debt and EV/EBITDA lose their meaning. Interest paid, usually a bank's largest expense, is a cost of doing business rather than a financing charge, so interest coverage tells you little.

Note that the rulebook matters. Under US GAAP, loans made are investing activities and customer deposits are financing activities, so the same swings sit outside operating cash flow. A bank's free cash flow therefore depends on which accounting standard it follows, not just on how it performed.

Analysts read banks differently. P/B and ROE come first, as in <Lesson id="181_pb_for_banks_regulatory_book"/>.

Then three concepts. Net interest margin is net interest income as a share of average earning assets. The nonperforming loan ratio is impaired loans as a share of total gross loans. CET1 is a capital ratio; Basel sets its minimum at 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% buffer.

## Three more families, three different failures

**Insurers.** An insurer collects premiums before it pays the related claims. Buffett calls the resulting pool float: money held but not owned. A growing insurer can show rising cash while its obligations to policyholders rise with it, so free cash flow flatters growth.

A debt ratio has a similar blind spot, because it counts borrowing, not the money held for policyholders. Read book value and ROE instead, plus the combined ratio: claims and expenses as a share of premium. Below 100 means an underwriting profit. It excludes investment income, which is where float earns.

**REITs.** A REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to keep its tax status, per SEC staff guidance. On Bursa Malaysia, a listed REIT is exempt from tax at the REIT level if it distributes 90% or more of its total income to unit holders. Both rules are measured on tax income, not on EPS.

Accounting then pulls EPS away from the cash. US GAAP deducts real estate depreciation, which Nareit calls artificial. Under IFRS and its Malaysian twin MFRS, a REIT that values property at fair value books revaluation gains and losses in profit instead. A payout above 100% of EPS can be normal, and a profit that is mostly revaluation is not cash.

Read FFO instead: net income with real estate depreciation, certain property-sale gains and losses, and impairments removed. It is a supplementary measure, not an accounting standard. Pair it with distribution yield, the distribution per unit divided by the unit price. <Lesson id="314_reits_adrs_closed_end_funds"/> covers the structure.

**Utilities.** Here the number is correct but the usual reading is wrong. A regulator sets revenue as the rate base, the utility's long-lived investment net of depreciation, times an allowed return, plus operating expenses. So once the regulator allows it into the rate base, the spending that makes free cash flow negative is the spending that earns the return.

Malaysia's Energy Commission works the same way: the base tariff covers capex and opex plus a fair return on the regulated asset base. Negative free cash flow and heavy debt are sector-normal, as <Lesson id="159_when_high_debt_is_ok"/> and <Lesson id="188_net_debt_to_ebitda"/> show. Utilities and REITs also sit in the rate-sensitive cohort of <Lesson id="250_rate_sensitive_sectors_hiking_cutting"/>.

## The trap

The trap is running every company through one dashboard. A beginner sees free cash flow of −8% of market value and reads cash burn, because for a manufacturer that is exactly what it means. The number is real, so trusting it feels like discipline.

The correct mental model comes before the ratio. Ask what the business sells and where its raw material comes from. Then ask whether the figure is undefined for this business, distorted by its accounting, or accurate but normal for its sector. The same number can be right on the sheet and wrong as evidence.

## The steelman

The strongest objection: calling a figure not applicable throws away information. A bank's loan growth is a real choice, a REIT whose distribution outruns even FFO really is stretching, and a utility whose regulator turns hostile really can run out of road. A blanket rule can hide the stress it should catch.

The falsifier is specific. For a bank, check whether the figure moves with loan growth rather than earnings. For a utility, check whether the regulator still allows a return on the new spending. If those hold, the figure is noise. If they break, the figure was a warning all along.

## Takeaway

Before trusting a figure, ask what kind of business it describes: for a bank or insurer it may be undefined, for a REIT distorted by accounting, and for a utility accurate but normal, and the same number can be right on the sheet and wrong as evidence.

*Where this comes from: Damodaran's treatment of financial service firms in "Valuing Financial Service Firms", and the IAS 7 classification of lending as an operating activity.*
