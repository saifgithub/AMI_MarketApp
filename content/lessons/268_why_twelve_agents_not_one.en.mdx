# Why twelve agents and not one

A single confident answer feels like a product. A debate among twelve role-specialised agents feels like research — and like a richer training environment. <Term id="ami" /> is a <Term id="training_simulator" /> built the second way on purpose. One agent answering "should I buy NVDA?" would inherit every blind spot of the underlying language models in one shot — same training data, same tendencies, same omissions — and would also collapse the user's evaluation surface into a tip to accept or reject. Splitting the work into twelve roles forces the question through twelve different frames so you can practise reading the dissent. By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why cognitive diversity is structural, not decorative.

## How this plays out in real markets

<Term id="ami_convene_the_room" /> on AAPL. <Term id="twelve_agent_team" /> stream in parallel. The Fundamentals Analyst pulls services revenue (+14% YoY), iPhone mix, gross margin 46%. The Market Analyst reads the chart — $228, holding the 50-day MA at $221, RSI 61. The News Analyst flags an EU regulatory ruling from yesterday. The Social Media Analyst notes neutral retail sentiment with a small uptick on r/wallstreetbets. The Bull Researcher writes a case around services flywheel and capital return. The Bear Researcher counters with hardware deceleration and China exposure. The Research Manager weighs the two: Bull lean ~0.58. The Aggressive Debator wants 5% size; Conservative wants 2%; Neutral lands at 3%. The Trader proposes entry $227.50, stop $215, target $244, horizon 6 weeks. The PM compliance-checks against your <Term id="ami_mandate" />.

Now imagine the same question routed through one generalist agent. You would get a confident paragraph that conflates all of that into a single voice — and you would have no way to see *which* frame disagreed. The twelve-agent architecture preserves the dissent. The Bear case is a labelled section in your transcript. The Conservative Debator's smaller-size suggestion is recorded. You can re-open any one of them in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> and ask *"why?"*. A one-agent answer collapses twelve perspectives into a sentence; AMI keeps them separable, by design.

## The trap

The trap is wanting one clean answer because twelve voices feel like noise. They are not noise — they are the raw material of an honest second-opinion training run. Users who skim the headline <Term id="ami_verdict" /> and skip the per-agent reasoning are paying the cost of twelve agents and practising the evaluation of one. The right mental model: each agent is a simulated specialist consultant whose job is to be wrong in a different way from the others. The Bull Researcher overstates upside; the Bear Researcher overstates risk; the Research Manager's job is to be honest about which exaggeration the data supports more. You read all three. The disagreement is what the simulator is built to train you to evaluate.

## Try it

In **Convene the Room** on any liquid US ticker, scroll past the headline verdict and find the Bear Researcher's section first. Read it before the Bull case. Then scroll to the Research Manager's synthesis and see how much weight the Bear arguments got. If you can articulate the Bear case in one sentence after, you used the architecture as intended.

## Takeaway

Twelve role-specialised agents exist to preserve disagreement, not to amplify confidence. Read the dissent or you spent the credits on the headline. AMI is teaching you to evaluate analyst output, not handing you a tip to take elsewhere.
