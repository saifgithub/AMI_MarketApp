# Investing vs trading

Investing and trading both move money in and out of stocks, but they are different jobs with different time horizons, different sources of return, and different ways to fail. An investor owns a business through its share certificate and gets paid by the business growing over years. A trader rents price movement for days, weeks, or months and gets paid when other participants change their mind. Picking which one you are doing — on each individual position — is the single most important decision you will make before placing an order.

## Example

Take MSFT. An investor who bought 100 shares of MSFT at roughly $240 in early 2023 and simply held was sitting on shares worth around $410 two years later — a paper gain near 71% over 24 months, plus dividends. They did nothing. They were paid for the business growing.

A trader looking at the same MSFT chart in that window saw something different: a sharp rally to $340 by mid-2023, a pullback to $310, another leg up to $430, a drop to $385, then a recovery. A trader who caught two of those swings net of slippage might have made 25% over six months — better annualised, but only if they were right twice. If they were wrong on the second swing and got stopped out near $385 on the way down, they could easily have ended the year flat or red while the investor was up 50%+ on the same ticker.

Same chart. Same company. Two different jobs, two different scoreboards. The investor's job is selecting a business; the trader's job is reading other people.

## The trap

The trap is mixing the two without noticing. A beginner buys MSFT because "the long-term story is good" — an investor's reason — then watches the price daily, sells in a panic at the first 8% drawdown, and calls themselves "stopped out." That is not a trade exit; it is a destroyed investment thesis triggered by a price move that has nothing to do with the business. The opposite trap is just as common: opening a trade on a chart pattern and then "becoming an investor" the moment it goes against you, holding a losing trade for a year and calling it "long-term." Decide which one you are doing **before** you click buy, write it down, and let the reason for entry dictate the reason for exit.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_mandate" /> editor in Settings. At the top, set your default horizon to either "swing (days to weeks)", "position (weeks to months)", or "long-term (years)." Then open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and look at any past simulated entry. Ask yourself: did the horizon I picked at entry match the horizon I actually held it for? If not, that mismatch is the lesson.

## Takeaway

Decide whether each position is an investment or a trade **before** you enter, and let that decision — not the P&L — dictate when you exit.
