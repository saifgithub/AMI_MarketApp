# Bias inventory — anchoring, recency, confirmation, hindsight, sunk-cost

Most trading mistakes are not new — they are the same five cognitive biases, recombined. After this lesson you will be able to name each bias by its operational signature in trading, identify which one is driving any specific decision through a falsifiable test, and apply a process antidote that does not require you to stop *feeling* the bias — only to stop letting it touch the position size. The biases will not go away. The antidotes are the only thing that can.

## How this plays out in real markets

Five compressed cases, one per bias:

**Anchoring** — A trader bought TSLA at $250 in 2023. The stock fell to $185. He refused to sell because "I'm anchored to my entry; it'll come back." The chart had broken support at $215. Anchoring kept him in a 26% loser for four months past the structural invalidation.

**Recency** — Following a 3-week run where every breakout worked, the same trader sized a JPM breakout at 2x mandate "because breakouts have been working." JPM was the first breakout in five weeks that failed. Recency made the most recent regime feel like the only regime.

**Confirmation** — Long NVDA, he read 11 bullish takes from the Bull Researcher and skipped a Bear Researcher report flagging a margin warning. The margin warning materialised in earnings two weeks later. Confirmation filtered the information set to match the position.

**Hindsight** — After GME pulled back from $325 to $54, he wrote in his journal: "It was obvious it was going to crash." It was not obvious in real time — it was obvious in hindsight, and the journal entry he wrote retrospectively destroyed his ability to learn from his actual real-time decision process.

**Sunk-cost** — He held PETRONAS through a 22% drawdown despite an invalidated thesis because "I've already lost so much, selling now locks it in." The loss was already locked in the moment the thesis invalidated; refusing to sell only converted a defined loss into an open-ended one. PETRONAS fell another 14% before he capitulated.

Each bias has a falsifiable signature, summarised: anchoring — exit reluctance correlated to entry price rather than chart level; recency — sizing or conviction correlated to last 5 trades' outcomes; confirmation — research consumption skewed >70% to bullish/bearish based on existing position; hindsight — journal entries describe past decisions as "obvious"; sunk-cost — hold rationale references prior loss rather than forward thesis.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that knowing about a bias prevents it. It does not. Awareness of confirmation bias does not stop you from reading more bullish takes when you are long. The correct mental model: biases are not bugs to debug — they are constants to engineer around. The antidote for each bias is a process step, not a feeling: for anchoring, require the exit decision to reference the chart-drawn invalidation level, not the entry price; for recency, require sizing to reference the written <Term id="ami_mandate" />, not the rolling 5-trade outcome; for confirmation, require at least one Bear Researcher consultation before any entry over a defined size threshold; for hindsight, require journal entries to be written *before* knowing the outcome; for sunk-cost, require the hold-rationale to be re-articulated weekly without reference to the prior loss. The Conservative Risk Officer's role is to ask the bias-shaped question for each one — what level, what mandate, what bear case, what was the real-time thesis, what is the forward thesis. Process beats awareness every time.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and audit five recent entries against the bias signatures: anchoring (does the exit reasoning reference entry price?), recency (is sizing correlated to recent outcomes?), confirmation (is the research mix >70% aligned to position?), hindsight (does the entry describe the trade as 'obvious'?), sunk-cost (does the hold rationale reference prior losses?). For each detected signature, write the corresponding process antidote into your mandate or journal template. Then open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Risk Officer and paste the five entries — ask which biases it can detect from the language alone. The match is usually high.

## Takeaway

Biases are not bugs to debug — they are constants to engineer around. Awareness is not enough; the antidote for each bias is a process step that runs whether you feel the bias or not.
