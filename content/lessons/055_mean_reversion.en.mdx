# Mean reversion

Mean reversion bets the opposite of trend-following: when price stretches far from its moving average, it tends to snap back. It works when markets are range-bound or when an instrument has stable underlying demand. It is the most dangerous strategy in this module because the entry feels like catching a falling knife, and a single trade that doesn't revert can wipe out months of small reversion wins. After this lesson you can specify a mean-reversion rule that knows the difference between a normal stretch and a regime change.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at JPM in mid-2024. The stock had a stable rising channel and oscillated around its 20-day MA. On July 12, 2024, after a strong Q2 print, JPM gapped down ~5% to ~$203 from a prior $213, taking RSI(2) to 4 — an extreme oversold reading. A simple mean-reversion rule: enter long when RSI(2) closes below 5 AND price is above the 200-day MA (regime filter). Exit when price closes back above the 5-day MA. JPM did exactly that — closed back above the 5-day three sessions later near $209, a roughly 3% snapback. A clean 3R if the stop was at $200 (a daily close below $200 invalidating).

Entry criteria: RSI(2) < 5 (or another extreme-oversold trigger) AND price > 200-day MA (the regime filter that keeps you out of downtrends) AND no negative news catalyst (a 5% gap-down on guidance withdrawal is not the same as a 5% gap-down on macro noise). Stop: daily close below the pre-gap swing low. Target: close back above the 5-day MA, OR a fixed-time exit at 5 sessions if it hasn't reverted. Individual-trade invalidation: a daily close below the stop. The strategy FAILS in trending bear markets and during true-regime-change events: in 2008, RSI(2) printed below 5 on most US large-caps and they kept going down for months. Mean reversion in a bear market is how you blow up.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is mean-reverting without the regime filter. RSI says oversold, you buy, and you're long a stock in a structural downtrend. Every day it gets "more oversold" and you average down. The correct mental model: mean reversion is a counter-noise strategy, not a counter-trend strategy. The 200-day MA filter exists exactly for this — it tells you whether the noise is mean-reverting around a healthy trend or whether the trend itself has flipped. No filter, no trade. And if there's a real fundamental catalyst (guidance cut, fraud, ratings downgrade) the statistical edge evaporates; oversold readings on broken companies stay oversold.
