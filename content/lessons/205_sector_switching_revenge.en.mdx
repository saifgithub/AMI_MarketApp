# Sector-switching revenge — same tilt, different ticker

The cleverest form of revenge trading does not return to the ticker that hurt you. It opens a position in a *different* sector — and your brain accepts the move as "diversification" or "fresh perspective" because it is no longer the loser's chart. After this lesson you will be able to recognise this disguise, name the bias underneath (cognitive distancing, <Lesson id="047_revenge_trading"/> extended), and apply the timing test that catches sector-switching revenge regardless of which industry the new trade lives in. The market does not care about your sector mix. Your tilt does not care either.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader on Bursa Malaysia stopped out of MAYBANK at -RM1,800 at 10:20am on a Tuesday. He felt the urge to "re-enter MAYBANK to make it back" and consciously suppressed it because he had read about revenge trading the week before. At 10:55am he opened a long on TENAGA — a utility, a different sector entirely — sized 1.8x his normal per-trade risk. "Diversifying away from the financials," he told himself. TENAGA stopped out at -RM2,100 at 1:40pm. At 2:15pm he opened GENTING, a consumer/gaming name. "Different sector again, fresh perspective." GENTING closed -3.2% on the day for -RM2,500. Total session loss: RM6,400 on three trades in three different sectors. He had not revenge-traded MAYBANK. He had revenge-traded *himself*, in three different costumes.

The falsifiable sector-switching revenge fingerprint, as a compound test independent of ticker: (1) two or more entries placed within 90 minutes of a closed loss; (2) at least one of those entries in a sector different from the original loss; (3) average size of the post-loss entries is 1.3x or more of the trader's documented per-trade risk; (4) the trades collectively were not on the written <Term id="ami_watchlist" /> at the start of the session. Three of four boxes ticked is a tilt session — the sector mix is camouflage, not diversification.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that the loser ticker (or its sector) is the carrier of the tilt. It is not. The tilt is carried by *you*, and changing the ticker changes only the disguise. Switching the symbol is avoidance, not distance: the reminder is gone, the tilt is not, and because the new chart never hurt you, your tilt-detection never fires. The correct mental model: your cooldown rule applies to *the trader*, not to the instrument. If you've told yourself you can't trade MAYBANK in the next 60 minutes because you just lost on MAYBANK, you also can't trade TENAGA or GENTING in that window — because the trader entering them is the same trader who just lost on MAYBANK. Nothing in AMI enforces this scope for you — no field, no block — so write your own rule as trader-wide, not per-ticker, and hold yourself to it across the whole <Term id="ami_watchlist" />. The Conservative Debator in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> is where you go to have that held up against your actual behaviour.

## Try it

Write down your cooldown rule with scope = trader (not per-ticker) — AMI has no field for this, so the definition lives wherever you keep it (a note, the top of your Decision Journal). If your working version has been per-ticker, rewrite it. Then open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, filter to your last 20 stop-outs, and tag each follow-up entry by sector relative to the original loss. If you see a cluster of "different sector, within 90 minutes" entries with elevated size, you have a documented sector-switching tilt pattern. Take one example to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and ask: "Was this trade on my watchlist before the loss?" The answer is the diagnosis.

## Takeaway

The cooldown applies to the trader, not to the ticker — and since AMI doesn't hold that scope for you, the rule only works if you write it and check it that way yourself. A trade in a different sector immediately after a loss is the same tilt with a fresh costume.
