# Monte Carlo simulation

A single historical backtest shows you one path — the one sequence of wins and losses that actually happened. Monte Carlo simulation asks a different question: given a strategy's true win probability and payoffs, what does the *distribution* of possible outcomes look like across many randomly generated paths, including the unlucky ones the one historical path never happened to show you? It converts "here's the one equity curve that occurred" into "here's the range of equity curves that were possible, and how often each kind occurs."

## Example

Recall the 60%-win, +$500/-$300 setup from the expected-value lesson (EV = +$180/trade). A single historical backtest might show a smooth, ever-climbing equity curve — but that's only one path out of many the same 60/40 process could have produced. Monte Carlo simulation asks: across many random 100-trade sequences drawn from this same 60%/40% process, how often does a bad losing streak occur? Take the simplest tail case — five losses in a row. Probability of five consecutive losses = 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.4⁵ = **0.01024**, or about **1.02%**.

That means in roughly 1 out of every 100 stretches of five trades, this genuinely positive-EV strategy produces a five-loss streak — a real, quantifiable tail event, not a sign the edge is broken. A single historical backtest, which only ever shows the one sequence that happened to occur, might never have contained a five-loss streak at all — giving a falsely smooth impression of a process that, run out across many possible paths, produces real bad stretches on a predictable schedule.

## The trap

The trap is looking at one smooth historical equity curve and assuming that's a representative picture of what the strategy will do going forward — when it's just the one path that happened to occur, out of a much wider distribution of paths the same underlying probabilities could produce. It feels reassuring because it's the *actual* history, not a hypothetical. The correct habit: even with genuinely positive EV, ask what a Monte Carlo simulation of many random paths from the same win/loss probabilities would show — including how often a losing streak of a given length shows up — so a real drawdown doesn't get mistaken for the edge failing.

## Try it

Take a strategy's win rate and payoff sizes from your Decision Journal and hand-calculate the probability of a 3-loss or 5-loss streak by raising the loss probability to that power. Compare it to whether you've actually experienced a streak that long. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

One historical equity curve is one path among many a strategy's true probabilities could produce — calculate the odds of a real losing streak directly, rather than judging risk by the one path that happened to occur.
