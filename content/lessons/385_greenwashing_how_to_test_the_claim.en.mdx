# Greenwashing: how to test the claim

Greenwashing is rarely a lie; it is usually a true sentence engineered to be unfalsifiable. After this lesson you will be able to sort a sustainability claim into one of three buckets — a commitment you can check, a process description that commits to nothing, and an impression with no content — and you will know which regulators have forced which claims to become checkable.

## Example

Three claims of the kind that appear in fund literature. They read alike and they are not alike at all.

**"At least 80% of the fund's assets are invested in issuers meeting the sustainability criteria described in the prospectus."** This is a **commitment**. It names a threshold, and it points at a definition you can go and read. If the portfolio drifts below the threshold, something has been broken.

**"Our investment process integrates ESG factors into fundamental research."** This is a **process description**. It is almost certainly true — considering a factor is not a high bar — and it constrains nothing about what the fund holds. A fund that considers ESG and buys the company anyway has satisfied it completely.

**"We are committed to a sustainable future."** This is an **impression**. Nothing about it can be checked, and nothing about the portfolio would contradict it.

Regulators have gone after the first gap in a specific way: forcing names to mean something. Under the U.S. Names Rule (Rule 35d-1), a fund whose name suggests a particular type of investment must adopt a policy to invest at least **80%** of its assets accordingly. The amendments adopted in **September 2023** extended that requirement to names suggesting *particular characteristics* — explicitly including terms indicating that the fund's investment decisions incorporate ESG factors. So a fund calling itself "sustainable" now has to have an 80% policy behind the word. (The compliance dates run on a size schedule: **11 June 2026** for fund groups above $1 billion in net assets, **11 December 2026** below it — the position as at August 2026, and worth re-checking, since the rule has been under a further SEC review.)

The EU took a different route. **SFDR — Regulation (EU) 2019/2088** — classifies products rather than names: an **Article 8** product *promotes* environmental or social characteristics; an **Article 9** product has *sustainable investment as its objective*. The label is a disclosure category, not a quality grade, and this is where the second-order confusion arrives: Article 8 covers an enormous range of products, from a fund with one exclusion to one built entirely around a theme, so "Article 8" is a statement about what a manager must disclose rather than a rating of how green the fund is.

The test that survives both regimes is one question: **what holding would contradict this claim?** For the 80% commitment there is an answer. For "integrates ESG factors" there is none, and that is the diagnostic — not evidence of dishonesty, but proof that the sentence is doing no work.

## The trap

The trap is grading the claim by its intensity rather than by its content. Strong language — *committed*, *deeply embedded*, *at the heart of everything we do* — reads as a stronger promise than a flat sentence about 80% of assets, and it is the reverse: the emphatic version is unfalsifiable and the dull one is enforceable. Marketing writes the emphatic sentence precisely because it costs nothing.

The correct mental model: read for the **falsifier**, not the adjective. Ask what would have to be in the portfolio for the claim to be false, and if you cannot construct such a holding, the claim has no content whatever its tone. This is the same discipline as reading a factor claim in <Lesson id="382_reading_a_factor_claim_someone_hands_you"/> and the same one that makes an invalidation condition worth writing in a decision journal — a statement that cannot be wrong cannot inform anything.

## The steelman

The strongest case against this framework: process claims are not empty. A manager who genuinely integrates environmental and governance analysis into fundamental research may avoid a company heading for a large regulatory penalty precisely because the analysis flagged it — a real effect on a real portfolio, produced by exactly the kind of statement dismissed above as committing to nothing. Demanding a checkable threshold from every claim would push managers toward mechanical exclusion rules that are easy to verify and worse at the underlying job. That argument has genuine force, and the reply is not that process is worthless. It is that a process claim is a claim about *capability*, and it should be assessed the way you assess any capability claim: by asking for instances. The falsifier is available and specific — ask the manager to name a position they declined or exited on these grounds and what the analysis showed. A manager doing the work can answer immediately and concretely. One who cannot has told you the sentence was marketing, and has done so without you having to argue about the framework at all.

## Try it

Find any fund, index or product you have seen described as sustainable — in an app, an advert, a factsheet — and sort its central claim into the three buckets: commitment, process, impression. Then write the sentence that would make it false. If you cannot write that sentence, you have learned something about the claim. Bring the same test to AMI: open Settings → Mandate, read the explanation under the ESG-lite flag, and ask yourself which bucket the app's own wording falls into.

## Takeaway

Read sustainability claims for the falsifier, not the adjective — and when the claim is about process rather than portfolio, ask for the instances instead of arguing about the words.

*Where this comes from: the SEC's Names Rule 80% requirement as extended to ESG-suggestive names (2023), and the SFDR Article 8 / Article 9 product disclosure categories.*
