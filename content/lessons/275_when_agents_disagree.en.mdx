# When agents disagree — reading conflict in the verdict

A verdict where all <Term id="twelve_agent_team" /> lean the same way is suspicious. A verdict with visible disagreement is honest. <Term id="ami" /> is built on the premise that the Bull and Bear Researchers should be sharply opposed in their drafts, that the Risk Debators should land at different sizes, and that the Research Manager's synthesis should weight which side the data supports more — without erasing the loser. Reading the disagreement is how you separate strong-conviction setups from coin-flips dressed in a structured verdict. By the end of this lesson you will be able to read the conflict layer and use it.

## How this plays out in real markets

<Term id="ami_convene_the_room" /> on META. Two scenarios with the same headline verdict, *BUY 3%*, but very different internals.

**Scenario A — narrow disagreement.** Bull Researcher writes a five-paragraph case (Reels monetisation, ad pricing recovery, Reality Labs cost discipline). Bear Researcher's case is two paragraphs hedged with *"if ad spend re-accelerates"*. Research Manager: Bull lean 0.68. Aggressive Debator 4%, Conservative 2.5%, Neutral 3%. Tight cluster. Trader: entry $612, stop $588, target $660, confidence 0.71. **You read this and see broad agreement with the Bear case essentially failing to land.**

**Scenario B — wide disagreement.** Bull writes the same case. Bear writes a strong counter (Reality Labs burn, regulatory risk in EU, antitrust). Research Manager: Bull lean 0.52 — barely. Aggressive Debator 5%, Conservative 1%, Neutral 3%. Wide cluster. Trader: entry $612, stop $588, target $660, confidence 0.54. **Same headline — *BUY 3%* — completely different conviction.**

The headline is identical. The <Term id="ami_verdict" /> body tells you these are not the same trade. Scenario A is the team broadly aligned on a long-side lean. Scenario B is the team barely on the long side, with the Bear case forceful and the Risk Debators spread wide. A reader who looks only at the headline cannot distinguish them. A reader who reads the disagreement can.

The disagreement signal also surfaces in the confidence number. 0.71 in Scenario A reflects broad alignment; 0.54 in Scenario B reflects a near-coin-flip. Same headline, different conviction — and the confidence is the Room's *weighted lean*, not a calibrated probability.

## The trap

The trap is treating agreement as confirmation. Two human analysts who never disagree are not validating each other; they are confirming each other's biases. The same is true of the underlying language models behind AMI's agents — if they all converge effortlessly on the same view, that may reflect shared training-data tendencies rather than independent confirmation. AMI's role-prompted Bull and Bear Researchers are *designed* to disagree because forced opposition is what surfaces the weak parts of each case. A debate where Bull and Bear visibly disagree is a debate working as intended. A unanimous verdict is the one worth checking twice.

## Try it

In **Convene the Room** on a recent <Term id="ami_watchlist" /> ticker, open the Verdict and write down three numbers before reading further: Bull/Bear lean from Research Manager, Risk Debator range (max % minus min %), and confidence. Use those three together — not the headline — to decide whether the trade is "high conviction" or "borderline" for you.

## Takeaway

Read the disagreement, not just the headline. Tight cluster + high lean is conviction; wide spread + ~0.5 lean is a coin flip wearing a Verdict's clothes. AMI is teaching you to evaluate analyst output — the disagreement is the practice surface, not a recommendation to follow.
