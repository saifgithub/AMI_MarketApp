# Meme-momentum FOMO

Meme-momentum FOMO is the specific FOMO flavour that happens when crowd attention, not chart structure, is the *only* reason a ticker is moving. The price goes vertical because new buyers arrive because new buyers are arriving. Building on lesson 048, this lesson gives you the diagnostic that separates meme momentum from a legitimate breakout, the falsifiability of "I am late to this," and the rule that converts a meme-FOMO urge into either a documented research note or a skip. The dangerous part is not that meme moves end. It is that they end on a single candle.

## How this plays out in real markets

January 2021, GME: the chart went from $20 to $80 in five sessions, then $80 to $325 in three. February 2021, AMC: $5 to $20 in seven sessions on the same social-media engine. In both cases, the Social Media Analyst's post-volume score was running 8–15x the 90-day median — not 3x, not 5x, but order-of-magnitude. The fundamental analyst reports for both companies in those weeks were unchanged. The chart had no prior structural breakout; the move was, mechanically, short-squeeze-driven and attention-driven. Traders who entered GME in the $250–$325 range — within 10% of the all-time high — saw the position print $54 within two sessions. The trades that closed green were the ones where the trader exited before the crowd did; the trades that closed -60% to -85% were the ones where the trader was the crowd.

A counter-example, same period: NVDA's breakout above $130 in early 2021 had post-volume elevation of roughly 1.4x the 90-day median, a clean prior consolidation, a fundamental catalyst (datacenter revenue ramp), and a multi-week base. That was a legitimate breakout you could enter on a retest. The chart looked similar at a glance to GME. The crowd signature did not.

## The trap

The trap is treating all vertical charts the same. They are not. A legitimate breakout has a chart-drawn base, a measurable catalyst, and a sentiment elevation in the 1.5–3x range. A meme-momentum move has no base, no catalyst, and sentiment elevation often above 5x median. The price action looks identical for the first three days; the underlying participation does not. The correct mental model: if the *only* visible cause of the move is crowd attention, you are not analysing a setup — you are betting on the timing of strangers' exits. That is not a strategy; that is a timing test. And the timing test has no edge for a late entrant, because the people with the lowest cost basis are the ones who get to decide when the move ends.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and filter to any past trade where the ticker was up more than 50% in the prior two weeks at entry. For each, log the Social Media Analyst's post-volume multiple on the entry day and whether a chart-drawn base existed. Compute the win rate. Then open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Social Media Analyst and ask: "What is the historical post-trade outcome distribution for entries into post-volume above 5x median?" Compare that distribution to your own track record on these trades.

## Takeaway

A meme-momentum chart is a timing bet on strangers' exits, not a setup. If the only visible cause of the move is crowd attention, the late entry has no edge — the lowest cost basis decides when it ends.
