# Distributions & fat tails

A distribution describes how likely each possible outcome is, and the normal ("bell curve") distribution — the one most risk models quietly assume — says extreme moves should be vanishingly rare, shrinking toward zero probability the further you get from the average. Real market returns don't behave that way: they're **fat-tailed**, meaning extreme moves happen far more often than a normal distribution predicts. A model that assumes normality will keep telling you a crash "shouldn't" happen right up until one does — because the model, not the market, is what's wrong.

## Example

Take a stock with typical daily volatility (standard deviation, or "sigma") of about **1%** — a fairly ordinary day moves the price roughly ±1%. On October 19, 1987 ("Black Monday"), the S&P 500 fell **20.5%** in a single day. Measured against a 1% daily sigma, that's a **20.5-sigma event** (20.5% ÷ 1% = 20.5). Under a normal distribution, a move that extreme has a probability so close to zero it would not be expected to happen even once across the entire history of the universe repeated many times over — and yet it happened, in one trading day, within recorded market history.

That gap — "the model says essentially impossible" versus "it happened" — is the fat-tail problem in one number. It isn't that Black Monday was a freak coincidence outside the system; it's that the normal distribution is the wrong tool for describing how markets actually move at the extremes. Fat-tailed distributions assign meaningfully more probability to large moves, which is why risk models built only on "typical" daily volatility keep getting blindsided by the moves that matter most.

## The trap

The trap is treating a risk estimate built on normal-distribution math ("a move this big is a 6-sigma event, essentially impossible") as if it settles the question of whether that move can happen. It feels rigorous because it's quantified — a precise-sounding sigma count reads as more trustworthy than a vague "markets can do surprising things." The correct habit: treat any "this can't happen" claim built on normal-distribution assumptions as a description of the *model's* comfort zone, not a hard limit on the *market* — and size risk assuming the tail is fatter than the model says.

## Takeaway

Markets move in fat tails, not bell curves — treat any "this is essentially impossible" risk claim built on normal-distribution math as understating the real chance of an extreme move, not as settling the question.
