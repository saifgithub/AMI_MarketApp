# Long-term wealth building

Long-term wealth building is the deliberately unsexy practice of owning quality businesses, reinvesting the cash they generate, and letting time do most of the work. It is not the same as "buying anything and holding forever" — that is hope. It is selecting a small number of resilient businesses or broad indexes, sizing them so a single bad pick cannot derail the plan, and committing to a horizon long enough that compounding has room to operate. The hard part is not finding the right stock; the hard part is staying in the seat for the 15+ years where the curve actually bends.

## How this plays out in real markets

Three real long-term holds, twenty-year-ish window (split-adjusted, dividends reinvested):

- **AAPL** — January 2005 close around $1.20 (split-adjusted); January 2025 close around $245. Approximately **204×** over 20 years, or roughly **29% annualised**. Investors who held through the 2008 crisis (AAPL fell roughly 60% peak-to-trough), the 2013 sell-off, and 2022's 30% drawdown captured all of it. Investors who sold in any of those drawdowns to "lock in gains" captured a small slice.
- **MSFT** — early 2005 around $26; early 2025 around $410. About **16× over 20 years**, roughly **15% annualised**, on top of growing dividends. Notable: MSFT spent most of 2000–2012 going sideways. The patient holder earned nothing visible for over a decade, then almost everything in the second half.
- **BRK-B** — early 2005 around $59; early 2025 around $475. About **8×** over 20 years, roughly **11% annualised**. Lower volatility than the tech examples, lower peak return, less psychological wear.

On Bursa Malaysia: **MAYBANK** (Malayan Banking Berhad) closed around RM5 in early 2005 and around RM10 in early 2025, plus an average dividend yield in the 5–7% range — total return roughly **3.5–4× over 20 years**, around **6–7% annualised** on price plus a high dividend stream. Lower headline return than US tech, but a steady cash-paying compounder that survived multiple regional crises.

The pattern across all four: the return came from staying invested, not from trading. Anyone who exited and re-entered "tactically" during the 20-year window mostly underperformed the buy-and-hold baseline.

## The trap

The trap is **selection bias dressed as strategy**. It is easy to look back and pick the obvious winners (AAPL, MSFT) and conclude "buy great companies and hold forever." But the same era contained Sears, GE in its decline, Nokia, Yahoo, and a long list of "blue chips" that destroyed real money. The correct mental model is not "pick the winner and hold" — it is "spread across enough quality businesses (or use a broad index) that you survive the losers and let the winners do the heavy lifting." A single 100× winner in a 30-stock portfolio is enough to carry the portfolio; a single 100× winner in a 1-stock portfolio is luck.

## Try it

Open <Term id="ami" />'s <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Portfolio Manager and ask: "Given my current <Term id="ami_mandate" />, what would a simulated 20-year buy-and-hold allocation look like across 4–6 quality businesses or a broad index proxy?" Write the PM's suggested allocation into the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> as a baseline plan. Revisit it every quarter — not to trade it, but to confirm nothing fundamental about each business has changed.

## Takeaway

Long-term wealth building is selecting enough quality businesses that you survive being wrong about any one of them, then staying in the seat for the 15+ years where compounding does the work.
