# Debt and the balance sheet

The balance sheet is the snapshot of what a company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and what's left for shareholders (equity). Debt sits on the liability side and behaves differently from every other corporate obligation: it has interest, a maturity date, and consequences for missing payments. By the end of this lesson you should know how to read a company's debt load in context, why an absolute debt number is almost meaningless on its own, and the specific configuration of debt and earnings that historically precedes balance-sheet stress.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at AAPL and XOM as of fiscal 2024. AAPL carried roughly $107B in long-term debt against ~$162B in cash and marketable securities — net cash positive by about $55B. Its debt-to-equity ratio sat around 1.5 on the surface, but on a net-debt basis the company effectively has no debt; it borrows because it's cheap, not because it has to. XOM carried about $37B in long-term debt against ~$23B in cash on a much larger equity base, with debt-to-equity around 0.2 — a far smaller leverage ratio, but with much higher earnings volatility tied to oil prices. Same word ("debt") in two very different shapes.

Now look at a stressed case. When BBBY (Bed Bath & Beyond) entered 2023 with roughly $1.2B in debt against a market cap that had collapsed below $200M and operating losses running at hundreds of millions a quarter, the absolute debt number ($1.2B) wasn't the problem — it was that interest expense was consuming more cash than the business was generating. The bankruptcy filing came in April 2023. The signal was visible months earlier in the interest-coverage ratio: operating income divided by interest expense. When that ratio drops below 1.5 and the trend is falling, the company is paying lenders faster than it's earning from operations — and the only way to keep going is either to grow earnings fast or to refinance into more debt. Both get harder under stress.

On Bursa Malaysia: AIRASIA carried significant aircraft-lease and borrowing obligations into the 2020 travel collapse. Revenue effectively vanished; debt did not. The same balance sheet that had been workable in a normal demand environment became immediately distressed. Debt is fine until cash flow disappears — then it isn't.

## The trap

The trap is judging debt by its dollar size instead of by its weight relative to earnings and cash. A beginner sees "$107B in debt" at AAPL and panics; meanwhile, they ignore a $1B debt load at a money-losing small-cap because the number "looks smaller". The correct mental model: debt is only dangerous when the company can't comfortably service it. Three ratios do that comparison — debt-to-equity (size of debt vs shareholder capital), debt-to-EBITDA (how many years of cash earnings the debt represents), and interest coverage (how many times operating income covers interest expense). A debt-to-EBITDA above 4x combined with interest coverage below 2x is the classic stress configuration. Below those thresholds, debt is mostly a capital-structure choice. Above them, it's a vulnerability.

## Takeaway

Debt is a number with context — judge it against EBITDA and interest expense, not against itself, and watch interest coverage as the early-warning gauge that absolute debt figures will always hide.
