# DCF basics: why terminal value eats everything

<Lesson id="037_valuation_what_is_a_stock_worth"/> introduced the DCF idea: a stock is worth the present value of future cash. The dirty secret of DCF models is that for most growth companies, 60–80% of the calculated intrinsic value comes from the *terminal value* — the catch-all assumption about what happens after year 10. Change the terminal-growth rate by 1 percentage point or the discount rate by 50 basis points and the "fair value" output moves by 20–40%. By the end of this lesson you should understand why DCF outputs are sensitivity ranges rather than point estimates, and the specific terminal-value assumptions that turn a DCF into a fantasy.

## How this plays out in real markets

Build a simple DCF for MSFT as of mid-2026. MSFT generates ~$74B in trailing free cash flow. Assume FCF grows 12% per year for years 1–10, then 3% in perpetuity. Discount at a 9% rate.

Year-by-year explicit FCF (years 1–10) sums to roughly $1.4 trillion in present-value terms. The terminal value — using the Gordon Growth Model: FCF_year10 × (1 + g) / (r − g), then discounted back to today — comes in around $2.0 trillion in present-value terms. Total enterprise value: ~$3.4T, near the market's mid-2026 quotation.

Notice the share: terminal value contributed ~58% of the total. Now shift the assumptions:

- Terminal growth from 3% to 4%: terminal-value contribution rises to ~$2.6T. Total EV: $4.0T. The stock looks 18% undervalued.
- Terminal growth from 3% to 2%: terminal-value contribution falls to ~$1.6T. Total EV: $3.0T. The stock looks 12% overvalued.
- Discount rate from 9% to 10%: terminal value falls to ~$1.5T. Total EV: $2.9T. Stock looks 15% overvalued.
- Discount rate from 9% to 8%: terminal value rises to ~$2.8T. Total EV: $4.2T. Stock looks 24% undervalued.

The base case said "fairly valued." Move two assumptions by 1% each, in directions a perfectly reasonable analyst might defend, and you get answers ranging from "20% overvalued" to "30% undervalued."

This isn't a problem with the math — it's the nature of compounding. A 1% perpetual growth difference, compounded to infinity and discounted back, moves enormous present-value amounts. Which is why a DCF is a sensitivity-table tool, not a point-estimate tool.

On Bursa Malaysia: try the same exercise for TENAGA, whose regulated returns make the terminal-growth assumption much narrower (utilities terminal-grow at ~2–3% historically). The terminal-value share of total EV is similar to MSFT's, but the assumption range is much tighter — DCFs are more honest on regulated utilities than on growth companies for exactly this reason.

The falsifiable claim: any DCF whose terminal value is more than 70% of total enterprise value is a terminal-value model dressed up as a DCF. Either accept that the central output is "what you assume about year-11-and-beyond" and act accordingly, or use a different framework.

## The trap

The trap is reporting a DCF output as a single number — "fair value is $480" — when the model is structurally a range. A beginner builds a 10-year explicit forecast in a spreadsheet, plugs in a terminal value, and treats the output as truth. The correct mental model: every DCF should be run with at least three terminal-growth scenarios (e.g., 2%, 3%, 4%) and at least three discount-rate scenarios (e.g., 8%, 9%, 10%). The output is a 9-cell grid of values, and the honest "fair value" is the *range across that grid*, not the center. The other version of the trap: assuming a competitive business will terminal-grow at 4–5% in perpetuity. Few businesses outgrow nominal GDP forever — 2–3% is the defensible band for most mature companies, and using anything higher is implicitly assuming a moat that lasts to infinity.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, pick a stock you're considering and ask: "Run a 3×3 DCF sensitivity grid — terminal growth at 2%, 3%, 4% by discount rate at 8%, 9%, 10%. Show me the share of total enterprise value coming from terminal value in each cell." In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write the value range, the terminal-share range, and one sentence on which assumption you'd most defend. Make decisions against the range, not the center.

## Takeaway

A DCF output is a range, not a number — and if terminal value is more than 70% of enterprise value, you're not running a DCF, you're betting on infinity.
