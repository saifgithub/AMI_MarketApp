# In-sample vs out-of-sample — the honest split

The backtesting foundation (057) introduced out-of-sample testing as a defence against overfitting. This lesson is about the data split itself — the single most consequential design choice you make before running a backtest. Get the split right and your test is informative; get it wrong and your "out-of-sample" result is contaminated by hindsight, peeking, or regime overlap. The standard splits are 70/30, 60/20/20 (with a validation set), and walk-forward (covered in 229). After this lesson you can choose a split appropriate to your strategy's holding period and data length, hold yourself to the firewall, and read the efficiency ratio honestly.

## How this plays out in real markets

You have 20 years of SPY daily data (2005-2024) and a swing-trading rule with a 5-day average hold. A reasonable split: in-sample 2005-2018 (14 years, ~3,500 trades possible), out-of-sample 2019-2024 (6 years, ~1,500 trades). You optimise all parameters on the in-sample. The in-sample period saw the 2008 crisis (so the rule was stress-tested), but the OOS includes the COVID crash and 2022 bear — regimes the rule never saw during parameter selection.

In-sample CAGR: 12.3%, max drawdown 18%, win rate 56%. OOS CAGR: 8.7%, max drawdown 22%, win rate 53%. Efficiency ratio 0.71. The OOS includes one regime (COVID crash) the in-sample didn't see well, and the rule's drawdown widened — but the edge survived. This is what an honest result looks like: meaningfully degraded but still positive, with the failure modes appearing in the regimes you'd expect.

Contrast with a different split: 2005-2024 in-sample, 2020-2024 OOS (overlap). The OOS data was already inside the in-sample, so the OOS result is meaningless. Or a split that puts only the bull-market years in-sample and the bear years out-of-sample — that's testing a different question (does the rule survive a regime it never trained on) and you can't compare in-sample numbers honestly. The correct split is contiguous time, no overlap, OOS at the end, and includes at least one bear regime if possible.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is "peeking" — running the rule on the OOS, seeing it disappoint, going back to the in-sample to tune, then re-running the OOS. The moment you tune after seeing OOS results, the OOS becomes in-sample and you have no honest test left. The correct mental model: the OOS is a tripwire you walk through ONCE. If it fails, the rule is dead and you start over with a new hypothesis on different data. The second trap: making the OOS window too short — six months of OOS on a rule that trades monthly is six data points, statistically nothing. The third trap: using random shuffled splits on time-series data. Random splits leak future information into the past because nearby days are correlated; always use contiguous time splits.

## Takeaway

The OOS is a tripwire you walk through once. Tune before, deploy after — and if you tune after, you've contaminated the only honest test you had.
