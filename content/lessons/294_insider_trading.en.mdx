# Insider trading: material non-public information

Insider trading has a precise two-prong test, and you can run it yourself: is the information **material** — would a reasonable investor consider it important to the price, price-sensitive information — and is it **non-public** — not yet released through proper disclosure, and/or obtainable only by position in a company (CEO, directors, employees) or by access to confidential information (lawyers, bankers, auditors)? Both prongs must be true. Material and public: trade freely. Non-public but immaterial: no case. Material *and* non-public — MNPI — and if you got it through one of those channels, or from someone who did, trading is off. This lesson makes the test automatic.

## How this plays out in real markets

December 27, 2001: Martha Stewart sold 3,928 shares of ImClone Systems after her broker's assistant tipped her that ImClone's CEO was dumping his own stock. The next day the FDA publicly rejected ImClone's cancer drug application and the stock fell. The sale avoided a loss of about $45,673 — pocket change against her net worth. She served five months in prison. (Legal nuance worth knowing: her conviction was for obstructing the investigation — lying about the trade — which is its own lesson about cover-ups.)

Scale it up: Raj Rajaratnam ran the Galleon Group hedge fund on a network of corporate tippers feeding him earnings and deal information before release. Wiretaps caught it. In 2011 he was sentenced to 11 years and later hit with a record $92.8 million SEC civil penalty. Two ends of the spectrum — a $45,673 avoided loss and a systematic tip network — same two-prong test, same offence.

## The trap

The beginner's rationalisation: "I didn't steal any documents — I just overheard it," or "my trade is far too small for anyone to notice." The size defence is fiction — Stewart avoided $45,673 and served five months. The overheard defence is subtler, because liability runs through the **breach of a duty**, not through the information alone: a genuine stranger who owes nobody a duty can fall outside it. Assume that is not you. A tippee inherits the tipper's breach the moment they know, or should know, that the information came out of a confidence — and a friend at the chip company talking over dinner is precisely that. The correct mental model is the **mosaic**: assembling *public* pieces — filings, channel checks on public data, industry knowledge — into a non-obvious conclusion is legitimate analysis. The moment one piece of the mosaic is material and non-public, the whole trade is contaminated.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the News Analyst, paste a rumour-style headline about a ticker on your watchlist ("sources say X is exploring a sale") and ask how it weighs sourced-but-unconfirmed stories against filed disclosures. Notice the discipline: AMI's simulated analysis leans on public, verifiable information — the same line the two-prong test draws for you.

## Takeaway

Run the two prongs before the trade: material? non-public? If both, ask the third question — did it reach you from someone who owed a duty of confidence, directly or down a chain? If so the trade is off, full stop, however small you'd size it. In practice, if you are holding MNPI and cannot name an innocent path it took to reach you, treat the trade as off. Note this is US law: other jurisdictions draw the line differently, and several run a possession-based rule closer to “if you hold it, do not trade”.
