# Why most traders fail

The uncomfortable truth: the published research on retail day-trading is consistent and brutal. Multiple peer-reviewed studies — Brazilian regulator-led work on equity day-traders, Taiwanese exchange data over multi-year windows, and US discount-broker datasets — converge on the same range: roughly **70% to 90% of retail short-term traders lose money over time**, and the share that are *consistently* profitable after costs over multi-year windows is in the low single digits. This is not a marketing scare line; it is the base rate. Knowing the patterns behind that base rate is the first real edge — because the patterns are knowable, and most of them are structural rather than mysterious.

## How this plays out in real markets

The failure patterns repeat with eerie consistency across markets and decades. Six dominate:

1. **Oversized positions.** A trader with $25,000 puts $10,000 (40% of capital) on a single TSLA swing. TSLA gaps down 18% on an earnings miss. The trader is down $1,800 — 7.2% of total capital — on one trade. Three more like it and the account is functionally finished.

2. **No predefined exit.** Entry feels exciting; exit is an afterthought. The trader buys NVDA at $115 with no stop and no target. Price runs to $130, then back to $108. They didn't sell at $130 ("it's going higher") and won't sell at $108 ("it'll come back"). Eventually they capitulate at $95.

3. **Revenge trading.** After a 5% loss, the trader doubles size on the next setup to "make it back." The next setup is lower quality precisely because they are now selecting for size, not for edge.

4. **Mistaking a bull market for skill.** 2020–2021 produced thousands of "10× traders." The market rose, almost any long worked, and traders attributed broad beta to personal alpha. 2022 corrected the attribution painfully.

5. **Friction blindness.** A trader makes 100 trades a year, claims a 60% win rate, and is somehow flat. The commissions, spreads, and short-term tax drag (the math from <Lesson id="010_compounding_vs_active_trading"/>) ate the gross gains.

6. **Identity-switching mid-position.** Enter as a trader, become an investor when down 15%, become a trader again when back to break-even, sell at break-even, miss the run, re-enter at the top. Each switch is rational in isolation; the sequence is fatal.

None of these are mysteries. Each is a structural pattern that can be designed against.

## The trap

The trap is reading the failure rate and concluding either "I won't be in that 80%, I'm different" (the overconfidence pattern that produces the 80%) **or** "trading is hopeless, I should give up" (which throws away the parts of the discipline — sizing, exits, journaling — that work even for long-term investors). Both responses miss the point. The patterns above are the curriculum. The 10–30% who *do* survive long-term mostly do it by mechanically refusing to commit the six errors, not by being smarter at picking direction. The edge is in the process, not the prediction.
