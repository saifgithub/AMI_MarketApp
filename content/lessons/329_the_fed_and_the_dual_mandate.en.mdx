# The Fed & the dual mandate

The US Federal Reserve operates under a statutory "dual mandate": maximum employment and stable prices — two goals written into the Federal Reserve Act, not one goal the Fed picked for itself. Most of the time these two goals pull in the same direction (a healthy, growing economy tends to deliver both). The interesting, market-moving moments are when they conflict — when fighting inflation risks jobs, or protecting jobs risks letting inflation run — because that's when the Fed has to reveal which side of its mandate it's actually prioritizing, and markets watch every word of that signal.

## Example

The 1977 amendment to the Federal Reserve Act formally wrote "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates" into the Fed's statutory mission — the dual mandate you'll hear referenced every time the Fed explains a decision. In 2012, the Fed went further and adopted an explicit numerical target: **2% annual inflation** (as measured by PCE, the gauge from the inflation lesson two modules back) as its longer-run goal.

2022 is the textbook case of the mandate in conflict. CPI inflation had peaked at 9.1% in June 2022 — more than four times the Fed's target — while unemployment sat near 3.5%, close to the lowest levels in over 50 years. Price stability said "hike aggressively, right now." Full employment said "the labor market looks fine, no urgency from that side." The Fed chose price stability as the dominant priority through 2022-2023, raising rates at the fastest pace in over 40 years, explicitly reasoning that a labor market this strong gave it room to fight inflation hard without an immediate employment cost — betting it could bring inflation down without breaking the jobs side of its own mandate.

## The trap

The trap is assuming the Fed always weighs its two goals equally, or assuming it will always choose the same priority it chose last time. The dual mandate is not a formula the Fed runs through a calculator — it's a judgment call the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) makes meeting by meeting, and which goal dominates depends on which one looks more at risk at that moment. In 2022-2023, inflation was clearly the dominant threat and the Fed acted accordingly. In a different environment — say, unemployment spiking while inflation is tame — the same Fed, under the same statutory mandate, would lean hard the other way. Read every Fed statement for *which half of the mandate it's currently worried about*, not for a fixed rule about which one always wins.

## The steelman

The strongest case against taking the Fed's stated reasoning at face value: central banks are political institutions in practice even when legally independent, and critics across the spectrum have argued the Fed moves too slowly, too fast, or for reasons other than the ones stated in its press releases — reading its public rationale as the full story can be naive. That's a real critique with real historical examples on both sides. The falsifier isn't trusting the stated reasoning blindly — it's comparing the Fed's *stated* priority in its FOMC statement language to what it actually *does* with the policy rate in the following months. When the two align consistently (as they did through the 2022-2023 hiking cycle — hawkish language followed by hikes, meeting after meeting), the stated reasoning has earned some credibility as a real predictor, whatever the deeper political dynamics behind it.

## Takeaway

The Fed's dual mandate isn't a fixed formula — it's a discretionary bet on which risk is bigger right now, and the Fed will tell you which one in its own words if you read the statement, not just the rate decision.

*Where this comes from: the statutory dual mandate — Federal Reserve Act, as amended 1977; the 2% inflation target — Federal Reserve, adopted 2012.*
