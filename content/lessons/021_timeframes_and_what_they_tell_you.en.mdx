# Timeframes and what they tell you

A chart is not a chart — it's a series of choices about how much time gets compressed into one candle. A 5-minute chart and a weekly chart of the same stock can disagree completely about what the trend "is", and both can be correct for the question they're answering. By the end of this lesson you should know what each common timeframe tells you, why a trade thesis needs at least two of them to agree, and why borrowing decisions from one timeframe to act on another is the fastest way to take random losses.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at MSFT through April 2026. On the weekly chart, MSFT had been carving higher weekly highs since the November 2025 low near $362 — a clean uptrend, each weekly candle closing above the prior week's open. Zoom to the daily: same period showed two distinct pullbacks — one from $428 to $401 in late January, another from $445 to $419 in early March — pullbacks invisible on the weekly. Zoom further to the hourly: each daily pullback contained five or six sessions of red candles and small rallies that looked, in the moment, like the end of the uptrend.

A swing trader using the weekly stayed long the entire run from $362 to $445 — about 23%. A daily-chart trader trimmed at each pullback and re-entered, capturing maybe 14%. An hourly trader scalped 1–2% moves and probably got chopped near flat after slippage and emotion. None of them was wrong about what their chart showed. They were answering different questions: "Is the long-term trend intact?" (weekly: yes), "Is there a tradable pullback?" (daily: yes), "Is there an entry trigger right now?" (hourly: maybe).

The thesis falsifies if MSFT closes a full weekly candle below the most recent weekly swing low — that's when the weekly trend itself is broken, and the daily trader's pullback thesis loses its anchor.

## The trap

The trap is making a decision on one timeframe using evidence from a different one. A beginner sees a "perfect" hourly setup and sizes it like a swing trade, holding through normal daily noise that completely invalidates the hourly trigger. Or they read the daily chart, decide the trend is up, then enter on a 5-minute spike and get stopped out by intraday volatility their daily thesis never accounted for. The correct mental model: pick the timeframe that matches your hold period. Then use one higher timeframe for context (is the bigger trend on your side?) and one lower timeframe for entry timing (am I entering in a sensible spot today?). Three timeframes max. Same trade, three angles.

## Takeaway

Pick the timeframe that matches your hold period, borrow context from one above and entry timing from one below — and never let a lower timeframe override the thesis a higher one set.
