# Home-country bias

Almost every investor holds far more of their own country's market than its share of the world would justify, and almost none of them decided to. After this lesson you will be able to measure your own home weight, quantify what it costs in volatility, and separate the reasons for it that survive scrutiny from the ones that are just familiarity wearing an argument's clothes.

## Example

The tendency is old and documented. French and Poterba (1991), working with data from the late 1980s, measured domestic shares of investors' equity holdings at **over 98% in Japan, 94% in the United States and 82% in Britain** — at a time when none of those markets was anywhere near that share of world equity value. Their point was not that investors were wrong about any particular country; it was that portfolios this concentrated imply the holders expected their own market to beat every other by several percentage points a year, which is a belief almost none of them would have stated out loud if asked.

What does the concentration cost? Take two openly hypothetical sleeves — a domestic one at **22% annualised volatility**, a foreign one at **18%**, correlated at **0.60** — and vary only the split:

| domestic / foreign | portfolio volatility |
|---|---|
| 100 / 0 | **22.00%** |
| 85 / 15 | **20.43%** |
| 70 / 30 | **19.13%** |
| 50 / 50 | **17.91%** |

The first fifteen points of foreign exposure buy **1.57 percentage points** of volatility — a large payment for a small change, and the reason the early moves away from a 100% home book are the ones worth making. Note also that this holds at a correlation of 0.60, which is high: these are not independent markets, and the benefit is real anyway. That is <Lesson id="371_diversification_is_correlation_not_count"/> restated at country level.

The concentration bites hardest where the home market is itself narrow. An investor holding only Bursa Malaysia holds a market whose index is heavily weighted toward banks, utilities and plantations; an investor holding only a GCC market holds one where a large share of listed earnings tracks the oil complex, and where the currency peg links monetary conditions to a foreign central bank's decisions. In both cases "100% domestic" is also a concentrated sector and macro bet that the holder never wrote down as one — the drift problem from <Lesson id="373_strategic_allocation_vs_tactical_drift"/>, at the level of a whole market.

## The trap

The trap is that home bias comes with a ready-made justification: *I understand these companies.* And it is often true — you use their products, you read the local press, you know which regulator matters. The problem is that familiarity is an argument about your **information**, and the position is an argument about your **risk**, and the two do not connect. Knowing a company well does not decorrelate it from the other companies in the same economy, under the same central bank, in the same currency.

The correct mental model: your home weight is a position with a size, and like any position it needs a reason that is not "this is where I live". Some reasons survive — you spend in this currency, so matching some assets to it genuinely reduces the risk that matters to you; local tax treatment or access costs may be real. Those are arguments for a home weight *above* the country's world share, and they are worth stating. What they are not is an argument for 100%, and the gap between "some tilt is justified" and "everything I own is here" is where the unexamined part lives.

## The steelman

The strongest case for a large home weight: your liabilities are domestic. You will spend in your home currency, so a foreign sleeve introduces exchange-rate risk against the only thing your portfolio is ultimately for, and the volatility table above measures the wrong quantity — it prices asset volatility in a common currency rather than in the currency you eat in. That is a genuinely strong argument and it is why the sensible target is a tilt rather than the world's market weights. Two things bound it, though. First, it is an argument about *currency*, and currency exposure can be managed separately from where the companies are listed. Second, it does not scale to the whole book: a currency-matching argument justifies a substantial home allocation, not the elimination of every foreign holding. The falsifier is concrete — write down the home weight your currency argument justifies *before* you look at your account. If it lands near what you already hold, the tilt is deliberate and the argument is doing real work. If your actual weight is far above it, the currency case is a rationalisation you found after the fact.

## Try it

Work out your own home weight: what fraction of your simulated holdings is listed in the country you live in? Then write, in one sentence, the reason that weight is what it is — and check whether the reason mentions risk or only familiarity. If you would like to pressure-test it, ask the Portfolio Manager in a 1-on-1 what would have to be true for a 100% domestic book to be the right shape, and evaluate the argument it gives you rather than accepting it. AMI's output is a training artifact for you to judge, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

You are allowed a home tilt, and you should be able to say in one sentence what it is for — if the sentence is only "this is where I live", you have a concentration rather than a decision.

*Where this comes from: French and Poterba's measurement of domestic concentration in equity portfolios — Investor Diversification and International Equity Markets (1991).*
