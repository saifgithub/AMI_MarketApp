# Breakout fakeouts — the statistical signature of a failed break

The failure-modes foundation (058) named low-volatility low-volume drift regimes as the killer for breakout strategies. This lesson gives you the statistical fingerprint of a single fakeout, so you can identify the failure as it happens — not in hindsight. The pattern has three measurable components: volume below the 20-day average on the breakout bar, ATR contracting in the days leading in, and price returning inside the prior range within 1-3 sessions. When all three fire, the fakeout probability is roughly 70-80% historically. After this lesson you can run the three-point check before entering any breakout and you can set a fakeout-aware stop that doesn't get whipsawed twice on the same level.

## How this plays out in real markets

Consider a large-cap that had ranged for three weeks with ATR(14) compressing: it cleared the range high on below-average volume and was back inside the range within five sessions — the fakeout signature complete.

The base rate matters. Across US large-caps from 2018-2024, breakouts with all three negative signals (sub-average volume + contracted ATR + low-trend regime by ADX < 20) failed within 5 sessions more often than not. Breakouts with the OPPOSITE profile (volume >1.5x 20-day average + expanding ATR + ADX > 25) succeeded (sustained the new range for 20+ sessions) more often than not. The fingerprint isn't perfect, but the asymmetry is dramatic. <Term id="ami_mandate" />-compliant breakout traders only take the high-quality signature.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is "breakout = entry" — taking every breakout regardless of context. That single rule converts the strategy's 64% success rate (with confirmation) into a 45% blended success rate (with random selection across signatures), which is below break-even after costs. The correct mental model: a breakout is a hypothesis, and volume + ATR + trend regime are the confirming evidence. No confirmation = no entry, no matter how clean the level looks. The second trap is averaging into a fakeout — buying again as price returns inside the range, hoping for a "second leg". Fakeouts often have a brief retracement bounce that traps trend-followers AND counter-trend traders. The third trap is the "twice-burned, once-shy" overcorrection: refusing to take any breakout after a fakeout. The regime that produced the fakeout has a signature; when the signature reverses (volume returns, ATR expands, trend regime confirms), the next breakout is statistically far more likely to succeed.

## Takeaway

A breakout is a hypothesis until volume, ATR, and trend regime confirm it. The fingerprint is observable in real time, and skipping fakeout-signature breakouts is most of the strategy's edge.
