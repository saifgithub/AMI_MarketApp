# Fiduciary duty: the client's interest first

A fiduciary must place the client's interest above their own — loyalty, prudence, and care, in that order of famous phrasing. It is the highest duty in finance, and the sharpest tool you have for evaluating anyone who touches your money: not "is this person smart?" but "whose interest does this recommendation serve?" This lesson gives you the test and shows, in dollars, what its absence costs.

## How this plays out in real markets

Two ways to own the S&P 500. An index ETF like SPY carries a 0.09% expense ratio. A commission-paid salesperson instead offers an actively managed US equity fund with a 5.5% front-end load and 1.5% annual expenses. Put $10,000 in each for 20 years and assume the same 7% gross market return. The loaded fund starts at $9,450 (the load comes off day one) and compounds at roughly 5.5% net: about **$27,573**. SPY compounds at roughly 6.91% net: about **$38,051**. Gap: **$10,478** — the index route ends 38% richer on identical market performance.

Nothing about recommending the loaded fund is necessarily illegal under a plain sales relationship. But a **fiduciary** must justify that recommendation by the client's interest — and against a $10,478 expected shortfall, "it pays my commission" doesn't survive contact with the duty. That's the entire architecture of the standard: it doesn't make salespeople evil, it makes the conflict *legally relevant*. Historically, US brokers owed only "suitability" while registered investment advisers owed the full fiduciary duty — one word of difference, $10,478 of consequence.

## The trap

Beginners equate duty with results: "my adviser made me money, so they did right by me." It feels fair — outcomes are what you wanted. But fiduciary duty is a **process and loyalty standard, not an outcome standard**. An adviser who churns your account through unnecessary trades that happen to profit still breached the duty — the churning served their commissions, not you. An adviser who prudently diversified you before a crash that hit everything did not breach it, despite the loss. Judge the duty by what the decision served at the time it was made, not by what the market did afterwards.

## Takeaway

Before "will this make money," ask "whose interest does this serve?" — fiduciary duty is that question with legal force, and it's judged on loyalty and process, never on outcome.

*Where this comes from: CFA Institute Standard III(A) — Loyalty, Prudence, and Care (2014), the profession's statement of the client-first duty.*
