# Sum-of-the-parts: when one company is really three

Some companies don't have one business — they have three or four, each with different growth rates, margin profiles, and natural multiples. Applying a single P/E or EV/EBITDA to the whole company averages all of that into a number that under-prices the best segment, over-prices the worst, and misleads on both. Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation values each segment separately at the multiple appropriate for *that* segment, then adds them up. By the end of this lesson you should know when SOTP is the right framework, how to assemble one cleanly, and the conglomerate-discount pattern that often emerges from the exercise.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take AMZN as of mid-2026. The retail business (online stores + third-party seller services + Prime memberships + physical stores) generates the bulk of revenue but at retail-margin economics. AWS (cloud) generates a smaller revenue share but at software-margin economics. Advertising generates a third revenue stream at very high incremental margins.

A clean SOTP looks roughly like this:

- AWS: ~$110B annual revenue, ~35% operating margin, deserving a software-business multiple (say ~25x operating income).
- Advertising: ~$60B annual revenue, ~50%+ incremental operating margin, deserving an ad-business multiple comparable to META or GOOGL (~20x operating income).
- Retail: ~$450B annual revenue, ~5% operating margin, deserving a retail-business multiple (~12x operating income).

Add up the three valuations and you get a SOTP estimate. Applying AMZN's blended P/E of ~45x to the whole thing under-prices AWS and over-prices retail simultaneously. The SOTP shows that AWS alone, valued on standalone software economics, accounts for 50%+ of AMZN's market cap by some analyst frameworks.

Compare to GOOGL. Search + YouTube + ad network is one business with similar margin profiles; Cloud is a separate one; the "Other Bets" (Waymo, etc.) are a third with venture-economics. SOTP is the only way to value GOOGL coherently — applying a single multiple averages high-margin ads with low-margin or money-losing bets, producing a meaningless number.

Now look at where SOTP reveals the conglomerate discount. Many diversified industrials (HON, MMM at various times) trade at a 10–20% discount to the sum of their segment valuations. The market is saying: "I'd rather own the segments separately than the conglomerate." When the discount reaches 25%+, the company often faces activist pressure for a spin-off — DIS, GE, IBM have all gone through versions of this in the last decade.

On Bursa Malaysia: GENTING is a textbook SOTP candidate — gaming, plantation, power, and property are four distinct businesses with different economics. A single P/E on the whole group obscures more than it reveals.

The falsifiable claim: a SOTP is necessary when any single segment contributes less than 50% of total operating income AND the segments have materially different margin profiles (operating margin spread of 15+ percentage points between highest and lowest segments). If the SOTP exceeds the current market cap by more than 20%, a conglomerate discount is in play and a corporate-action catalyst (spin-off, divestiture, activist pressure) may be ahead.

## The trap

The trap is double-counting corporate-level costs and debt. A beginner builds a SOTP, values each segment generously, and forgets to subtract: (a) corporate overhead (the holding company's central costs that don't belong to any segment), (b) net debt at the parent level, and (c) the tax friction of any actual segment separation (spin-offs often have tax costs). The correct mental model: SOTP starts with segment operating income at standalone multiples, subtracts allocated corporate overhead at a fair multiple, subtracts net debt, and ends with equity value. Skipping any of those three adjustments inflates the answer. The other trap is over-confidence in segment multiples — AWS doesn't trade publicly, so any standalone multiple applied to it is an analyst guess. Use a range, not a number, for every segment exactly as in DCF.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, pick a diversified company you follow and ask: "Build a SOTP. Segment-by-segment, what's the operating income, what multiple would you apply, and what's the equity value after subtracting net debt and capitalised corporate overhead?" In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write the SOTP, the current market cap, and the implied discount or premium. A discount above 25% is a watch item — write down what catalyst (spin-off, activist, management change) might unlock it.

## Takeaway

When a company is really three businesses, one multiple averages them all into a wrong number — SOTP each segment, subtract overhead and net debt honestly, and the answer is usually narrower than the headline suggests.
