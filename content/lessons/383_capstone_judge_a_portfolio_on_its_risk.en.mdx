# Capstone: judge a portfolio on its risk, not its returns

You now hold the whole M19 toolkit: the frontier and the portfolios that sit below it, beta and the R² that says whether beta means anything, factors as exposures before premiums, risk shares against dollar weights, what a hedge can and cannot reach, and the six questions for a claim someone hands you. This capstone runs one book through all of it in a single pass and produces a verdict that never mentions its return. Nothing new is introduced; the skill is doing the whole sequence before forming an opinion.

## Example

An openly hypothetical simulated book of **$200,000**, up 19% over the past year. Two sleeves: **growth equities at 70%** ($140,000, 24% volatility) and **defensive at 30%** ($60,000, 7% volatility), correlated at **0.15**. The growth sleeve is six names; the holder describes them as "different companies in different industries." Book beta against the market: **1.35**. Average R² of the growth holdings against the market: **0.30**. No real company appears anywhere; the arithmetic is the lesson.

**Volatility and risk shares.** Portfolio volatility computes to **17.24%**. The risk shares:

| sleeve | dollar weight | share of portfolio risk |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | 70% | **96.7%** |
| Defensive | 30% | **3.3%** |

The defensive sleeve holds 30 cents of every dollar and answers for a thirtieth of the risk. As a risk decision it is almost not there. Moving to 55/45 gives 14.02% volatility and shares of 91.8/8.2; moving to 40/60 gives 11.04% and 80.6/19.4. Even at 40/60 the growth sleeve dominates — which is what a 24%-against-7% volatility gap does, and is worth knowing before anyone calls a 40/60 book conservative.

**Market exposure.** Beta 1.35 on $200,000 is **$270,000** of index-equivalent exposure; a 10% market decline implies about **−$27,000**.

**What a hedge could reach.** At an average R² of 0.30, a perfect market hedge leaves σ√(1 − 0.30) = 83.7% of the volatility standing: **17.24% would become 14.42%**. Seventy percent of this book's risk is not market risk and no index hedge touches it.

**Where that other risk lives.** Six names described as "different industries" with an average R² of 0.30 means most of their movement is not the market's — which is not the same as it being independent of each other. If they share a characteristic, they share a driver, and <Lesson id="379_factors_value_size_momentum_quality_low_vol"/> is the reason ticker-level diversity says nothing about it. The test is one line of arithmetic: compute their pairwise correlations. Until that is done, "different industries" is a description of the labels.

**The verdict.** This book has one meaningful sleeve, a large market exposure, a majority of risk that no market hedge reaches, and an unmeasured concentration inside its growth names. It is up 19%, and none of the four findings depends on that in any way — nor would any of them have changed had it been down 19%.

## The trap

The trap is the 19%. It arrives first, it is the number the holder cares about, and it silently reframes every subsequent finding as an objection to be answered rather than a fact to be recorded. A book up 19% with 96.7% of its risk in one sleeve is described as "concentrated in what's working"; the identical book down 19% is described as "dangerously concentrated." The risk figures are the same in both cases because they were never a function of the outcome.

The correct mental model: the return is one draw from a distribution the risk figures describe. Judging the construction by the draw is <Lesson id="357_process_vs_outcome"/> at portfolio level, and it fails in the more dangerous direction here — a good year removes the pressure to look, so the audit that would have found the concentration is the one nobody runs. Do the risk pass first, write the findings down, and only then look at the return. If the order feels artificial, that is the discipline working.

## The steelman

The strongest case for this book: every finding above is a description, and none of them says the holder is wrong. A 96.7% risk share in growth is exactly what someone who wants growth exposure should hold; a 1.35 beta is what conviction looks like in a rising market; the untouchable 70% of risk is the specific risk you take deliberately when you pick companies rather than buy an index. On this reading the audit produces four accurate sentences and zero problems, and treating description as criticism is how risk analysis turns into an argument for owning nothing. That case is largely right, and it is why the verdict above lists findings rather than errors. But it has a precise limit, and it is the same one M18 found: a deliberate position has a number attached to it that was chosen in advance. The falsifier is a question, not a computation — ask the holder what risk share they intended the defensive sleeve to carry. If they say "a few percent, it is there for optionality rather than balance", the book is built and the audit merely confirms it. If they say "about thirty percent, that's why it's 30% of the money", then the sleeve was bought under a misunderstanding of what dollar weights buy, and the finding is real.

## Try it

Run the sequence on your own simulated book, in this order, and write each answer down before moving on. What is each sleeve's dollar weight? What is its share of the risk? What is the book's beta, and how much index-equivalent exposure does that imply? How much of the risk would a market hedge actually reach? Group your equity names by driver rather than by ticker — how many bets do you really hold? Only when all five are written down, look at your return. If any finding feels different after you look, that is the trap, and you have just caught it happening.

## Takeaway

Compute the risk before you look at the return, because the return cannot change any of the risk figures but it will change how you read them.

*Where this comes from: Markowitz's covariance view of portfolio risk (1952) and Sharpe's split of it into market-linked and specific components (1964).*
