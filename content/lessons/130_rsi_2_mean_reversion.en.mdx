# RSI(2) — short-lookback mean reversion

<Lesson id="027_rsi_momentum_oscillator"/> used the default RSI(14). Drop the lookback to 2 and the indicator becomes something different — a short-term mean-reversion oscillator that whipsaws between extremes within days. RSI(2) was popularised by Larry Connors for buying pullbacks in stocks that are above their 200-day moving average. The mechanics: in stocks confirmed to be in long-term up-trends, RSI(2) drops below 10 typically once every 6–12 weeks during a sharp 2–4 day pullback. Buying that dip and selling when RSI(2) reaches 70 produces an average holding period of three to five sessions. It is not a trend system; it is a snapback system. And it only works inside an established up-trend.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at GOOGL through 2025. GOOGL closed above its SMA200 every single trading day from March through December — a confirmed long-term up-trend. Within that year, daily RSI(2) dropped below 10 nine separate times. Buying the next day's open on each signal and exiting when RSI(2) crossed back above 70 produced:

- 7 winning trades averaging +1.8% per trade, holding period 3–4 days
- 2 losing trades averaging -2.2% per trade

Total: roughly +9.2% across nine trades over 10 months on a single ticker, with an average holding period under a week. The 78% win rate and small holding period are the signature of a working mean-reversion system in trend.

Falsification is mechanical and severe — RSI(2) mean-reversion outside an SMA200 up-trend has historically returned roughly zero before fees. If price closes below SMA200, the system is offline until SMA200 turns back up and price re-crosses it. The same RSI(2) signal in a confirmed down-trend (price below declining SMA200) produces buys that get cut in half.

Contrast: TSLA in early 2025 traded below SMA200 from January through March. RSI(2) dropped below 10 four times in that window. Each "signal" produced losses averaging -3.4% as the down-trend continued. Same indicator, no trend filter — system breaks.

On Bursa Malaysia: PUBLIC BANK during 2024 (above SMA200 most of the year) had eight RSI(2) < 10 signals, six profitable averaging +1.3%; the system worked because the trend filter was on.

## The trap

The trap is using RSI(2) as a stand-alone signal without the SMA200 trend filter. RSI(2) < 10 looks like "obvious oversold" — and in a stock falling 30% from its high, "oversold" can keep getting more oversold for weeks. The signal works because it catches normal pullbacks in established up-trends; it fails when the underlying trend has rolled over and the pullback turns into a decline.

The correct mental model: RSI(2) is a precision tool for a specific regime — stocks confirmed in a long-term up-trend (price above rising SMA200). The signal extracts the snapback after a 2–4 day pullback within that uptrend. Outside the trend, it has no edge. Combine the SMA200 filter with the RSI(2) trigger and you have a system; drop the filter and you have a noise generator.

In the Market Analyst <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />, ask: "On my <Term id="ami_watchlist" />, which stocks are currently above a rising SMA200 and have a daily RSI(2) reading below 10? Walk me through the last three such signals on each — entry, exit, holding period." Then verify on the chart. This is a precision trade type; use it only when the trend filter is on.

## Takeaway

RSI(2) is a mean-reversion precision tool for stocks above a rising SMA200 — typical signal once every 6–12 weeks, 3–5 day hold, 70–80% win rate when the trend filter is on, zero edge when it isn't.
