# Customer concentration risk

Lesson 032 ended on segment concentration — when one product line carries a company. This lesson narrows further: when one *customer* carries it. A single buyer above 10% of total revenue creates a fragility that doesn't show up in the headline growth rate. Lose them, and the revenue line breaks in a single quarter — not because the business got worse, but because the relationship changed. By the end of this lesson you should know how to find concentration in disclosures, the threshold at which it materially changes the risk profile, and the specific scenario where a "great quarter" is actually a warning.

## How this plays out in real markets

US 10-K filings require disclosure of any customer above 10% of total revenue. Read the "Customers" section in the annual report or the Risk Factors. Apple is the textbook example seen from the *other* side — many of Apple's suppliers depend on it heavily. In fiscal 2024, AAPL was disclosed as a >10% customer for multiple chip and component vendors. When a vendor like Skyworks Solutions reports roughly 65–70% of revenue from "a single large customer" (consistently understood to be Apple), the entire business is one renegotiation away from a revenue cliff. The 2024 transition of certain Apple radio components in-house compressed several suppliers' revenue by 20–40% in a single fiscal year.

For a clearer concentration example, look at semiconductor capital-equipment names. ASML, KLA, and AMAT all disclose customer concentration — TSM and Samsung typically together account for 30–50% of revenue at each. The concentration is structural to the industry: there are only three leading-edge logic foundries on the planet.

On Bursa Malaysia: many electronics manufacturing services (EMS) companies on Bursa derive 40%+ of revenue from one or two anchor customers. When the anchor shifts production or insourcing strategy, the EMS revenue line can drop 25% in a quarter without anything having "gone wrong" at the EMS level.

The falsifiable trigger: when a single customer above 20% of revenue ends or materially renegotiates a contract, expect a one-quarter revenue contraction of 15–35% in the affected segment within two reporting periods. That is the historic base rate, not a guess.

## The trap

The trap is rewarding companies for landing a "marquee customer" without pricing the concentration that follows. A beginner sees "won contract with [big tech name], expected to add $500M revenue" and treats it as an unambiguous win. It is, in the near term. But if that contract pushes the customer to 25% of revenue, the same announcement has just doubled the company's single-point-of-failure risk. The correct mental model: customer concentration is a leverage on the business — it amplifies wins *and* losses. Above 10%, take note. Above 20%, the company is partly a derivative of that customer. Above 40%, you are effectively underwriting the customer's continued willingness to keep buying.

## Takeaway

A customer above 20% of revenue is a structural lever — they amplify your gains and they will amplify your losses. Find the number before you size the position.
