# AI hallucinations — and how AMI's safety floor mitigates them

Any system built on top of large language models can produce a hallucination: a plausible-sounding output that is wrong in a verifiable way. <Term id="ami" /> is no exception — an agent might cite a P/E ratio that's two quarters stale or describe a chart pattern that isn't actually there. Because AMI is a <Term id="training_simulator" /> (not a licensed advisor), the design question is not "how do we make the agents perfect" but "how do we structure the practice environment so a wrong agent can't quietly slip a <Term id="ami_mandate" /> violation past the user". The defence is not "prompt the model harder". The defence is structural: the Chief Investment Officer runs a deterministic compliance check on every simulated <Term id="ami_verdict" />, and the rules of that check live outside the prompt layer. By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why the <Term id="ami_pm_safety_floor" /> is not bypassable, even by a confidently wrong agent.

## How this plays out in real markets

Suppose you convene the Room on TENAGA (Bursa Malaysia). The Bull Researcher gets carried away and writes:

> *Bull Researcher:* TENAGA's dividend yield is 8.5%, payout ratio 60%, and the chart shows a clean breakout above RM14.20 — strong BUY at 8% size.

Two things are potentially hallucinated: the yield number, and the breakout claim. The Research Manager flags the yield against the Fundamentals Analyst's pulled data (actual: ~3.9%) and rebases the case. The Execution Desk proposes 6% size anyway. The Verdict reaches the PM.

Your Mandate says `risk_score: 3 (per-name cap 3%), max_drawdown_pct: 20, region: bursa_malaysia`. The PM's safety-floor message comes back as plain text in the Verdict footer:

> *Chief Investment Officer (compliance check):* Proposed size 6% > risk-tier cap 3% (risk_score 3). Trade blocked. Adjusted recommendation: size 3% or reject. Compliance: PASS only if size ≤ 3%.

The block is not a prompt nudging the Execution Desk to be careful. It's a deterministic function: `if proposed_size_pct > risk_tier_cap(mandate.risk_score): block`. Even if every agent insisted at 6%, the function would still block. The mandate clauses themselves — `risk_score`, `max_drawdown_pct`, `halal`, `no_fossil_fuels`, `long_only` — are stored as your data, not the model's parameters, and the compliance function reads that data directly. You can verify this by editing your Mandate to `risk_score: 2` (cap 1.5%) and re-running the Verdict: the PM will block anything > 1.5%, every time.

## The trap

The trap is believing hallucinations can be fixed by "better prompts" or "a smarter model". They can be reduced that way, never eliminated. The structural answer is to assume some agent output will be wrong some of the time, and put deterministic guards on the parts that matter most — position size, drawdown, exclusions. AMI's safety floor is that guard. **<Term id="ami_coach_your_agent" />** can change the Bull Researcher's tone, weighting of catalysts, or skepticism level — it cannot change the PM's compliance check, because the check isn't a prompt. The PM's mandate enforcement is uncoachable by design. Read that twice.

## Try it

Open **Settings → Mandate**, set `risk_score` to 2 (cap 1.5%). Convene the Room on any liquid US ticker. When the Verdict comes back, look for the PM's compliance-check line in the footer. Then change `risk_score` to 5 (cap 4.5%) and re-convene. The PM's allowed-size ceiling moves with your mandate — proof that the check reads your data, not the model's mood.

## Takeaway

Hallucinations are an LLM-layer reality; AMI's answer is a deterministic mandate check at the PM that cannot be coached, prompted, or charmed around. AMI is teaching you to evaluate analyst output under realistic failure modes — not handing you advice to follow.
