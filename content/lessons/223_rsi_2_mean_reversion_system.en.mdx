# RSI(2) mean-reversion system mechanics

The mean-reversion foundation (055) used RSI(2) with a regime filter. RSI(2) — the 2-period relative strength index — is the engine. The short look-back makes it whippy and extreme, which is the point: RSI(2) prints sub-5 readings several times a year on healthy uptrending instruments after sharp pullbacks, and a meaningful fraction of those readings precede 1-to-5 day snapbacks. The strategy is one of the most-studied short-term mechanical systems in published research. After this lesson you can specify a full RSI(2) rule with entry threshold, time-based exit, regime filter, and a position-size scheme that survives the inevitable cluster of losing trades.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at JPM through 2024. The system: enter long when RSI(2) closes below 5 AND price closes above the 200-day MA. Exit on the first daily close above the 5-day MA, OR at 5 sessions elapsed (time stop). JPM triggered six times in 2024. A representative trade: April 16, 2024 — JPM closed at $182.66 on a market wobble, RSI(2) at 3.8, price still above the 200-day MA (~$163). Entry on the close at $182.66 with a stop placed below the prior day's swing low at $179.50. Two sessions later, April 18, JPM closed at $186.79 — above the 5-day MA at $185.10 — and the position exited. Profit per share: $4.13 on $3.16 of risk = 1.3R in two days. Across 2024, JPM's six trades produced a hit rate of around 75% with average win ~1.2R and average loss ~1R — net positive expectancy from a strategy that holds losing trades only a few days at most.

Entry criteria: (1) RSI(2) closes below 5, (2) price closes above the 200-day MA (regime filter), (3) no confirmed negative fundamental catalyst (guidance cut, regulatory action, fraud allegation). Exit criteria: first daily close above the 5-day MA, OR 5 sessions elapsed (time stop) regardless of P&L. Stop on entry: previous day's low or current-day low, whichever is tighter. Individual-trade invalidation: stop hits, or time stop fires with the trade still red. The strategy FAILS in three regimes: (1) confirmed bear markets — RSI(2) prints sub-5 daily and price keeps dropping; (2) low-volatility creeping uptrends where RSI(2) rarely reaches the trigger and the few signals overlap with one-day reversals that complete before next-day entry; (3) high-correlation drawdowns where a portfolio of RSI(2) trades all draw down simultaneously.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is sizing RSI(2) trades by the same per-trade risk as longer-term setups, ignoring the strategy's cluster property. RSI(2) trades cluster — on a bad market week you can have 4-5 simultaneous entries that all go red together. Each individually is "only 1% risk", but together you've put 4-5% at risk in correlated trades. The correct mental model: an RSI(2) system is a PORTFOLIO of mean-reversion trades, not a series of independent ones. Size each entry at HALF of your normal per-trade risk, and cap total simultaneous RSI(2) exposure at 2-3% of account. The other trap: holding past the time stop. The whole edge is short-term mean reversion; if it hasn't reverted in 5 sessions, the statistical edge is gone and you're now running a bag-holding strategy.

## Takeaway

RSI(2) is a portfolio strategy disguised as a per-trade signal. Size for correlation, cap simultaneous exposure, and never hold past the time stop. The edge dies on day 6.
