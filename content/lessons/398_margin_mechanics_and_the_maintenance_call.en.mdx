# Margin — borrowing to buy, and the call that arrives at the worst time

Buying on margin means borrowing from your broker against the securities you hold. Two separate rules govern it: an **initial** requirement set by the Federal Reserve's Regulation T, historically 50% of the purchase, and a **maintenance** requirement set by the exchange and usually raised further by the broker, which is the equity percentage you must keep at all times. The initial rule decides how much you can borrow. The maintenance rule decides when you are forced to sell — and only the second one has teeth, because it is enforced continuously, against a price you do not control.

## How this plays out in real markets

You have **$10,000** cash and buy **$20,000** of stock on margin — the Reg T maximum. You have borrowed $10,000, and your equity is $10,000, which is 50% of the position.

Now the stock falls **20%**. The position is worth **$16,000**. The loan does not shrink, so:

    equity = 16,000 − 10,000 = $6,000
    equity % = 6,000 / 16,000 = 37.5%

Still above a typical 25–30% maintenance level. Continue: a **30%** fall puts the position at $14,000 and equity at $4,000, or **28.6%**. At a 30% house requirement you are now short of it, and a maintenance call arrives — deposit cash or the broker sells enough to restore the ratio, at their discretion and on their timetable.

Two things to notice. First, the loss is amplified: a 30% fall in the stock is a **60%** loss of your capital, because you lost $6,000 of a $10,000 stake. Second — the part people miss — the *percentage* deteriorates faster than the price, because the debt is fixed while the asset shrinks. Every dollar the position falls comes entirely out of your equity.

**AMI Trade does not offer leverage, and that is a permanent design decision rather than a missing feature.** This lesson is here so you understand a real brokerage's mechanics, not so you can look for the setting.

## The trap

The trap is planning around the initial requirement and forgetting the maintenance one. Traders size a position at "50% down is my limit" and are stopped out at 30%, because the maintenance test is a ratio, not a price, and the broker may raise the house requirement on a name at any time — often on the volatile names, often after they become volatile.

The second trap is treating a margin call as a request. It is not a negotiation and it does not wait for your thesis. If you do not meet it, the broker liquidates positions of their choosing at market, which typically means the most liquid holdings rather than the ones you would have picked.

## Steelman

Margin is not automatically reckless. Used at a fraction of the permitted amount it is simply a cheap source of capital, and there are respectable uses — bridging a settlement gap, or holding a position through a known short-term cash need rather than selling and re-buying with the costs and tax consequences that involves. What makes leverage dangerous is not its existence but its interaction with forced selling: it converts a temporary price move into a permanent realised loss at the worst available price. A trader who uses a quarter of their available margin and keeps cash aside for a call is running a different instrument from one who is fully extended, even though both are "on margin".

## Try it

You cannot use leverage in AMI Trade, so do this on paper instead. Take your largest current position and compute what your equity percentage would be after a 25% fall if half of it had been borrowed. Then look up your broker's actual maintenance requirement if you have a real account. Write both numbers in your Decision Journal — the point is to know the ratio, not the price, at which someone else starts making your decisions.

## Takeaway

The initial requirement decides how much you can borrow; the maintenance requirement decides when you are sold — and because the debt is fixed while the asset falls, the ratio breaks faster than the price does.

*Where this comes from: the initial margin requirement for purchasing securities on credit, set under the Federal Reserve's Regulation T, and the separate continuously-tested maintenance requirement applied by exchanges and brokers.*
