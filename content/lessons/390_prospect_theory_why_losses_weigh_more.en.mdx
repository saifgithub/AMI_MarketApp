# Prospect theory — why a loss weighs more than the same-sized gain

Classical finance assumes you value outcomes by where they leave your wealth. Prospect theory says you do something else entirely: you value **changes**, measured from a reference point — usually the price you paid — and losses below that point hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please. That asymmetry is not a flaw you can talk yourself out of. It is the shape of the machinery, and it explains two behaviours that otherwise look contradictory: refusing small risks when you are ahead, and taking large ones when you are behind.

## How this plays out in real markets

Two hypothetical positions, both bought at **$100**, both now at a different place.

**Position A is at $120.** Offered a coin flip that takes it to $140 or back to $100, most people decline. A sure $20 gain beats an even chance at $40.

**Position B is at $80.** Offered the mirror flip — $100 or $60 — most people *take* it. The certain $20 loss feels worse than a gamble that might erase it.

Same person, same coin, opposite choices. The difference is not risk appetite; it is which side of $100 they are standing on. Above the reference point the value curve is concave and you become risk-averse. Below it the curve is convex and you become risk-seeking, which is the technical description of *doubling down*.

Now notice the reference point itself is arbitrary. If Position B had been bought at $60 rather than $100, $80 would be a gain and the same flip would look unattractive. The identical asset, at the identical price, flips your risk preference depending on a historical number that no longer has anything to do with what it is worth.

## The trap

The trap is believing awareness fixes it. Knowing about loss aversion does not stop the loss hurting twice as much — the asymmetry is in how the outcome is valued, not in whether you have read about it.

What awareness *can* change is the reference point you present yourself with. A trader who thinks in "I am down $400 on this name" is standing below a reference point and is therefore, predictably, risk-seeking at the worst moment. The same trader thinking in "this position is 2% of my capital and my mandate caps single-name risk at 5%" has no reference point to be below. Do not try to feel differently about the loss. Change the number you are looking at.

## Steelman

Prospect theory is descriptive, not normative — it says what people do, not what is correct — and the honest objection is that risk-seeking below the reference point is sometimes *right*. A position down 20% on noise, with the thesis intact, may genuinely deserve more capital, and refusing to add because you have read about loss aversion is its own error. The distinguishing question is not how you feel but what changed: if the reason you owned it is still true and the price is lower, adding is analysis. If the only new fact is the loss, adding is the value function talking.

## Try it

Open your Decision Journal and find a position you added to while it was underwater. Write down what you were measuring at the moment you added: the entry price, or the position's share of your capital. Then open Settings → Mandate and read your single-name cap. That number is a reference point that does not move when the price does.

## Takeaway

You do not judge outcomes by where they leave you, you judge them by distance from what you paid — so when you catch yourself getting braver as a position falls, change the number you are looking at rather than trying to feel differently about it.

*Where this comes from: prospect theory's value function, defined over gains and losses from a reference point rather than over final wealth, and its asymmetric treatment of the two — Kahneman & Tversky's behavioral work.*
