# Network effects: when each user makes the next one stickier

<Lesson id="036_competitive_advantage_moat"/> listed network effects as one of the five moat categories. The textbook idea is simple — each additional user makes the service more valuable to every other user — but the application is messy. Most so-called "network effect" businesses are just two-sided marketplaces with weak network density, and they get steamrolled when a better-funded competitor shows up. A real network-effect moat shows up in unit economics: customer-acquisition cost falls or stays flat as the network grows, and retention rises with cohort age. By the end of this lesson you should be able to distinguish a genuine network effect from a popular two-sided product, and the specific cohort-economics pattern that confirms it.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at META in 2024. Facebook + Instagram + WhatsApp have roughly 4 billion combined monthly users. The network effect is that your friends and family are on the platform — switching to a competitor means leaving your social graph behind. The financial fingerprint shows up in operating margin (~38% in 2024) and ad pricing power: META can raise ad rates because advertisers cannot reach those users elsewhere at the same density. When TikTok launched and grabbed real engagement share starting around 2021, META's user base barely moved — they lost time-on-app, not the network itself. That's a network-effect moat under stress test: pressure, not collapse.

Now look at network effects that didn't hold. UBER in its early years was sold as a winner-take-all network business — more drivers means shorter wait times means more riders means more drivers. In practice, riders and drivers multi-home (every driver runs Uber and Lyft simultaneously), and the network effect compressed into "slight cost advantage from scale" rather than "moat that locks out competitors." UBER's path to profitability took longer than the network-effect thesis predicted because the moat was thinner than claimed.

Compare to V (Visa) and MA (Mastercard). The payments network — every merchant accepts the card because every consumer carries one, and every consumer carries one because every merchant accepts it — is a textbook two-sided network effect that has held for decades. V and MA gross margins routinely sit above 75%, operating margins above 60%, and new entrants (despite billions in funding) have failed to dislodge them.

On Bursa Malaysia: GRAB (listed in the US as GRAB but a Southeast-Asia-native business) faces the same multi-homing problem as UBER in its ride-hailing segment, but has built denser network effects in its super-app integration of food delivery + payments + financial services within Malaysia and Indonesia, where switching costs compound across the bundle.

The falsifiable claim: a real network-effect moat shows declining or stable customer-acquisition cost as the user base grows AND retention rates that improve with each successive cohort age. If CAC is rising faster than user growth, or retention rates flatten or decline as cohorts mature, what you have is a popular product without a network effect.

## The trap

The trap is calling every two-sided marketplace a network-effect business. A beginner sees a platform that connects supply and demand and assumes it must compound — but most marketplaces compete on the basis of operational scale, not network density. The correct mental model: a network effect exists when the value to user N+1 increases because user N is there, in a way that competitors with smaller networks cannot replicate by spending money. If a competitor can land in the market with a cheaper price or better UX and pull users away, the network was thin. A real test: ask whether a billion dollars of marketing spend by a competitor could neutralise the advantage. For META the answer is no (no amount of ads brings your friends to a new social platform). For most "platform" startups, the answer is yes.

## Try it

<Term id="ami_convene_the_room" /> on a platform business you use daily. Read the Bull Researcher's argument. Then in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, ask for: customer-acquisition cost over the last 5 years, and cohort retention rates by cohort age. In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write one sentence: "The network effect is [confirmed / not confirmed] because CAC is [trend] and cohort retention is [trend]." If you can't fill in the numbers, the network-effect thesis is rhetoric, not evidence.

## Takeaway

Every platform claims a network effect; the financials of only a few back it up — flat-or-falling CAC and rising cohort retention are the test, and money alone cannot beat a network that passes it.
