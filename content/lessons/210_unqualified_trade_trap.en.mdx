# The unqualified-trade trap

An unqualified trade is one that does not tick every box on your written entry checklist — taken anyway because you "see something" or "have a feel." Building on lesson 050, this lesson teaches you why unqualified trades have a measurably worse expected value than qualified ones, the falsifiable test for "this trade is unqualified," and the rule that converts the gap between checklist and gut into either a documented exception (rare) or a skip (almost always). Your strategy's edge is the *average expected value of qualified trades only*. Unqualified trades drag the strategy's measured edge toward zero, no matter how many of them happen to close green.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader's audit of 180 closed trades over a year, split by checklist compliance: 122 trades ticked every box on the written entry checklist; 58 trades missed at least one box. Win rate on qualified trades: 61%. Win rate on unqualified trades: 44%. Average expectancy on qualified: +4.9% per trade. Average expectancy on unqualified: -0.8% per trade. The unqualified trades, taken across the same strategy on the same instruments by the same trader, had *negative* expectancy as a group. They were not random noise; they were a structurally losing subset masquerading as part of the strategy.

The most uncomfortable line in the audit: the trader's *gross* P&L was driven entirely by the qualified subset. The 58 unqualified trades produced a small net loss that obscured the qualified subset's actual edge in the headline numbers. If he had simply not taken the 58 unqualified trades, his annual return would have been roughly 28% higher with two-thirds the trade count and meaningfully lower drawdowns. The strategy was good; the bored version of him was the strategy's largest cost.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that "experience" justifies a checklist exception. It might — for a few traders, after a decade, with statistical evidence. For most traders most of the time, "I just feel this one" is the verbal signature of boredom or overconfidence wearing experience's clothes. The correct mental model: the checklist is not a constraint *on* your edge — it is the *definition* of your edge. The strategy's expectancy is computed across qualified trades only, by definition. An unqualified trade is, by construction, not part of the strategy whose performance you are projecting forward. The disciplined response is to either revise the checklist *before* the next session (a deliberate, documented criteria change) or to skip the unqualified trade entirely. There is no third option that survives an audit.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and tag your last 30 closed trades as "qualified" or "unqualified" against your written entry checklist. Compute the win rate and expectancy of each subset. If the unqualified subset has lower expectancy (it almost always does), the empirical case for skipping them is built. Take the result to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and ask: "If I had not taken the unqualified trades, what would my account return look like?" The answer is the cost of the boredom you have not yet stopped paying.

## Takeaway

The checklist is not a constraint on your edge — it is the definition of your edge. Trades that miss the checklist are not part of the strategy you are sizing against.
