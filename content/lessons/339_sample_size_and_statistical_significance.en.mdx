# Sample size & statistical significance

A win rate calculated from a small number of trades carries a wide margin of error — small-n noise that can make an ordinary run of luck look like a real edge, or a real edge look like noise. The standard error of a win rate is roughly √(p(1-p)/n), where p is the observed win rate and n is the number of trades — it tells you how much that win rate could plausibly wobble just from randomness alone. A rough rule of thumb: an edge needs to clear about two standard errors above 50% before you should take it seriously as more than a coin flip.

## Example

A trader runs 20 trades and wins 14 of them — a 70% win rate (p = 0.7, n = 20). Standard error = √(0.7 × 0.3 / 20) = √(0.21 / 20) = √0.0105 ≈ **10.25%**. A rough two-standard-error bar is 2 × 10.25% = **20.5 percentage points**. The observed edge — 70% versus a 50% coin flip — is 20 points, which sits just **under** that 20.5-point bar. In other words, at only 20 trades, a 70% win rate is not yet clearly distinguishable from a 50% coin flip getting lucky, even though it feels like a strong result.

Now compare a bigger sample: 200 trades at the same 70% win rate. Standard error = √(0.7 × 0.3 / 200) = √(0.00105) ≈ **3.24%**, and 2 × 3.24% = **6.5 points** — the same 20-point edge now clears the bar with room to spare. The win rate didn't change; the confidence you can place in it changed enormously, purely because n went from 20 to 200.

## The trap

The trap is treating a small-sample win rate as if it were the true, stable edge — 14 wins out of 20 trades *feels* like solid proof, because 70% is a big, round, impressive-sounding number. The correct habit: before trusting any win rate, check the sample size behind it and estimate the standard error. A 70% record over 20 trades and a 70% record over 200 trades are not the same claim, even though they report the identical number.

## Try it

Open your Decision Journal, count how many trades your longest logged streak covers, and hand-calculate its standard error using √(p(1-p)/n). Compare the result to how confident you feel about that streak being a "real" edge. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

A win rate is only as trustworthy as the sample size behind it — always ask "how many trades is that based on?" before treating any streak as proof of an edge.
