# Size creep on winning streaks

Size creep is the slow, mostly invisible increase in per-trade risk that happens during a winning streak. It rarely arrives as a single 3x bet — it arrives as 1.0x, 1.1x, 1.2x, 1.4x, 1.7x, 2.3x across 20 trades. Building on lesson 049, this lesson teaches you the per-trade audit that catches the drift before the streak ends, why size creep is the *single most expensive* psychological pattern in trading, and how to wire the <Term id="ami_mandate" /> so size is decoupled from the equity curve. The trade that ends a winning streak is the largest one — and that is not a coincidence.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader's audit of 60 consecutive trades on US large-caps, May–August 2024: trades 1–20 averaged 1.02% per-trade risk; trades 21–40 averaged 1.31%; trades 41–60 averaged 1.83%. His mandate per-trade risk was 1.0%. He had not consciously raised it. The win rate across all three buckets was nearly identical at ~58%. The losing trades, however, paid out differently — the average losing-trade impact on account was 1.0% in bucket one, 1.3% in bucket two, and 1.9% in bucket three. The strategy's edge had not changed; the cost of being wrong had. By the time the inevitable losing cluster arrived (trade 58 was -2.4%, trade 59 was -2.1%, trade 60 was -2.6%, all within mandate-rule but at the creep-elevated size), the drawdown from the streak's peak was -8.7% on roughly 50/50 win-loss in the cluster. The same losing cluster at the original 1.0% mandate sizing would have been a -3.8% drawdown — survivable noise, not a streak-ending event.

The falsifiable size-creep fingerprint: rolling per-trade risk over the last 10 trades exceeds rolling per-trade risk over the prior 10 trades by 25% or more, AND no written change in setup criteria justifies the difference. Two numbers, both auditable. The streak is not the evidence; the position-size *vector* is.

## The trap

The trap is that size creep does not feel like rule-breaking. Each individual trade is "only slightly larger than the last one," and each is justified by a high-conviction note — "this setup is the cleanest one this month." The cumulative drift, across 20 trades, is invisible at any single decision point because the comparison the trader makes is between the *current* trade and the *last* trade, not between the current trade and the *mandate*. The correct mental model: the mandate is the only valid baseline for comparison, and per-trade risk should be a function of *setup quality measured against pre-written criteria*, not of recent outcomes. If you cannot point to a written setup-criteria change that justifies the size shift, the shift is creep — regardless of how well-justified each individual trade felt in isolation. The PM's <Term id="ami_pm_safety_floor" /> should refuse any single trade above the mandate, and the trader should refuse any *trend* above the mandate even if no single trade has crossed it.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and pull your last 30 trades with their per-trade risk percentages. Compute the rolling 10-trade average across the full series. Plot the trend. If the rolling average is up more than 25% from start to end with no written setup-criteria change, you have a documented size-creep pattern. Take the screen to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Portfolio Manager and ask: "Run my mandate against this size series — where would the safety floor have flagged?" If the answer is "nowhere on a per-trade basis," that is the diagnosis: the rule was per-trade, the failure was on the trend, and the mandate needs a rolling-average enforcement line added.

## Takeaway

Size creep ends every winning streak. Per-trade risk must be a function of setup quality, not of recent outcomes — and the mandate should enforce a rolling average, not just a per-trade cap.
