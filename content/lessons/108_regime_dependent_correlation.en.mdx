# Regime-dependent correlation: why correlations rise in crashes

This lesson assumes you've completed lesson 017 (Portfolio exposure and correlation); here we go deeper on a property of correlation that breaks most beginners' diversification math: pairwise correlations are not stable across regimes. In calm markets, NVDA and XOM might trade with a 0.05 correlation — practically independent. In a crash week, the same pair can run at 0.85 because everyone is selling everything to raise cash. Your "diversified" book becomes one big position the day you actually need the diversification. This lesson teaches how correlations spike with volatility, why "average" correlation hides the spike, and how the PM accounts for it.

## How this plays out in real markets

Pull a 90-day rolling correlation between NVDA and XOM on a calm market stretch in late 2024 — call it 0.04. Pull the same pair across a March 2020-style risk-off week and the realised correlation jumps to 0.72. Same two tickers, completely different relationship. The mechanism: in calm markets, each stock trades on its own fundamentals (NVDA on AI demand, XOM on oil supply). In a crash, everyone is selling whatever they own to meet margin calls, fund redemptions, or just stop the bleeding — and the selling pressure overwhelms the fundamental story. Correlations collapse toward 1 for everything liquid that's losing money.

Concrete: take a "diversified" 5-position book — NVDA, JPM, XOM, KO, IHH — each sized at 1% account risk. In calm 2024, the average pairwise correlation was about 0.15. The PM modelled total tail-event exposure at roughly 2.5% (not the naive 5%). In an actual 5-sigma risk-off day, realised pairwise correlations across the same 5 names jumped to 0.65 average. The realised loss was closer to 4.1% — not the 2.5% the calm-state model suggested, and not the 5% if everything moved together perfectly. The diversification did not vanish, but most of it did when it mattered most. On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK, PETRONAS, IHH, MRDIY, TENAGA — a sector-diverse book — saw average correlations rise from 0.20 in 2024 calm to about 0.55 in the Q1 2025 sell-off. Falsifiable test: pick any 5 single-stock positions from your <Term id="ami_watchlist" />, compute the rolling 30-day correlations on the 10 worst market days of the last year vs the 10 best — the bad-day correlations will average at least 2× the good-day correlations.

## The trap

The trap is sizing the portfolio off "long-run average" correlations and assuming you've diversified. A beginner reads that NVDA and JPM had 0.12 average correlation over the last 5 years and concludes the two positions are independent for risk purposes. The flaw: the 0.12 average hides the fact that in the worst 5% of days, correlation was 0.6+. Your portfolio's worst-case exposure is governed by the bad-day correlation, not the average. The correct mental model: assume correlations spike to 0.5–0.8 across any equity-heavy book in a real risk-off event, and size for that. The PM models a "stressed correlation" scenario — when you ask it for current portfolio exposure, ask separately for the stressed exposure. That second number is what your account actually has to survive.

## Try it

Open the Portfolio Manager <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> and ask "Across my current open positions, what is the calm-state average pairwise correlation, and what is the stressed-correlation estimate?" The PM will show both. If the stressed correlation pushes your single-factor exposure above your mandate ceiling, that's the trade you'd want to reduce before the next risk-off event, not during it.

## Takeaway

Diversification works on calm days and partly breaks on bad days — size for the stressed correlation, not the long-run average, or your real exposure shows up on the worst day of the year.
