# Transaction-cost modeling — the gap between paper and live

The backtest foundation (057) named look-ahead bias and overfitting as the killers; this lesson names the third silent killer for high-turnover rules: cost modeling. A backtest that assumes zero costs is fiction. A real backtest must model commissions, bid-ask spread, slippage on market orders, and market-impact for any size beyond tiny. For low-turnover strategies (1-2 trades/year) costs are a rounding error. For high-turnover strategies (RSI(2), intraday, scalping) costs can eat 80% of the gross edge. After this lesson you can apply realistic cost assumptions, identify when a paper-profitable strategy is actually unprofitable live, and choose cost-aware position sizing.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take an RSI(2) mean-reversion rule on SPY: buy when RSI(2) < 5, sell when RSI(2) > 95. Over 2015-2024 the zero-cost backtest shows 18.2% CAGR, 9% max drawdown, 71% win rate, 89 trades/year. Looks like a money printer. Now add costs.

SPY is the most liquid ETF on the planet. Spread is typically $0.01 on a $500 share — 2 basis points round-trip. Commissions on a modern broker are $0 per share. Slippage on a market order is typically 1-3 bps round-trip in normal hours, more around news. Total cost per round-trip: ~3-5 bps. With 89 round-trips per year, total cost drag is roughly 3-4.5% per year. Net CAGR drops from 18.2% to roughly 14%. Still very good — SPY's liquidity is the strategy's friend.

Now try the same rule on a small-cap like a low-volume Bursa name (a thinly-traded name on the Main Market with a 30-50 bps spread and meaningful slippage). Round-trip cost: 70-120 bps. With 89 trades/year, costs eat 60-100% of the paper edge — the strategy is unprofitable live. The same rule, the same parameters, the same edge — but the implementation surface destroys it. The honest backtest assumes the worst-case retail spread for your venue (US large-caps: 3-5 bps round-trip; US small-caps: 20-50 bps; Bursa large-caps like MAYBANK: 10-15 bps; Bursa small-caps: 50-150 bps), commissions per your broker, and a slippage model proportional to (your-order-size / average-daily-volume).

## The trap

The beginner mistake is using one cost number across all strategies. Costs are strategy-dependent, instrument-dependent, and size-dependent. A 5 bps assumption is right for liquid US large-caps at retail size and catastrophically wrong for any small-cap or any size beyond ~0.1% of average daily volume. The correct mental model: model costs per leg, then SCALE them by realistic worst-case (entry at the wrong end of the spread, partial fills on stops, occasional gap slippage). The second trap is ignoring asymmetric costs: stops fired at gap-down opens often execute well below the trigger price — slippage on a stop can be 5-10x normal-hours slippage. The third trap is testing on a venue you can't actually trade. <Term id="ami" /> is a <Term id="training_simulator" />, but the costs in the sim must mirror your real venue or the <Term id="ami_verdict" />s won't translate.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Research Manager, paste your current strategy's average trades-per-year, the typical instrument bid-ask spread, your broker commissions, and your expected order size as a percentage of average daily volume. Ask: "Compute realistic round-trip cost and net CAGR after costs. Is this strategy viable on this venue at this size?" Log the net number in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> — it's what you size against, not the gross.

## Takeaway

Gross CAGR is fiction; net CAGR after realistic costs is the only number that matters. High-turnover strategies live or die by the spread.
