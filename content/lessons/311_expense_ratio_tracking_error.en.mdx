# Expense ratio & tracking error

An expense ratio looks small — a fraction of a percent, deducted quietly from a fund's assets before you ever see a statement. That's exactly why it's dangerous: a fee too small to notice in any single year compounds into a number too large to ignore over decades, because it doesn't just cost you the fee — it costs you the returns that fee would have earned had it stayed invested. After this lesson you'll be able to compute what a fee actually costs over time and explain why tracking error is a second, separate cost most beginners never check.

## Example

Run the same $10,000 through two funds holding identical broad-market exposure, growing at the same 7% gross annual return before fees, for 30 years. A low-cost index fund charging a 0.03% expense ratio nets a 6.97% annual return and grows to **$75,484.86**. A comparable actively-managed fund charging a 0.75% expense ratio nets 6.25% and grows to **$61,640.79**. The gap — **$13,844.08** — is 18.3% of the low-cost fund's terminal value, given up for identical gross market exposure, not for worse stock-picking. That's the fee alone, before judging whether the active manager's picks even worked.

Well-known low-cost index products illustrate the scale funds operate at today: broad-market ETFs like VOO (tracking the S&P 500) or VTI (total US market) run expense ratios in the fractions-of-a-percent range — nowhere near the 0.75% used above, which represents a higher-cost actively managed category, not any specific fund quoted live. On Bursa Malaysia, an FBM KLCI tracker fund plays the same structural role: broad exposure at a low, disclosed annual fee versus a higher-cost actively managed unit trust holding similar large-cap names.

## The trap

Beginners compare funds on 1-year performance and treat the expense ratio as an afterthought — "0.75% is nothing, it's less than 1%." It feels harmless because it's deducted invisibly from the fund's NAV, never billed to you directly like a subscription. The correct mental model: a recurring fee is a *permanent tax on compounding*, charged every single year regardless of whether the fund made or lost money that year. A fee difference of 0.72 percentage points doesn't cost you 0.72% once — compounded over 30 years, it cost the $10,000 example above more than the entire original $10,000 investment.

Tracking error compounds the problem in the other direction. Even a passive index fund rarely delivers the index's exact return — sampling (holding a representative subset instead of every constituent), cash drag (holding a small cash buffer for redemptions), and the expense ratio itself all pull the fund's realized return slightly away from its benchmark. A wider tracking error means the fund is a less faithful copy of what you thought you were buying, on top of whatever it charges.

## Try it

Pull up the fact sheet for one ETF on your watchlist (in the app's fund-literacy reference or off-app). Find its stated expense ratio, then estimate the 10-year cost in dollars on a $10,000 position using the same method as this lesson's worked example — compare it to what a 0.75% comparable would have cost over the same period.

## Takeaway

A fee is not what you pay once — it's what you pay every year, forever, on top of whatever gains you would have compounded on that money instead.

*Where this comes from: Bogle's cost-matters hypothesis — Common Sense on Mutual Funds (1999) — the case that in a mostly efficient market, cost is the most reliable predictor of relative fund performance.*
