# Why activity costs you, before costs

<Lesson id="366_what_the_data_actually_says_about_day_trading"/> gave you the population base rate. This lesson gives you the single biggest mechanism behind most of it — and it isn't bad stock picking. It's how many times you pick.

## How this plays out in real markets

Barber and Odean followed 66,465 households at a large U.S. discount broker from 1991 to 1996, sorted every year into five equal groups by portfolio turnover. The gap between the busiest and the quietest group is the whole lesson in one table.

| Group | Behaviour | Net annual return |
|---|---|---|
| Quintile 1 | Least active (closest to buy-and-hold) | 18.5% |
| Quintile 5 | Most active | 11.4% |
| Average household | ~75% annual turnover | 16.4% |
| Market (value-weighted index) | — | 17.9% |

That's a 7.1-point annual gap between the most and least active groups, and here's the part that should surprise you: it wasn't because the busy group picked worse stocks. Barber and Odean also computed *gross* returns — performance before any commission, spread, or tax is subtracted — and found that quintile 5 did not significantly outperform quintile 1 before costs. Both groups picked stocks about equally well. The entire 7-point gap opened up after costs.

Run the mechanism yourself, at a much smaller scale than the paper's aggregate. Take a $20,000 account trading a liquid name like AAPL, where the combined bid-ask spread and a discount broker's commission might run on the order of 0.05–0.1% of position value per round trip — call it $15 on a $20,000 position, roughly 0.075%. Trivial, per trade. Do that three times a day, fully sized each time, across 250 trading days: 3 × 250 × 0.075% is around 56% of account value paid in friction over the year, purely from the *count* of trades, before a single one of them has to be right or wrong. Barber and Odean's quintile 5 households were nowhere near 750 round trips a year on average — their real drag was smaller than this toy number — but the direction and the mechanism are identical: cost scales with how often you trade, not with how good the trades are.

## The trap

Someone reading this will object, correctly, that a 1990s discount-brokerage sample of average households says nothing about a specifically skilled trader today. Fair — except the paper's own test is built to catch exactly that claim. If the busiest households had genuinely sharper picks, it would show up gross of costs, before the friction bill arrives. It didn't. The trap is treating "I trade more because my edge is sharper" as self-evidently true, when it's a claim your own <Term id="ami_decision_journal"/> can test the same way Barber and Odean tested 66,465 accounts: split your gross P&L from your net P&L and see whether the gap between them is growing faster than your edge is.

One caution running the other direction: AMI's simulator fills your <Term id="simulated_trade"/>s at the quoted price without modelling spread or commission drag. That's a deliberate simplification for a training app, not a claim that frictions don't exist — it means the gap this lesson describes will not show up automatically in your simulated P&L the way it would in a real account. Your simulated numbers are gross numbers. Treat them that way.

## Try it

Open your <Term id="ami_decision_journal"/> and pull your last ten closed <Term id="simulated_trade"/>s. Sum the price-move P&L alone, then count how many round trips it took to get there. Take that count to the Trader agent in <Term id="ami_one_on_one"/> and ask what a realistic per-trade cost would look like at your position size and frequency — spread, commission, and short-term tax treatment, since AMI's simulator won't compute that for you. Do the multiplication yourself before you decide your edge is real.

## Takeaway

Barber and Odean found a level playing field before costs and a lopsided one after them: 11.4% versus 18.5%, from quintiles that picked stocks about equally well. The cost of a trade isn't only the spread you paid on that trade — it's every other trade compounding fees and friction against you before your picks ever get judged on their own merits.
