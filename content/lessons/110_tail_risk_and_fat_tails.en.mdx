# Tail risk and fat tails

This lesson assumes you've completed <Lesson id="018_drawdown_management"/> (Drawdown management); here we go deeper on a specific reason drawdowns get out of hand — market returns do not follow a normal bell-curve distribution. They have "fat tails" — extreme events occur far more often than a normal distribution predicts. A move that should happen once every 10,000 years under a normal model happens roughly once every 10–15 years in real equity markets. Sizing as if returns are normal is the single biggest reason "this can't happen" portfolios blow up. This lesson teaches what fat tails are, the historical record, and the sizing adjustment that turns tail-aware into a real defence.

## How this plays out in real markets

The S&P 500's daily return distribution from 1928 to 2024 shows about 130 daily moves greater than 5% — up or down. Under a normal distribution fit to the same standard deviation, the predicted count would be roughly 0 across the entire 96-year window. Real moves of that size happened about 130 times more often than the normal model says they should. October 1987: −20.5% in a single session. March 2020: the S&P 500 fell 11.98% on 16 Mar. August 2024: the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% on 5 Aug. The pattern is consistent — once or twice a decade, a move occurs that a normal-distribution risk model says is statistically impossible.

Single-stock examples are worse. NVDA on a single session after a guidance shock: −17% in one day (27 Jan 2025). META on a 2022 earnings miss: −26% overnight. On Bursa Malaysia, AIRASIA during the 2020 pandemic shock: limit-down sessions back to back for −40%+ over a week. These are not "outliers to be ignored"; they are recurring features of equity markets. A 1% account risk per trade is built on the assumption that you can be wrong, but the gap risk in <Lesson id="104_stops_around_earnings"/> plus the tail risk discussed here means a "1%" position can deliver a 3–6% loss when the tail visits. Falsifiable test: look up any 20-year equity index history and count the number of >5% daily moves; the count will be at least 30× what a fitted normal distribution predicts.

## The trap

The trap is sizing positions as if "average" volatility is what defines risk. The beginner reads that NVDA's daily standard deviation is 2.5% and concludes that a 1% account risk position with a 2× standard-deviation stop is "very safe" — 95% of days won't hit it. The flaw: it's the 5% of days that kills portfolios, not the 95%, and the 5% are far more extreme than the 95% would predict. A 5σ event in a normal model should happen once every ~3.5M trading days (~14,000 years); in real markets, 5σ moves in single stocks happen multiple times a year. The correct mental model: the relevant question is not "what's my expected loss on a normal day?" but "what's my realised loss in the worst 1% of days, and can the account survive that?" Tail-aware sizing pulls per-trade risk down, increases cash reserves, and uses single-name caps to limit the damage from a single fat-tail event.

## Try it

Open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and ask "If my largest current position took an unexpected −20% single-day move tomorrow, what does my account look like the next morning?" Then open the <Term id="ami_mandate" /> editor and check your `risk_score` — the per-name cap it derives (`risk_tier_cap`) tops out at 4.5% even at the highest setting; if your largest position is sized well above that, the worst-case from the Conservative Debator's answer is the size of the buffer you've decided to live without.

## Takeaway

Markets visit the tail more often than the bell curve predicts — size for the bad 1% of days, not the average; tail events compound when you're not ready and stay survivable when you are.
