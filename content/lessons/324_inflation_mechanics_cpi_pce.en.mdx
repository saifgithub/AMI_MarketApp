# Inflation mechanics — CPI, PCE & why prices rise

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise across the economy, and it comes from two directions: demand-pull (too much money chasing too few goods) and cost-push (input costs — energy, wages, supply chains — force prices up regardless of demand). The two headline US gauges are CPI (Consumer Price Index, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics) and PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures, the Fed's own preferred measure) — they're built differently and can print different numbers in the same month. What matters for you as an investor isn't memorizing the formulas; it's understanding that inflation quietly eats your nominal returns, and the gap between nominal and real is not an approximation you can eyeball.

## Example

US CPI year-over-year inflation peaked at **9.1% in June 2022** (BLS) — the highest reading in over four decades, driven by a mix of pandemic-era demand-pull (stimulus-fueled spending) and cost-push (energy prices spiking after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, plus snarled supply chains). That single print reshaped the entire 2022-2023 market narrative: it's the number that pushed the Fed into the most aggressive hiking cycle in 40 years, which you'll trace through in M17.

Now the arithmetic that actually matters to your portfolio. Say your portfolio returned a nominal **10%** in a year where inflation ran **5%** (illustrative, clean numbers — not a live data point). The lazy shortcut is real ≈ nominal − inflation = 10% − 5% = 5%. The exact relationship (the Fisher equation) is: (1 + real) = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation). Solving: (1 + real) = 1.10 / 1.05 = 1.04762, so real return = **4.762%**, not 5%. The subtraction shortcut overstates your real gain by roughly a quarter of a percentage point here — small at low inflation, but the gap widens fast as inflation rises, which is exactly why the exact formula matters more in a high-inflation year like 2022 than in a calm one.

## The trap

The trap is anchoring to the subtraction shortcut and believing your portfolio kept pace with inflation when it didn't, quite. At low inflation (1-3%) the shortcut is close enough that the error rarely matters. At the kind of inflation the US saw in 2022 (peaking near 9%), the gap between the shortcut and the exact Fisher calculation grows large enough to change whether you actually beat inflation or merely broke even against it. The correct habit: when inflation is elevated, run the exact division, not the subtraction — especially before concluding "I beat inflation this year."

## The steelman

The strongest case against sweating CPI-versus-PCE precision at all: "Both measures move together over long stretches, revisions are common, and most investors will never build a portfolio strategy around a 0.3% gauge difference — this is textbook pedantry that doesn't change any real decision." That's largely true for casual reading of headlines. The falsifier: the two gauges diverge meaningfully in exactly the periods that matter most — CPI weights housing more heavily than PCE, so during a housing-cost surge (like 2022-2023) CPI can run persistently hotter than PCE, and the Fed sets policy off PCE, not CPI. If you're reading news about "the Fed's target" and mentally substituting the CPI number you saw that morning, you're reading the wrong gauge for the Fed's own reaction function — a distinction that matters precisely when the two disagree.

## Takeaway

Inflation is not one number — CPI and PCE can disagree — and once inflation runs hot, "real return ≈ nominal minus inflation" stops being close enough; do the exact division.

*Where this comes from: the Fisher equation relating nominal and real rates — Irving Fisher, The Theory of Interest (1930).*
