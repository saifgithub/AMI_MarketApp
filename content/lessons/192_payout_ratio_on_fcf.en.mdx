# Payout ratio on free cash flow — the real sustainability test

Lesson 043 introduced the payout ratio on earnings and flagged that the meaningful sustainability check is the payout ratio on free cash flow. This lesson does the deep version. Free cash flow (FCF) = cash from operations minus capex. Payout-on-FCF = total dividends paid / FCF. The reason this matters: a company can report positive earnings while burning cash if capex is high or working capital is consuming cash. Earnings can hide a dividend that isn't actually being earned in cash. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute payout-on-FCF, distinguish a dividend funded by operations from one funded by financing activity, and name the threshold above which a "high yield" should be reclassified as a cut waiting to happen.

## How this plays out in real markets

JPM in fiscal 2024 paid roughly $14B in common dividends against cash from operations of ~$54B (banks don't have traditional capex, so CFO ≈ FCF for our purposes here). Payout-on-FCF ≈ 26%, comfortable. KO in 2024 paid ~$8B in dividends, FCF was ~$10B, payout-on-FCF ≈ 80% — high but the FCF base is stable. T (AT&T) is the textbook stress case: in some years T paid more in dividends than it generated in FCF, with the gap funded by new debt. That is the precise pattern that preceded T's 2022 dividend cut.

A clean illustration of the earnings-vs-FCF gap: ITW (Illinois Tool Works) in 2024 reported net income near $3.7B and FCF near $3.3B — earnings slightly overstate cash, mostly from working-capital absorption. Dividends were ~$1.7B. Payout on earnings = 46%; payout on FCF = 52%. Both are comfortable, and the gap is small, signalling that earnings are a fair proxy for cash here.

Now an EM example where the gap is wide. A capex-heavy infrastructure-style business might report net income of $400M while FCF is only $50M — depreciation is a non-cash item and the cash actually went into new plant. If the company pays $300M in dividends, payout-on-earnings is a perfectly reasonable 75%, but payout-on-FCF is 600%. The dividend is being funded by debt or balance-sheet drawdown. Earnings hide the problem; cash flow exposes it.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK's payout-on-FCF runs in the 60–70% range, consistent with a mature bank returning most surplus capital to shareholders. KLCC, a REIT, is structurally near 100% by mandate — REITs have to distribute taxable income, so the FCF metric is interpreted differently. For non-REIT industrials on Bursa, the same 80%-FCF-payout heuristic applies.

Falsifiable claim: payout-on-FCF sustained above 100% for two consecutive years, funded by rising debt or shrinking cash on the balance sheet, predicts a dividend cut within 24 months with high frequency — outside the REIT/MLP structures that are mandated to distribute most cash. The signal invalidates for one-off years where capex spiked due to a specific large project, but only if the company guides to capex normalisation and the guidance proves out.

## The trap

The trap is sticking with payout-on-earnings because it's the number reported in most data feeds. Earnings can be manipulated through depreciation policy, working-capital management, or acquisition accounting; FCF is harder to fake because it's cash that either came in or went out. The correct mental model: earnings = the accounting story; FCF = what actually happened to the bank account. A dividend has to be paid in cash, so the only honest sustainability check is the cash-based ratio. What invalidates the FCF reading: changes in working capital can temporarily lift or depress FCF — strong receivables collection in one year boosts FCF that year while hurting it the next. Always look at the three-year average rather than a single quarter.

The funding source of any payout-above-100% year is the cut-trigger evidence.

## Takeaway

Earnings are the story; cash is the truth. A dividend is paid in cash, so the only honest sustainability check is payout-on-FCF — and a payout above 100% funded by debt is a cut waiting to happen.
