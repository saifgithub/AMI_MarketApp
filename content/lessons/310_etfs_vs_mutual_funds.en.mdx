# ETFs vs mutual funds vs index funds

"ETF," "mutual fund," and "index fund" describe two different things people mix up as one. ETF vs mutual fund is a question about *wrapper* — how you buy it, when it prices, how it's taxed. Index vs active is a question about *strategy* — does the fund copy a benchmark or pay someone to try to beat it. After this lesson you'll be able to place any fund on both axes independently and explain why the wrapper changes your cost and behavior even when the underlying holdings are identical.

## Example

Look at three ways to hold the same broad market exposure. SPY (SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust) trades on NYSE Arca all day like a stock — buy 3 shares at 10:47am, sell them at 2:15pm, at whatever price the market is making that minute. VFIAX (Vanguard 500 Index Fund, Admiral Shares) tracks the same S&P 500 index, but as a mutual fund it prices once, at 4pm ET market close — every order that day fills at that single net asset value (NAV), whether placed at 9:31am or 3:59pm. A third option, a traditional actively managed large-cap fund, also prices once daily, but a manager picks stocks and deviates from any benchmark, betting the deviation earns more than it costs.

The wrapper matters structurally, not just cosmetically. ETFs use an in-kind creation/redemption mechanism — institutional "authorized participants" swap ETF shares for a basket of the underlying stocks rather than cash. That mechanism is why ETFs are generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds in a taxable account: fewer forced capital-gains distributions land on shareholders who didn't sell anything. Mutual funds, needing cash to meet redemptions, sometimes must sell holdings and pass the resulting taxable gain to everyone still in the fund — including investors who held the whole year.

## The trap

Beginners treat "ETF" as a synonym for "cheap" and "index fund" as a synonym for "boring but safe," collapsing two independent choices into one. It feels right because low-cost index ETFs are the products beginners hear about first, so the three words blur into a single idea. The correct mental model: pick your strategy (index or active) and your wrapper (ETF or mutual fund) separately. You can buy an actively managed ETF (higher cost, ETF tax treatment) or an index mutual fund (low cost, single daily price) — four combinations exist, not one.

## The steelman

The case against ETFs-are-simply-better: a mutual fund's single daily price is a feature, not a bug, for a systematic investor. It removes the temptation to watch a ticker tick and trade on the noise — you place an order and it fills at end-of-day NAV regardless of intraday panic. Automatic monthly contribution plans, common with 401(k)-style mutual fund lineups, also don't map cleanly onto ETF share-based buying. The falsifier: if intraday liquidity caused worse investor outcomes, ETF investors should show measurably worse behavior-gap returns (fund return minus what the average dollar in the fund actually earned) than mutual fund investors in the same index. Where that gap has been measured, it favors the discipline argument in some years and the cost/tax argument in others — the honest read is that the wrapper choice is a real tradeoff, not a solved question.

## Try it

Open the app's fund-literacy reference (or your own brokerage's fund screener off-app) and find one ETF and one mutual fund that track the same index. Note the ticker, the trading mechanism (intraday vs once-daily NAV), and which one you'd expect to distribute more in taxable capital gains in a normal year — then check if your reasoning matches the fund's actual distribution history.

## Takeaway

Wrapper and strategy are two separate questions — ask "how does this trade and get taxed" and "does this copy a benchmark or try to beat it" separately, every time.
