# Base rates — why they beat the story

A base rate is how often something is actually true across the whole population it's drawn from, before you look at any specific evidence in front of you — and base-rate neglect is ignoring that background frequency because a vivid, specific-feeling story (a chart pattern, a screener flag, a hot tip) feels more persuasive than a boring population statistic. The outside view asks "how often does this kind of claim turn out to be true, across everyone who's made it?" before it asks "does this particular case feel convincing?" Skipping that first question is how a genuinely rare event gets treated as a sure thing.

## Example

Say a breakout screener scans 1,000 stocks. The true base rate of a real breakout among all scanned stocks is 10% — 100 real breakouts, 900 non-breakouts. The screener is good: it correctly flags 90% of real breakouts (sensitivity), and it also mistakenly flags 10% of non-breakouts as "breakout" too (false-positive rate). Run the numbers: true positives = 90% × 100 = **90**. False positives = 10% × 900 = **90**. Total flagged as "breakout" = 90 + 90 = **180**. Of those 180 alerts, only 90 are real — precision = 90 / 180 = **50%**.

A screener that's 90% accurate on both fronts still produces alerts that are correct only half the time, because non-breakouts vastly outnumber breakouts in the underlying population (900 vs. 100) — even a small false-positive rate applied to a huge pool of non-events generates as much noise as the real signal. The screener's accuracy numbers sound great in isolation; the base rate is what tells you the alert itself is a coin flip.

## The trap

The trap is reacting to "the screener flagged it" as if that alone means "it's probably a real breakout" — treating a specific-feeling alert as stronger evidence than the boring population math sitting behind it. It feels right because the flag is concrete and immediate, while the 10%-base-rate arithmetic is abstract and easy to skip. The correct habit: before trusting any flag, alert, or hot tip, ask what fraction of the underlying population it's actually drawn from — a 90%-accurate signal on a 10%-base-rate event still means about half the alerts are false alarms, as the 50% precision above shows directly.

## Takeaway

Before trusting any flag, alert, or tip, ask how rare the thing it claims to detect actually is in the whole population — a highly "accurate"-sounding signal on a rare event can still be wrong about half the time.
