# The efficient frontier, in plain arithmetic

The efficient frontier is usually drawn as a curve and explained as a concept, which hides the one result worth having: mixing a riskier asset into a safer one can lower the portfolio's volatility *and* raise its expected return at the same time. After this lesson you will be able to compute that crossover point for two assets by hand, and to recognise the portfolios that sit below it — the ones that take risk and are paid nothing for it.

## Example

Two openly hypothetical sleeves, with assumed inputs stated up front so nothing is smuggled in. Sleeve **A**: 20% volatility, 8% expected return. Sleeve **B**: 12% volatility, 5% expected return. Correlation between them: **0.20**. The expected returns are assumptions of this example — they are what a frontier calculation takes as input, not something anyone can observe.

Vary the weight in A and compute both coordinates:

| weight in A | portfolio volatility | expected return |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | **12.00%** | 5.00% |
| 10% | **11.37%** | 5.30% |
| **21.43%** | **11.11%** | **5.64%** |
| 30% | **11.26%** | 5.90% |
| 50% | **12.65%** | 6.50% |
| 100% | **20.00%** | 8.00% |

Read the 21.43% row against the 0% row. Holding B alone gives 12.00% volatility and 5.00% expected return. Adding 21.43% of the *more volatile* asset gives **11.11% volatility and 5.64% expected return** — less risk and more return than the safe sleeve on its own. Nothing is being conjured: A's returns are only weakly related to B's, so a slice of A cancels some of B's variation, and the cancellation outweighs A's own volatility until the weight gets large enough.

That 21.43% is the **minimum-variance portfolio**, and it comes from a closed form rather than from trial and error:

w_A = ( σ_B² − ρσ_Aσ_B ) ÷ ( σ_A² + σ_B² − 2ρσ_Aσ_B )

Substituting: (0.0144 − 0.0048) ÷ (0.0400 + 0.0144 − 0.0096) = 0.0096 ÷ 0.0448 = **21.43%**.

Now the part that gives the frontier its name. Every mix **below** 21.43% in A — including holding B alone — is **dominated**: for each one there is a portfolio further along the curve with the same volatility and a higher expected return. Those mixes are not merely conservative; they take risk and are paid less than the same risk earns elsewhere on the curve. The **efficient frontier** is the portion from the minimum-variance point upward, and everything below it is a mistake under this model's own assumptions, however cautious it looks.

## The trap

The trap is treating the safest-looking sleeve as the safest portfolio. Holding B alone feels like the conservative choice — it is the lower-volatility asset, and no one who bought it took a risk they cannot explain. But the table shows a mix holding 21.43% of the *riskier* asset with lower total volatility, so "B only" is both riskier and lower-returning than an available alternative. The intuition fails because it reasons about assets one at a time, and risk is a property of the combination.

The correct mental model is that assets have no risk ranking outside a portfolio. A 20%-volatility asset is a risk-*reducer* in a book that does not already own its drivers, and a risk-*adder* in one that does. That is why the question "is this a risky stock?" has no answer, and the question "what does this do to my book's variance?" always does.

## The steelman

The strongest case against acting on any of this: the frontier's shape is driven by inputs nobody knows. Expected returns in particular are estimated with enormous error — a mean return measured over a few years has a standard error wide enough to contain almost any plausible value — and small changes in those estimates move the optimal weights violently. Optimisers built on such inputs are notorious for producing confident, unstable, concentrated portfolios. That criticism is correct and is why practitioners lean on the parts of the calculation that estimate best. Volatilities and correlations are second moments, and they are estimable from far less data than means; the minimum-variance point uses **only** those — no expected return appears anywhere in its closed form. So the falsifier is worth stating precisely: if you find yourself trusting an exact optimal weight that depends on a return forecast, the criticism applies in full and you should distrust it. If you are using the frontier to notice that a portfolio is *dominated* — a structural fact that survives wide error bars on the means — it is doing work the criticism does not touch.

## Try it

Take two holdings from your simulated Portfolio whose drivers you believe differ — different customers, different input costs, different rate sensitivity. Write down your rough estimate of each one's annual volatility and of their correlation, then compute the minimum-variance weight from the closed form above. You will get a number, and the useful part is comparing it with what you actually hold. Then ask the Portfolio Manager in a 1-on-1 what would have to be true for your current split to be the better one, and judge the argument rather than adopting it.

## Takeaway

Risk is a property of the combination, not the asset — which is why a slice of the more volatile holding can lower your portfolio's volatility, and why the cautious-looking single-asset book is often the one taking risk it is not being paid for.

*Where this comes from: Markowitz's mean-variance framework and the efficient set it defines — Portfolio Selection (1952).*
