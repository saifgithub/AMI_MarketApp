# Time horizon and goals

Your time horizon is the single number that drives everything else: which instruments are sensible, how much volatility you can tolerate, how often you should look at prices, and which agents in <Term id="ami" />'s team are even relevant to your decisions. A 30-year retirement goal and a 6-month house-deposit goal both involve buying assets, but they are not the same exercise — they have different acceptable drawdowns, different rebalance schedules, and different definitions of "a bad year." Naming your goal and naming its deadline is what turns a vague intention into a testable plan.

## How this plays out in real markets

Two readers, same starting capital of $20,000, same year — 2022.

Reader A's goal: retirement, 25-year horizon. Buys a broad-equity proxy similar to SPY. SPY closed 2022 down roughly 18%. A's account: $16,400. A's correct response: do nothing. The 25-year horizon contains many 18%-down years. A 2026 view of that account: SPY recovered, hit new highs in 2024–25, and the position is worth substantially more than the entry. Drawdown was noise.

Reader B's goal: house down payment in 18 months. Same SPY position, same $20,000. Same 18% drawdown lands B at $16,400 — except B needs the cash by mid-2023. B can no longer wait for recovery. B locks in the loss, or worse, waits, panics, and sells lower. The same instrument was correct for A and wrong for B.

The mistake was not picking SPY. The mistake was picking an instrument whose normal volatility (SPY has had drawdowns above 20% several times in the last 25 years) exceeded the horizon. An 18-month goal probably belongs in short-duration cash equivalents or T-bills, not equities, even when equities look attractive.

## The trap

The trap is letting market conditions, not the goal, set the horizon. A bull market makes every horizon look short — "I'll just hold for one more year, the trend is strong." A bear market makes every horizon look long — "I'm a long-term investor now." Neither is honest. Your goal's deadline does not move because the chart is green or red. Write your horizon down in the <Term id="ami_mandate" /> before you ever look at a quote, and treat any urge to extend it during a drawdown or shorten it during a rally as a signal to re-read this lesson, not as new information.

## Try it

Open the Mandate editor and fill in two fields you may have skipped: **primary_goal** and **horizon_years**. Pick the goal that matters most ("retirement", "house deposit", "education", "general wealth"). Then start a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the <Term id="concierge_agent" /> and ask: "Given a horizon of X years and a goal of Y, what mandate settings are typical?" The answer is a starting point, not gospel — but it gives you something concrete to push back on.

## Takeaway

The horizon sets the instrument, not the other way around — write it down before you look at a chart, and don't let a green or red day move it.
