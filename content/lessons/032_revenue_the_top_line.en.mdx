# Revenue: the top line

Revenue is the first number on the income statement — the total money a company collected for its goods and services in a period, before any costs are subtracted. It's called the "top line" because it sits at the top of the page; everything below it (costs, taxes, profit) is what's left after subtraction. By the end of this lesson you should know what revenue does and doesn't tell you, how to read it as a growth signal, and the specific situation where rising revenue is a warning instead of a win.

## Example

Look at NVDA across fiscal years 2024 and 2025. NVDA reported revenue of $60.9B for fiscal 2024 and $130.5B for fiscal 2025 — roughly 114% year-over-year growth driven by data-centre GPU demand. That's an extreme number; most mature companies grow revenue 3%–10% a year. By contrast AAPL reported revenue of $383.3B in fiscal 2023 and $391.0B in fiscal 2024 — about 2% growth on a much larger base. Both are "good" businesses, but the revenue trajectories tell you very different stories: NVDA is in a demand explosion phase, AAPL is in a mature-cash-cow phase.

The composition matters as much as the headline. NVDA's data-centre segment grew from $47.5B to $115.2B between those two years — that single segment carried the entire company. If you only read the top line and didn't ask "where did the growth come from?", you'd miss that NVDA had become a one-segment story. AAPL's iPhone revenue (~$201B in fiscal 2024) is similarly concentrated — about half the company. When one product or segment carries the top line, a shock to that segment is a shock to the whole company.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK reported group revenue of roughly RM27B in 2023, growing in the mid-single digits — typical of a large mature bank where loan growth and net interest income move slowly. A 30% revenue jump would be more suspicious than impressive for a bank that size.

## The trap

The trap is treating revenue growth as a stand-alone "good" number. A beginner sees revenue up 40% and assumes the business is thriving — without checking whether that growth came from selling more at the same price (real demand), raising prices into a flat market (pricing power, but with churn risk), or buying another company (acquisition revenue, which is one-off in the year of the deal). Worse, revenue can grow while margins collapse — a company chasing the top line by discounting heavily or paying customers to use the product can post lovely revenue growth and bleed cash doing it. The correct mental model: revenue tells you the *size* of the business, not the *health* of it. Always ask where the growth came from and what it cost to get.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and pick a stock you've tracked. In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, ask: "Break down this company's last four quarters of revenue by segment, and tell me which segment is driving growth." Then write the segment-concentration number in the journal entry. Next time you size that position, you'll have the concentration risk in front of you.

## Takeaway

Revenue tells you how big a business is, not how good it is — always ask where the growth came from and what it cost to get there before you call it a win.
