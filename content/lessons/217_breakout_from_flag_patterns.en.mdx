# Breakout from flag and pennant patterns

The breakout foundation (053) used horizontal range resistance. Flags and pennants are a tighter, faster variant: after a sharp directional move (the "flagpole"), price consolidates in a narrow, slightly counter-trend channel for 5 to 15 sessions before resuming the original direction. The edge is in the contraction — volatility compresses, volume dries up, then the breakout from the consolidation completes the prior move with a measurable price projection. After this lesson you can name what qualifies as a flag, what disqualifies one, and how to size the position from a measured-move target.

## Example

Look at NVDA in late October 2024. The stock had run from $130 on October 9 to $144.42 on October 21 — a roughly 11% flagpole over nine sessions. From October 22 through October 30, price drifted sideways-down in a tight channel between $138 and $144 on visibly declining volume. The 20-day average daily volume was around 220 million shares; the consolidation days averaged closer to 165 million. On November 1, NVDA closed at $138.42 — but the cleaner trigger came on November 6 when it gapped through $144.42 and closed at $145.61 on 240 million shares (above average), completing the flag breakout. Measured move: $144.42 - $130 = $14.42 added to the breakout level gives a target of $158.84. NVDA reached $152 within four sessions and tagged $148 to $150 repeatedly through November, satisfying most of the measured move.

Entry criteria: (1) prior flagpole of 5%+ in 5 to 15 sessions, (2) consolidation channel that retraces less than 50% of the flagpole, (3) volume contracts during consolidation vs the flagpole, (4) daily close above the consolidation high. Stop: a close back inside the consolidation channel, OR the low of the consolidation, whichever is tighter. Target: measured move = flagpole height added to the breakout level. Individual-trade invalidation: a daily close back inside the consolidation range — the flag failed; exit. The strategy FAILS when the consolidation is too long (over 15 sessions) — at that point it's no longer a flag, it's a base, and the measured-move projection loses its statistical edge. It also FAILS in choppy macro tapes where every "flag" is a distribution top in disguise.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is calling any pullback inside an uptrend a "flag" and projecting a measured move that has no basis. Real flags have three properties: a steep flagpole, a tight counter-trend channel, and visible volume contraction. Miss any of the three and you're not looking at a flag — you're looking at a normal pullback or, worse, distribution. The correct mental model: the measured-move projection is only valid when all three properties hold. If volume didn't contract during the consolidation — if it stayed flat or rose — the flag is suspect and the target math is fantasy. The other trap: chasing the breakout candle's high. Wait for the close.

State the flagpole, consolidation range, volume profile, and measured-move target. If it disqualifies, say why." ## Takeaway

A flag is a flagpole, a tight counter-trend channel, AND volume contraction — all three. Without all three, the measured-move target is invented arithmetic.
