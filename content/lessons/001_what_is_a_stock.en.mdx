# What is a stock?

A stock is a fractional ownership claim on a real company. Buy one share and you legally own a sliver of the business — its factories, its software, its brand, its future profits. If the company has 100 million shares outstanding and you hold one, you own one-hundred-millionth of it. That ownership comes with two specific rights: a claim on whatever profits the company chooses to distribute as dividends, and (usually) a vote on big company decisions. That is the whole definition. Everything else in this app builds on that single idea.

## Example

Apple (ticker: AAPL) had roughly 15 billion shares outstanding in early 2026. Suppose you buy 10 shares at $190 each — that's a $1,900 position. You now own 10 / 15,000,000,000 of Apple, or about 0.0000000007 of the entire company. Tiny, but real. Apple paid a dividend of roughly $1.00 per share over the past year. So your 10 shares would have paid you about $10 in cash, deposited into your account regardless of what the stock price did that quarter.

If Apple's share price rises to $209 over the year, your 10 shares are now worth $2,090. Your position gained $190 in price plus $10 in dividends — a $200 total return on a $1,900 investment, or 10.5%.

On Bursa Malaysia: a Maybank (MAYBANK) share at RM10 with 12 billion shares outstanding works the same way. One share = one twelve-billionth of Maybank. The currency changes; the mechanic does not.

## The trap

The most common beginner mistake is treating a stock like a lottery ticket — a piece of paper that goes up or down for reasons nobody can pin down. That framing makes you a gambler. The correct mental model is: a stock is a slice of a business, and a business produces (or fails to produce) cash. Price moves on what people think that future cash will look like. The lottery framing tells you "buy what's hot." The ownership framing tells you "buy what you'd be happy to own a slice of if the market closed for five years." That second question is the one every analyst on your team is trained to answer.

## Try it

Open your Watchlist and add one ticker you recognise — AAPL, NVDA, or MAYBANK if you're in Malaysia. Tap it and look at the "shares outstanding" field. Divide one by that number on a calculator. That fraction is what you'd own per share. Sit with how small it is. That's the unit of ownership we'll talk about for the next 80 lessons.

## Takeaway

A stock is a sliver of a real business — a claim on its future profits, not a ticket in a price-guessing game. Buy slivers of businesses you would still want to own if the screen went dark for five years.
