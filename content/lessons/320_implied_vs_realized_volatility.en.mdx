# Implied vs realized volatility

Implied volatility (IV) is the market's forward-looking guess, baked into option prices, of how much an underlying is likely to move before expiry. Realized volatility is what the stock actually did once the period is over. The two rarely match exactly, and the gap between them is where option buyers and sellers systematically win or lose — not from being right about direction, but from being right or wrong about how much the market's *estimate of uncertainty* was worth. After this lesson you'll be able to tell the difference between "this stock is about to crash" and "the market is pricing a big move, in either direction."

## Example

Ahead of NVDA's quarterly earnings, options implied volatility might run well above NVDA's typical level the rest of the year (illustrative — not a live quote), because the market is pricing in the chance of an outsized post-earnings move. After the print, once the uncertainty resolves, implied volatility "crushes" back down toward normal levels regardless of which direction the stock actually moved — because the event IV was pricing has now happened and passed. This is why two options on the same stock, one filed before earnings and one after, can imply very different expected ranges even though nothing about the company's long-run business changed.

## The trap

Beginners conflate "implied volatility is high" with "the stock is about to crash." IV measures the *size* of an expected move, not its direction — a stock can have sky-high IV heading into a binary event and still rally hard. The mirror trap: assuming a "cheap-looking" option with a low premium is automatically a bargain, without checking whether IV is low because the market genuinely expects a quiet stretch — in which case a low price for a low-probability event can still be a poor bet, not a discount.

## The steelman

A skeptic could argue that since implied volatility is "the market's best guess," it's already efficient — there's no edge in noticing IV-versus-realized spreads, you're just reading a price the market already set correctly. The falsifier: the historical spread itself. Across long samples, implied volatility has tended to run persistently above subsequently realized volatility — a volatility risk premium — which is why systematic option-selling strategies have documented a long-run edge — not because any single trade is a coin flip, but because each one is a high-win-rate, negatively skewed bet (roughly 70-90% probability of a small profit, offset by rarer, larger losses when the tail hits). If IV were unbiased on average, that persistent spread wouldn't exist. The falsifier is checking the historical IV-minus-realized gap on a broad index across many cycles, not the outcome of any one earnings print.

## Try it

## Takeaway

Implied volatility prices the size of an expected move, not its direction, and it tends to overstate what actually happens on average — realized volatility is the scorecard that eventually settles the bet.

*Where this comes from: the volatility risk premium — the documented, long-run tendency for implied volatility to exceed subsequently realized volatility across broad equity index option markets.*
