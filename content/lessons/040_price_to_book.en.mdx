# Price-to-book

Price-to-book is what you'd pay today for one dollar of a company's accounting net worth. Formula: P/B = market price per share / book value per share, or market cap / shareholders' equity. Book value is total assets minus total liabilities — what the balance sheet says is left for shareholders if you liquidated everything at carrying cost. By the end of this lesson you should know where P/B works (banks, insurers, asset-heavy businesses), where it breaks (software, brands, IP-heavy companies), and the specific condition that turns a "cheap" P/B into a warning.

## How this plays out in real markets

JPMorgan in mid-2026 trades around $220 with book value per share of roughly $115, so P/B ≈ 1.9x. Berkshire Hathaway B-shares trade near $480 with book value around $400, P/B ≈ 1.2x. Both are financials where book value is mostly liquid, mark-to-market-ish assets (loans, securities, cash), and P/B carries real information. Historically, large US banks trade in a 0.8x–1.5x range; a sustained move above 1.5x usually requires ROE meaningfully above the cost of equity. JPM's 1.9x is defensible because it has earned 17%+ ROE — see <Lesson id="041_return_on_equity"/> — well above the ~10% cost of equity, and the market is paying a premium for that excess return.

Now look at MSFT. Stock near $480, book value per share around $42, P/B ≈ 11x. Is MSFT "ten times more expensive" than JPM? No — the comparison is meaningless. Most of MSFT's value is intangible: software IP, Azure infrastructure earnings power, the Office franchise, decades of R&D that GAAP expensed as it happened and never capitalised on the balance sheet. Book value systematically understates the worth of asset-light businesses. P/B for MSFT, GOOGL, META is essentially uninformative.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK trades around 1.3x book — typical for a large regional bank earning low-to-mid teens ROE. PUBLIC BANK has historically traded at 1.8x–2.0x book because of consistently higher ROE and lower credit-loss volatility. Same sector, similar size — different P/B premiums driven by return quality, not size.

Falsifiable claim: P/B is a useful ranking tool only when (a) the business is asset-heavy (financials, REITs, industrials, miners) AND (b) book value is reasonably representative of liquidation or replacement value. For software, consumer brands, and platforms, a P/B above 5x is the rule, not the exception, and the ratio carries no signal.

## The trap

The trap is screening for "cheap" stocks at P/B below 1.0x and assuming they're undervalued. A bank trading at 0.6x book is almost never a steal — it's the market signalling that loan-book quality is suspect, that credit losses are likely to chew through equity, or that ROE has collapsed below cost of capital. European banks spent the better part of a decade trading at 0.4x–0.7x book; investors who treated each one as a "deep value" buy got slowly hollowed out as book value itself eroded. The correct mental model: P/B and ROE are joined at the hip. A high ROE justifies a high P/B; a low or negative ROE explains and *justifies* a low P/B. What invalidates the signal: large goodwill from acquisitions inflates book value with something that may be impaired tomorrow. Always check tangible book (book value minus goodwill and intangibles) for acquisition-heavy companies before trusting the P/B.

## Takeaway

Price-to-book is a sector-conditional tool: useful for financials and asset-heavy businesses, near-useless for software and platforms. The ratio is only meaningful when paired with ROE — high ROE justifies a high P/B, low ROE explains a low one.
