# A dividend safety scoring framework

Lesson 043 introduced the concept of a sustainable dividend, and lesson 192 went deep on payout-on-FCF as the headline cash-coverage check. This lesson builds a multi-factor scoring framework — six tests, each binary — that produces a single dividend-safety read. The framework isn't a black box; it's a checklist that forces the reader to look in the places where a dividend gets cut. By the end of this lesson you should be able to apply the six tests to any dividend-paying name, score it, and name the precise factor combination that predicts a cut roughly 12–18 months ahead.

## How this plays out in real markets

The six-test framework:

1. **Payout on FCF below 80%** (over a 3-year average, not just one year).
2. **FCF trend flat or growing** over 3 years.
3. **Interest coverage above 3.0x** so the debt-service cushion exists.
4. **Net debt / EBITDA below 4.0x** (or below 5.0x for utilities/REITs).
5. **Dividend has been maintained or grown** through the last full economic cycle.
6. **No material refinancing wall** in the next 24 months that could force a capital allocation choice between bondholders and shareholders.

Apply it to KO in fiscal 2024. Payout-on-FCF roughly 75% (3-year average) → pass. FCF growing slowly → pass. Interest coverage roughly 11x → pass. Net debt / EBITDA roughly 1.7x → pass. Dividend maintained and raised through COVID → pass. No near-term refinancing wall → pass. Six of six: a dividend that is functionally guaranteed barring catastrophe.

Apply it to T (AT&T) circa 2021 — before the cut. Payout-on-FCF over 90% in the trailing year → fail. FCF declining for two consecutive years → fail. Interest coverage roughly 3.0x → marginal. Net debt / EBITDA over 3.0x and rising due to media-segment investment → fail. Dividend maintained for many years → pass. Refinancing wall ahead → fail. Four to five fails of six. T cut the dividend in 2022 — the framework would have flagged this two years earlier.

Apply it to MSFT in 2024. Payout-on-FCF roughly 25% → pass. FCF growing strongly → pass. Interest coverage roughly 38x → pass. Net debt / EBITDA negative (net cash position) → pass. Dividend maintained and raised every year since initiated in 2003 → pass. No refinancing wall → pass. Six of six: ultra-safe.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK scores roughly five of six — the only marginal factor is payout-on-FCF being elevated in the 65–75% range, common for mature banks. KLCC as a REIT is structurally close to 100% payout by mandate, so the framework reads differently for REITs — the relevant test there is occupancy, rent escalations, and refinancing of the property-level debt, not the corporate-style payout ratio.

Falsifiable claim: a dividend-paying name that fails three or more of the six tests has a forward 12–24-month dividend cut probability materially higher than the base rate (the historical base rate of dividend cuts among S&P 500 dividend payers in any given year is roughly 2–4%). The framework invalidates if there's a structural reason for a "fail" — a one-time refinancing wall the company has already pre-funded, for example — but those need to be verified, not assumed.

## The trap

The trap is treating the scoring framework as a black box and ignoring context. A REIT will fail the payout-on-FCF test by construction, because REITs are mandated to distribute 90%+ of taxable income. A bank will look unusually leveraged on traditional D/E because banks fund loans with deposits. The correct mental model: the framework is a checklist, not a formula. Each fail is a question, not a verdict — "why is this factor failing, and is the reason structural or worrying?" What invalidates the surface reading: management's stated dividend policy matters. A company that has publicly committed to a payout target tied to free cash flow signals different behaviour than one with no policy and a history of cutting when convenient.

## Takeaway

A sustainable dividend is one that passes a checklist, not one that has a long history. The history is the lagging indicator; payout-on-FCF, FCF trend, coverage, and refinancing exposure are the leading ones.
