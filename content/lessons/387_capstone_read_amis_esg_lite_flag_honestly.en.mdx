# Capstone: read AMI's ESG-lite flag honestly

You now hold the M26 toolkit: a score is one agency's opinion under one methodology, a claim is worth what its falsifier is worth, and exclusion, integration and stewardship do three different jobs. This capstone turns all of it on the product you are holding. AMI ships an ESG-lite Mandate flag; the exercise is to describe exactly what it does, decide which of the three approaches it is, and judge whether its own description of itself passes the test you have been applying to everyone else's.

## Example

Here is what the flag mechanically does, read off the code rather than the marketing.

**It is an exclusion, and only an exclusion.** With `esg_lite` set, the Portfolio Manager's safety floor refuses a simulated trade in a ticker that lands in a curated exclusion set. It does not score anything, rank anything, or price anything.

**The set is three reviewable buckets, joined.** Fossil fuels (oil and gas across integrated, exploration, midstream, refining, equipment and drilling, plus thermal and coking coal, each additionally gated on the company sitting in the Energy sector), *plus* tobacco, brewers, wineries and distillers, gambling and casinos, *plus* one industry bucket for aerospace and defence. That is the whole rule, and it is stated as a rule rather than derived from a score.

**The classification is by industry label, from a market-data feed.** Which produces two consequences you should be able to state before you rely on it. A diversified conglomerate earning a slice of its revenue from an excluded activity is **not** caught, because its industry label is something else — this is a category rule, not a revenue-share screen. And the aerospace-and-defence bucket is a single industry string, so a company whose business is largely commercial aircraft is excluded alongside the weapons primes. That breadth is deliberate and disclosed as curation, not presented as precision.

**Coverage is the S&P 500 parent set.** A ticker outside it has not been classified, and AMI returns **UNKNOWN** — which **permits** the trade while telling you it is unclassified. That choice is load-bearing: if UNKNOWN blocked, the filter would silently reject every name AMI has not classified, which is a much worse failure than letting an unscreened name through with a notice. There is a fourth state, **UNAVAILABLE**, for when the classification data is stale; that one blocks, because a stale ruling presented as current is exactly the confident-but-false output this app is built to avoid.

**And it says so.** Every ESG-lite verdict carries the phrase *"AMI's curated best-effort ESG proxy (fossil fuels + tobacco/alcohol/gambling + weapons/defense), NOT a rated ESG score"*, with the source and the as-of date attached.

Now apply the module. Against <Lesson id="384_what_an_esg_score_is_and_is_not"/>: this is not a score at all, so the rater-divergence problem does not apply to it — but the three judgements that produce divergence are still being made here, by us, and they are published as a list rather than compressed into a number. Against <Lesson id="385_greenwashing_how_to_test_the_claim"/>: the claim is falsifiable, and you can construct the contradicting holding — a permitted trade in a pure-play tobacco company inside the S&P 500 would prove the flag broken. Against <Lesson id="386_exclusion_integration_stewardship"/>: it is exclusion, so it achieves "I do not own this" within its coverage, and it does nothing about pricing and nothing about company behaviour.

**The honest summary is unflattering in a specific way.** The flag is called ESG and contains no governance dimension whatever, and its social dimension is one list of sin industries. It is an E-and-a-bit-of-S exclusion rule with an ESG name — which is, in miniature, exactly the naming problem the SEC's Names Rule went after. Knowing that is the difference between using the flag for what it does and believing it does something else.

## The trap

The trap in a capstone about your own product is grading it kindly because it is yours, and the specific form it takes here is accepting "it says it is best-effort" as though the disclaimer settled the question. A disclaimer is a claim like any other, and the test from <Lesson id="385_greenwashing_how_to_test_the_claim"/> applies to it unchanged: what would make it false? The disclaimer is accurate — the set really is curated, it really is not a rated score — and that is a finding, not a courtesy.

The correct mental model: honest labelling and adequate coverage are two separate audits, and passing the first does not pass the second. This flag passes the labelling audit comfortably: it says what it is, it names its source and date, and it refuses to imply a rating. On coverage it is narrow, industry-level rather than revenue-level, and silent on governance. Both sentences are true at once, and the useful reader is the one who can hold them together rather than choosing whichever one matches how they already felt about the feature.

## The steelman

The strongest case against shipping a flag this narrow at all: calling something "ESG-lite" when it carries no governance dimension may mislead more than it helps, and a user who ticks it and assumes their book is screened has been handed a false sense of completeness that no disclaimer fully undoes — CR038's finding that prompt-level warnings do not travel with a screenshot applies to interface copy just as well. On that reading the honest options are a rated feed or nothing, and a curated proxy is the worst of both. That is a serious argument and it is why the flag's messaging is structural rather than advisory: the disclaimer is attached to every verdict, not stated once at sign-up, and UNKNOWN reports itself rather than passing silently. The falsifier is about users rather than about intentions, and it is worth stating so it can actually be run: ask someone who has the flag switched on what it excludes. If they can name the three buckets and know that names outside the S&P 500 are unscreened, the labelling is doing its job. If they believe their portfolio has been checked for governance quality, the critique is correct and the name is the thing to change — a finding this lesson would rather surface than settle.

## Try it

Open Settings → Mandate and read the ESG-lite explanation, then write down, from memory, what you believe it excludes. Compare your answer with the three buckets in this lesson — fossil fuels within the Energy sector, tobacco and alcohol and gambling, and aerospace and defence. Then propose a simulated trade in a name outside the S&P 500 and read the verdict AMI returns: it should permit the trade and tell you the ticker is unclassified. That message is the feature working. If anything you read in the app contradicts what you just learned, that is worth reporting through Feedback — the app's description of a control is supposed to match the control.

## Takeaway

A control is only usable once you can state what it excludes, over what coverage, at what resolution, and what it says nothing about — and "it labels itself honestly" is a separate finding from "it covers enough."

*Where this comes from: the rating-divergence result in Berg, Kölbel & Rigobon (2022) and the SFDR distinction between promoting characteristics and pursuing a sustainable objective, applied to a product rather than to a fund.*
