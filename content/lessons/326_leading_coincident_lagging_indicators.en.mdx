# Leading, coincident & lagging indicators

No single economic number tells you where the economy is headed — you need a dashboard, and every gauge on it falls into one of three categories: leading indicators move *before* the economy turns (new orders, building permits, the yield curve you met in the asset-classes track), coincident indicators move *with* it in real time (payrolls, industrial production, personal income), and lagging indicators confirm a turn only *after* it's already happened (the unemployment rate, CPI). Reading macro well means knowing which category a headline number belongs to before you decide what it's telling you.

## Example

Take the ISM Manufacturing PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index) — a classic leading indicator, since it surveys purchasing managers about *new orders*, activity that will show up in production and hiring months later. A PMI reading above 50 signals expansion; below 50 signals contraction. In **November 2023**, the ISM Manufacturing PMI printed **46.7** (ISM) — its 13th consecutive month below the 50 threshold, a persistent contraction signal in the manufacturing sector specifically, even while broader US GDP was still growing (recall the Q3 2023 4.9% annualized print from two lessons ago). That divergence is a dashboard doing its job: a leading indicator (PMI) flashing weakness in one sector well before a lagging indicator (the unemployment rate, which stayed low through this stretch) would ever show it.

Compare that to a lagging indicator like the unemployment rate: by the time it's clearly rising, the labor-market weakening it reflects usually started months earlier. That's why the Fed and market participants lean so heavily on leading and coincident data — waiting for lagging confirmation means reacting to news that's already stale.

## The trap

The trap is reading every economic number as if it's telling you the same thing: "the economy right now." A trader who sees PMI at 46.7 and unemployment at 3.4% in the same news cycle and concludes "the data is contradictory, so it's unreadable" has missed that these are two different clocks — one running ahead, one running behind. The correct model: sort every macro number you read into leading, coincident, or lagging before you decide what weight to give it, and expect leading and lagging indicators to *disagree* at every turning point — that disagreement is the dashboard working, not a data error.

## The steelman

The strongest case against leaning too hard on leading indicators: they generate false positives — PMI, LEI, and similar gauges have flagged looming contractions that didn't fully materialize, and a reader who trades every leading-indicator wobble will get whipsawed. That's a legitimate caution. The falsifier: leading indicators earn credibility through *persistence and breadth*, not a single reading. One month of sub-50 PMI is noise; 13 consecutive months (as in the November 2023 example) across a broad survey of purchasing managers is a much harder signal to wave away — even if it hadn't yet shown up as a broad recession by that point. Weight a leading indicator by how long and how widely it's been signaling, not by its most recent print alone.
