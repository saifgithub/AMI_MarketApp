# Beta and CAPM — what beta is, and what it is not

Beta is the most quoted risk number in investing and one of the most misread: it does not measure how risky a holding is, it measures the part of its movement that the market explains. After this lesson you will be able to compute a beta from a return series, read it alongside the number that tells you whether it means anything, and say precisely which risks CAPM claims you are paid for — and which it says you are not.

## Example

Twelve months of openly hypothetical returns, printed in full so every figure below can be reproduced. No real instrument appears.

- **Market:** +2%, −1%, +3%, 0%, −2%, +4%, +1%, −3%, +2%, +1%, −1%, +2%
- **Steady:** +3%, −2%, +3%, +1%, −3%, +4%, 0%, −4%, +3%, +2%, −2%, +1%
- **Story:** +10%, −8%, −2%, +7%, −12%, 0%, +8%, +1%, +8%, −7%, +4%, −1%

Beta is a ratio of two quantities already computed in <Lesson id="371_diversification_is_correlation_not_count"/>: β = cov(asset, market) ÷ var(market). Running it:

| | beta | R² | monthly volatility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | 1.00 by definition | 1.00 | **2.10%** |
| Steady | **1.21** | **0.90** | **2.68%** |
| Story | **1.00** | **0.09** | **7.08%** |

Now put the two rows side by side. **Story has a beta of exactly 1.00** — by the textbook gloss, "moves with the market one-for-one" — and it is **3.4 times as volatile as the market**, with only **9%** of its variation explained by the market at all. Steady, with the higher beta of 1.21, is the far quieter holding.

Beta did not lie; it was asked one question and answered it. It measures the *slope* of an asset against the market — how much of the market's move typically passes through — and says nothing about how much else is going on. **R²** is the number that tells you how much else: at 0.90, Steady's movement is largely the market's movement, and its beta describes most of what it does. At 0.09, Story's beta describes about a tenth of what it does, and the other 91% is company-specific noise that a beta figure was never designed to see. **A beta quoted without an R² is a slope with no indication of whether anything lies near the line.**

CAPM (Sharpe, 1964) takes the next step and says which risk gets paid. Expected return = risk-free + β × (market premium). With a 4% risk-free rate and a 5% assumed market premium — both assumptions of this example, not forecasts — Steady's expected return is 4 + 1.21 × 5 = **10.05%**, and Story's is 4 + 1.00 × 5 = **9.00%**. Story carries 3.4 times the market's volatility and is offered *less* expected return than the quieter Steady, because CAPM pays only for the risk you cannot diversify away. Story's extra 91% is idiosyncratic — the kind <Lesson id="372_the_floor_diversification_cannot_cross"/> shows averaging out across a book — and a model built on diversified holders will not compensate anyone for carrying it.

## The trap

The trap is using beta as a single-stock risk gauge: "beta 0.7, so it's a defensive name; beta 1.8, so it's aggressive." It sounds like a risk ranking, and for holdings with high R² it very nearly is. But applied to a Story-type name it inverts: beta 1.00 reads as ordinary, and the holding swings three times as hard as the market on news that has nothing to do with the market.

The correct mental model: beta answers "if the market moves 1%, how much of that typically arrives here?" and nothing else. Total risk is volatility. The market-linked share of it is R². Beta is the exchange rate between the two, and quoting an exchange rate tells you nothing about the size of the transaction. There is also a second, quieter trap: CAPM's claim that idiosyncratic risk goes unpaid is conditional on being diversified. A holder with three positions has not diversified that risk away — they are carrying it and, under the model, being paid nothing for it, which is a considerably worse position than the beta figure suggests.

## The steelman

The strongest case against all of this: CAPM's central empirical prediction — higher beta, higher average return — has held up badly. The relationship measured in the data is far flatter than the model requires and has at times run the wrong way, which is a large part of what motivated the factor work in <Lesson id="379_factors_value_size_momentum_quality_low_vol"/>. A model whose main testable claim fails is a poor guide to what returns to expect. That is a fair reading of the evidence and it should change how the model is used, not whether. Two of its components survive the criticism intact because they are decompositions rather than predictions: splitting risk into a market-linked part and a specific part is arithmetic, and it holds whatever the reward for beta turns out to be. The falsifier is specific: if realised returns lined up neatly with beta across a broad sample, the model's pricing claim would be vindicated and the flat-line criticism would be the thing to abandon. They do not — so use beta to understand *what a holding is exposed to*, and treat any expected-return figure it produces as an assumption you have chosen, which is exactly how the 10.05% above was labelled.

## Try it

Open a holding in your simulated Portfolio and ask the Technical Strategist in a 1-on-1 for its beta, then ask a second question the answer will not volunteer: how much of this holding's movement is actually explained by the market? Judge whether the reply engages with that or restates the slope. Then look at how many positions you hold. If it is a handful of low-R² names, the risk you are carrying is mostly the kind CAPM says nobody pays you for — worth knowing before you size the next one. AMI's answers are training artifacts for you to evaluate, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Beta is the market's share of a holding's movement, not the size of it — read it with R², and never let a beta near 1.00 convince you a holding is ordinary.

*Where this comes from: Sharpe's Capital Asset Pricing Model, which prices only non-diversifiable risk — Capital Asset Prices (1964), building on Markowitz (1952).*
