# Brand moat: when a logo is pricing power

Lesson 036 said a moat is a structural cause backed by a financial fingerprint. Brand is the slipperiest of the moat categories — almost every company has a "brand", almost none have a brand moat. A brand moat exists when customers will pay a measurable premium for the same physical thing because of the name on it, and that premium shows up in gross margin year after year. By the end of this lesson you should be able to separate brand awareness (everyone knows the name) from brand power (people pay more for it), and the specific gross-margin pattern that confirms or kills the claim.

## How this plays out in real markets

Compare LULU (Lululemon) and NKE (Nike) against generic athletic apparel. LULU posted gross margins around 58% in fiscal 2024 selling stretch fabric that, at the input level, costs comparable to what every athleisure competitor sources. The 58% margin against the apparel-industry median of 35–40% is the brand moat made visible — customers pay $128 for leggings that cost meaningfully less to produce because the name on the waistband carries value to them. NKE runs gross margins in the 44–46% range, lower than LULU but still 10+ points above generic apparel, and has held that gap across two decades and multiple CEO changes. That durability is the real test.

Now look at brand recognition without brand power. Most US airlines (DAL, UAL, AAL) are household names with massive ad spend, yet operating margins float in the low single digits — when fuel rises or capacity adds, those margins disappear entirely. Everyone knows the logos; nobody pays a premium because of them. The brand is awareness, not pricing power.

LVMH (the European luxury group, LVMUY on US OTC) is the textbook brand moat: gross margins consistently above 65% across handbags, fragrance, and watches, sustained through three decades and multiple recessions. The structural cause is scarcity-by-design — they could make more bags, they choose not to — combined with a 150-year-old name that competitors cannot replicate by spending money.

On Bursa Malaysia: NESTLE (Nestle Malaysia) holds gross margins in the high-30s on packaged food, well above smaller local FMCG peers — a brand-and-distribution moat in a market where shelf space and trust compound.

The falsifiable claim: if a "brand moat" exists, the company sustains gross margin at least 8–10 percentage points above its industry median for five or more years. If the premium narrows to under 5 points and stays there for two consecutive years, the brand moat is eroding regardless of how famous the logo still is.

## The trap

The trap is mistaking advertising spend for a moat. A beginner sees a company spending $2B a year on marketing and assumes that builds a moat — but heavy advertising that doesn't translate to durable gross-margin premium is just an operating expense, not a moat. Pepsi and Coca-Cola both spend billions on ads; only one of them historically commands the gross-margin gap that signals true brand power, and even that gap has narrowed as private-label cola gained share. The correct mental model: a brand moat is what's left after you stop advertising for a year and customers still pay the premium. Most "brands" don't survive that test. Companies with real brand moats often spend less per dollar of revenue on advertising, not more, because the brand does the selling.

## Try it

Pick a brand you personally pay a premium for. Ask the Fundamentals Analyst in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "For [ticker], show me the last 10 years of gross margin alongside the industry median, and the ratio of marketing spend to revenue." In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write one sentence: "The brand premium is X percentage points, sustained for Y years, with marketing-to-revenue at Z%." If the premium has narrowed in the last three years while marketing-to-revenue rose, that's the warning signal — flag it in the journal as a watch item, not a thesis-killer yet.

## Takeaway

A famous logo is not a moat — a durable gross-margin premium sustained without outsized advertising is, and the day the premium starts narrowing is the day the brand stops being economic and starts being decorative.
