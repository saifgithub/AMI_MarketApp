# How rates price equities (the discount rate)

A stock's value is the present value of all the cash it will ever generate for you — and "present value" requires a discount rate to translate future dollars into today's dollars. That discount rate is built on the same interest-rate machinery you just learned for bonds: it starts at the risk-free Treasury yield and adds a premium for equity risk on top. When rates rise, every future dollar a company might earn gets worth less today, which is why a Fed rate decision can move the entire stock market on a day when not a single company's earnings actually changed. After this lesson you'll be able to explain why growth stocks react harder to rate moves than value stocks.

## Example

Recall the stock valuation framework from <Lesson id="037_valuation_what_is_a_stock_worth"/>: a stock's fair value is its expected future cash flows discounted back to today. The discount rate used is roughly the risk-free rate (the 10-year Treasury yield) plus an equity risk premium. When the 10-year Treasury yield rose sharply through 2022 as the Fed hiked aggressively, high-growth, long-duration names — companies like many in the Nasdaq's most speculative tier, priced heavily on cash flows expected a decade or more out — fell far harder than steady-cash-flow value names, even though many of those growth companies' near-term business fundamentals hadn't changed. The mechanism is the same duration intuition from <Lesson id="305_duration_and_convexity"/>: a stock whose value depends on distant future cash flows behaves like a long-duration, low-coupon bond — its price is highly sensitive to the discount rate. A mature, dividend-paying value stock behaves more like a short-duration bond — most of its value comes from cash flows arriving sooner, so it's less sensitive to the same rate move. This is why "growth stocks are rate-sensitive" isn't a vague market truism — it's the same duration math from M13 applied to equities instead of bonds.

## The trap

Beginners treat a stock's price drop during a rate-hike cycle as evidence the company is doing something wrong — "the business must be struggling." Often nothing about the business changed at all; only the discount rate did. The correct mental model: separate the numerator (the company's actual expected cash flows) from the denominator (the discount rate applied to them). A rate hike attacks the denominator across the entire market simultaneously, hitting long-duration growth names hardest — that's a valuation mechanics story, not a verdict on any single company's operations. Conflating the two leads to selling good businesses at exactly the wrong moment, or buying into "it's cheap now" without asking whether the discount rate justifies the new price.

## The steelman

A skeptic could argue: "This framework overstates how mechanically rates drive stock prices — plenty of stocks fell in 2022 for company-specific reasons (slowing growth, margin compression, competitive pressure), not purely because of the discount rate." That's true and important: rates are one input, not the only one. The falsifier: isolate the rate effect by comparing how similarly-profiled companies with genuinely unchanged near-term fundamentals moved during the same rate-hike window. If long-duration, high-multiple names across unrelated industries all compressed together while their actual near-term guidance stayed intact, that's the discount-rate mechanism at work independent of company-specific stories. Where a company's own guidance also deteriorated in the same period, both effects are stacking — and separating them is exactly the analytical discipline this lesson is teaching, not a reason to dismiss the rate effect.

## Takeaway

A rate move changes the discount rate applied to every future dollar a stock might earn — hitting long-duration growth names hardest — so before reading a rate-driven selloff as a verdict on the business, separate what actually changed in expected cash flows from what changed in the rate used to discount them.
