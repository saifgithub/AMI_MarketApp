# What moves stock prices?

A stock's price changes only when buyers and sellers disagree about what to pay. The interesting question is what makes them disagree. Almost every price move you'll ever see traces back to one of four drivers: company-specific earnings and news, broader macro events, crowd sentiment, and forced flows (money that has to buy or sell for non-opinion reasons). Knowing which driver is moving a price tells you whether to act on it or ignore it.

## How this plays out in real markets

On 21 February 2024, NVIDIA (NVDA) released a quarterly earnings report that beat Wall Street expectations on every line — revenue, profit margin, and forward guidance. The stock opened the next day up 16%, adding more than $270 billion in market capitalisation in a single session. That's driver one: **earnings**. The company reported numbers that forced everyone holding NVDA to re-rate what the business was worth.

A different example: in early August 2024, the Bank of Japan raised interest rates, the yen surged, and on 5 August the S&P 500 fell 3% in a day. No US-specific company news — purely **macro**. Investors expected an unwind of global leveraged positions and sold first. By the end of that week most of the losses were recovered.

Driver three is **sentiment**. In January 2021, GameStop (GME) ran from $20 to $483 in three weeks on social-media-driven coordinated buying. The underlying business hadn't changed. Crowd mood had.

Driver four is **forced flows** — index rebalances, fund redemptions, margin calls. When a stock is added to the S&P 500, every index fund must buy it, regardless of price. That mechanical demand can lift the stock 5-10% with zero new information.

On Bursa Malaysia: when Tenaga Nasional (TENAGA) announced higher tariffs in 2024, the stock moved on driver one — company earnings. When the ringgit weakened sharply against the dollar that same month, exporters like Top Glove (TOPGLOV) moved on driver two — macro.

## The trap

The trap is to react to every price move the same way. A 3% drop on no news is not the same event as a 3% drop after a guidance cut. New traders treat them identically — they panic, or they "buy the dip" — because they're reacting to the colour of the candle, not to what's actually driving the move. The right habit is to ask, every time: which of the four drivers caused this? If you can't name the driver, you don't yet have a reason to act. The News Analyst can tell you whether new information has hit the wire; the Market Analyst can tell you whether the move is technically consistent with the recent range or an outlier. Together they give you the context you need before you do anything.
