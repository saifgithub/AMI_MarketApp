# What is volatility?

Volatility is the size of a stock's typical price swings. A stock that drifts up and down 0.5% a day is low volatility. A stock that routinely swings 5% a day is high volatility. Volatility is not the same as risk of loss — a stock can be highly volatile and still trend up, or quiet and slowly bleed money. But volatility tells you how wide a normal day's move is, which sets your expectations and tells you what size position you can stomach without panicking.

## How this plays out in real markets

Compare two real names on the same day. Berkshire Hathaway Class B (BRK-B) typically moves about 1% on a normal trading day. Tesla (TSLA) typically moves about 3-4% on a normal trading day. On a wild day, BRK-B might move 3% — for TSLA, that's Tuesday. A 5% move in TSLA is normal; the same move in BRK-B would be the biggest day it's had in two years. Same dollar of capital, very different ride.

The S&P 500 has its own gauge: the **VIX index**, often called the "fear index." VIX measures the market's expected volatility over the next 30 days, derived from options prices on the S&P 500. A VIX reading below 15 means the market expects calm — typical daily moves under 1%. Between 15 and 25 is normal. Above 30 means traders are pricing in big swings — often during a crisis or a sharp selloff. In March 2020, as COVID hit, VIX touched 82 — its highest reading ever. In quiet periods like mid-2017, VIX sat near 10.

A simple rule of thumb: a stock's "normal daily move" is roughly its 30-day average daily move. If TSLA has averaged 3.2% daily moves for the past month, then a 3% move tomorrow is unremarkable. A 7% move is a real event worth investigating.

On Bursa Malaysia: AirAsia (CAPITALA) and Top Glove (TOPGLOV) have historically been high-volatility names — 4-6% daily swings during stressed periods. Maybank (MAYBANK) and Public Bank (PUBLIC) are low-volatility — usually under 1% a day. The KLCI as a whole is far less volatile than the S&P 500.

## The trap

The trap is treating volatility and danger as the same thing. They're not. A high-volatility stock is wider, not necessarily worse. If you size your position to its volatility — small position in a wild stock, larger in a quiet one — you can hold either comfortably. The mistake beginners make is buying a high-volatility stock at the same dollar size as a low-volatility one, then panicking the first time it moves 5% against them. That's not the stock being dangerous; that's you mis-sizing for the volatility. The other trap is the opposite — assuming a quiet stock can't hurt you. Low-volatility names can still trend down for months. Volatility tells you about width, not direction.

## Takeaway

Volatility is the width of a stock's normal daily swing, not its direction or its riskiness. Size your position to the stock's volatility, and a 5% day in a wild name feels the same as a 1% day in a quiet one.
