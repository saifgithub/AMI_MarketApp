# Risk parity for strategies — equal risk, not equal dollars

The diversification lesson (236) used equal-dollar weighting across strategies. That's a reasonable starting point but it leaks the diversification benefit when the strategies have very different volatilities. A momentum strategy with 18% annualised vol and a mean-reversion strategy with 8% annualised vol at equal dollars means momentum contributes ~70% of portfolio risk — the portfolio is effectively a momentum bet with a small mean-reversion overlay, not a diversified combo. Risk parity equalises the RISK contribution of each strategy by allocating inverse-volatility weights. After this lesson you can compute risk-parity weights, understand when they help, and recognise when risk parity introduces its own trap.

## How this plays out in real markets

Three strategies, annualised vols:
- Trend-following on SPY: 11%
- Mean-reversion on US large-caps: 8%
- Momentum top-decile S&P 500: 18%

Equal-dollar weights (33/33/33): portfolio vol contribution roughly 24% / 17% / 59%. Momentum dominates risk by a large margin even though it's only a third of dollar exposure. Equal-dollar diversification is mostly cosmetic.

Risk-parity weights (proportional to 1/vol):
- Trend: 1/11 = 0.091
- Mean-reversion: 1/8 = 0.125
- Momentum: 1/18 = 0.056

Normalise: trend ~33.5%, mean-reversion ~46%, momentum ~20.5%. Now each strategy contributes roughly 33% of portfolio risk. The portfolio is genuinely diversified across risk, not just across dollars.

Backtested 2010-2024: equal-dollar weights aggregate CAGR 10.4%, vol 12.8%, max drawdown 14%. Risk-parity weights aggregate CAGR 9.3%, vol 9.2%, max drawdown 9%. CAGR is slightly lower (because momentum had the highest individual CAGR and you're now under-weighting it), but vol and drawdown are meaningfully better. Sharpe roughly equal. The Q1 2020 drawdown: equal-dollar -11%, risk-parity -6%. The November 2020 momentum crash: equal-dollar -4%, risk-parity -2%.

Key caveat: vol estimates use a trailing window (e.g., 90 days). When volatility regimes shift, the weights lag the regime. In rising-vol regimes, the weights are too high (computed from prior low vol); in falling-vol regimes, weights are too low. Practical risk-parity uses a smoothed vol estimate (e.g., 90-day blended with 252-day) and rebalances monthly, not daily.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is treating risk parity as a magic formula. Risk parity gives you equal risk contribution from each strategy ASSUMING the volatility estimates are accurate and the correlations don't spike. When stress correlations spike toward +1 (covered in 238), risk parity stops working — every strategy is contributing the same risk because they're effectively the same strategy. The correct mental model: risk parity is one tool, useful when strategies are genuinely diversified and the dominant strategy is much more volatile than the others. It's NOT useful when stress correlation is high or when one strategy's expected return is dramatically higher than another (you're throwing away alpha by under-weighting it). The second trap: using risk parity on too few strategies. With two strategies and high stress correlation, risk parity gives you roughly half-and-half with no diversification benefit — the parity is mathematical, not behavioural. The third trap: rebalancing too frequently. Daily rebalancing of risk-parity weights generates substantial transaction costs that eat any benefit; monthly is the typical cadence.

## Takeaway

Risk parity equalises strategy-level risk contribution, not portfolio-level risk. It helps when strategies are genuinely diversified and one is dominant in vol; it stops helping when stress correlations spike.
