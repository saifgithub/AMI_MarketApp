# Combining signals: orthogonality and weights

Standardised signals can be added together, but adding them naively double-counts whatever they share. Two decisions remain: how much of each signal is genuinely new information, and how much weight each one earns. Both have a defensible answer, and both are easy to get wrong in the same direction.

## Example

Start with a signal that has already been standardised and cross-sectionally demeaned, per <Lesson id="400_standardising_a_signal_before_you_combine_it"/>.

**Ask what is already explained.** Suppose this signal correlates 0.8 with a momentum factor you already hold — one of the factors covered in <Lesson id="379_factors_value_size_momentum_quality_low_vol"/>. Squaring that correlation gives R² = **0.64**. So 64% of the signal's variation is movement you already own, and only **36%** is new.

The part that is left over after removing what a factor explains is called the **residual**. Working with the residual instead of the raw signal is *orthogonalisation*, and the reason for it is simple: adding a signal that is two-thirds momentum to a book that already holds momentum is buying the same exposure twice while believing you diversified.

**Then decide the weights.** Three hypothetical names, each with an identical residual signal strength of 0.6 — Beta's pointing the other way — but different volatilities:

| Name | Residual ÷ volatility | Raw weight |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | +0.6 ÷ 1 | **+0.6** |
| Beta | −0.6 ÷ 2 | **−0.3** |
| Gamma | +0.6 ÷ 2 | **+0.3** |

Dividing by volatility is inverse-volatility weighting, the same idea as in <Lesson id="380_risk_parity_and_risk_budgeting"/>: equal conviction in a noisier name earns a smaller position, because the position size that matters is the risk it contributes.

**Finally, normalise.** The absolute values sum to 0.6 + 0.3 + 0.3 = **1.2**. Dividing each by 1.2 gives **+50%**, **−25%** and **+25%**, and their absolute values now sum to exactly 1.

Note the absolute value. Beta's weight is negative and still consumes a quarter of the budget, because a position that pays when a name falls is still a position with risk in it. A budget that netted the long and short legs against each other would report this book as half-invested when it is fully committed.

## The trap

The trap is adding a fourth signal that correlates 0.9 with one already in the book and recording it as diversification. The count of signals went up, the number of distinct bets did not. At 0.9 correlation only 19% of the new signal is new, so roughly four-fifths of the position it earns is a top-up of a bet already held — taken while believing risk had been spread.

The correct habit: before a signal earns weight, measure what it shares with everything already in the book and keep only the residual. The test is uncomfortable on purpose, because a signal that looks strong in isolation frequently turns out to be a signal you already own wearing different notation. Counting signals measures your library. Counting residuals measures your bets.

## The steelman

The strongest case against all of this: every step adds a parameter fitted to history, and each one is a chance to overfit. The correlation used for orthogonalisation is itself estimated from a sample and will be different next year; the volatilities used for weighting are backward-looking; and a book weighted this way concentrates capital in whatever has recently been quiet, with no view on whether it is worth owning. Those are real objections, and <Lesson id="342_overfitting_and_curve_fitting"/> is the reason to take them seriously.

What survives is the ordering, not the precision. You do not need the correlation to be exactly 0.8 to know that a signal sharing most of its variation with one you hold is mostly not new. Fixed, simple weights — inverse volatility, or even equal weights — cannot overfit in the way an optimiser fitted to the same history can, which is a genuine argument for the crude version over the sophisticated one. The falsifier is available: run the book with the residual step and without it, on data neither version was built on, and see which holds up.

## Try it

Pick two holdings in your simulated Portfolio that you believe you hold for different reasons. Write down each one's last twelve monthly moves and estimate how closely they track each other. Square that estimate to see how much of the second holding is explained by the first, and ask yourself whether the remaining share justifies a separate position. Then put the same question to the Chief Investment Officer and grade its reasoning against your own arithmetic rather than the other way round — AMI's answers are training artifacts, never advice.

## Takeaway

A signal earns weight only for the part of it you do not already own, and the weight it earns shrinks with its volatility — count residuals rather than signals, and use absolute values when checking the budget, because a short position spends risk too.

*Where this comes from: the residual-after-factors decomposition introduced in Fama & French's multifactor work (1993) and the multifactor treatment in Bodie, Kane & Marcus, Investments.*
