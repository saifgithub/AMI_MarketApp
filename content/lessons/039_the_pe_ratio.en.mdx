# The P/E ratio

Price-to-earnings is the single most-quoted number in equity investing — and the single most-misused. The formula is trivial: P/E = price per share divided by earnings per share, or equivalently market cap divided by net income. What's hard is interpretation. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute a P/E, read it in the right context (sector, growth, debt structure), name the specific condition under which a "high" P/E is justifiable, and recognise the most common way the ratio lies to you.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at AAPL in mid-2026. Trailing-twelve-month EPS is roughly $7.10, the stock trades near $230, so P/E = 230 / 7.10 ≈ 32x. The S&P 500 trades around 22x. Is AAPL "expensive"? Depends on what you compare it to. Against the broad index, yes — 45% premium. Against megacap tech peers (MSFT ~38x, GOOGL ~25x, META ~27x), it sits mid-pack. Against AAPL's own ten-year history (range roughly 15x–35x), it's at the upper end.

Now NVDA. EPS around $3.20, price ~$135, P/E ≈ 42x. Higher than AAPL, but NVDA's earnings have grown more than 100% year-over-year through the AI capex cycle. A useful framing is the PEG ratio — P/E divided by expected earnings-growth rate. NVDA at 42x with 50%+ forward growth gives a PEG well under 1.0; AAPL at 32x with single-digit growth gives a PEG above 4.0. By PEG, AAPL looks more expensive than NVDA despite the lower headline multiple. That's the point: P/E in isolation tells you nothing about whether you're overpaying.

Falsifiable claim for this lesson: a P/E above 40 is justifiable only if forward earnings growth is expected to exceed roughly 25% annually for at least three years. Below that growth rate, the multiple compresses toward the sector median, and the price has to fall (or earnings have to grow into the multiple) to close the gap. On Bursa Malaysia: TENAGA has historically traded around 12–15x earnings — a utility multiple consistent with stable but low single-digit earnings growth. A sudden re-rate to 25x would require a fundamental change in growth profile, not just sentiment.

## The trap

The trap is comparing P/E across sectors and concluding one is "cheaper" than the other. Banks trade at low P/Es (JPM ~12x, MAYBANK ~10x) because they carry massive leverage and earn returns on equity that depend on credit cycles. Software companies trade at high P/Es because earnings compound on near-zero marginal cost. Comparing JPM's 12x to MSFT's 38x and concluding "JPM is three times cheaper" is meaningless — they're different businesses with different earning quality. The correct mental model: P/E is a *within-sector* ranking tool first, a *vs.-own-history* tool second, and a cross-sector tool almost never. What invalidates the signal: one-off accounting items (a big writedown, a tax-law change, a sale of a division) can compress or inflate trailing EPS for a single year, making the trailing P/E meaningless. Always check forward P/E and normalised earnings before trusting the number.

Next time you debate whether the price is "good", you'll have a within-sector, within-history anchor instead of a single headline number.

## Takeaway

P/E is a within-sector, within-history ranking tool — never a standalone verdict. A high multiple is only justified by growth or earnings quality; a low multiple is only a discount if earnings aren't declining.
