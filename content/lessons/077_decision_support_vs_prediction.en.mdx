# Decision-support vs prediction

<Term id="ami" /> is a decision-support <Term id="training_simulator" />, not a prediction system. The distinction matters: a prediction system claims to know what a price *will* be; a decision-support training environment structures your reasoning *practice* about what to do given what you know now. AMI's simulated <Term id="ami_verdict" />s describe current state, current setup, and conditional probabilities — never point forecasts, and never as investment advice. The Research Manager's job is to synthesise the bull/bear debate into a *position*, not a *prophecy* — and your job is to evaluate that synthesis. By the end of this lesson you will be able to spot the difference in any output AMI gives you.

## How this plays out in real markets

You ask the Research Manager in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: *"Where will GOOGL be in 30 days?"*

> *Research Manager:* Wrong shape of question for me. What I can give you is a current-state synthesis. GOOGL trades $182, Q4 cloud revenue grew 30% YoY, ad revenue grew 11%, forward P/E 22, sector rotating into mega-cap tech, sentiment neutral-positive. The Bull case (AI infra monetisation, search resilience) and the Bear case (regulatory overhang, ad cyclicality) are roughly balanced — Bull probability ~0.55 in our debate. That doesn't tell you what GOOGL closes at in 30 days. It tells you that, conditional on the current setup holding, the asymmetry favours a long-side position at modest size — and what would invalidate that view: a break below $172, a cloud revenue miss, or DOJ remedies materially harsher than consensus.

Notice what the Research Manager actually did: described state, weighted competing cases, expressed a conditional probability, and named the invalidation conditions. That's decision-support. Nowhere is there a sentence like *"GOOGL will be $195 on day 30"*. The same agent asked the same question by a prediction-mode user might be tempted to anchor on a number — but AMI's prompts are written to refuse that frame, and the verdict shape (entry, stop, target, horizon, confidence, invalidation) carries no point-forecast slot.

A second example: the Trader proposes *entry $182, stop $172, target $198, horizon 6 weeks, confidence 0.58*. That is four conditional statements bundled together — *if the entry triggers, here is the risk you're agreeing to and the reward you're hoping for, with a falsification level*. It is not a claim that the stock will reach $198. The confidence 0.58 is the Room's weighted judgment, not a calibrated probability — read it as "lean, not lock".

## The trap

The trap is reading a Verdict as a prediction because it contains numbers. Numbers feel like forecasts. A target of $198 reads, in the wrong frame, as "AMI thinks GOOGL will be $198". It doesn't. The target is the level at which the Trader *would* lock in profit *if the thesis plays out inside the scenario*. The stop is the level at which the thesis is wrong. The horizon is the window over which the trade is meant to express. None of these are predictions; all of them are conditional scaffolding for the training scenario. The correct frame: AMI is staging a structured evaluation exercise — entry/stop/target/horizon/confidence are training-scenario constructs, not advice to act on. The price forecast is the part AMI deliberately does not ship.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the Research Manager, paste any ticker on your <Term id="ami_watchlist" /> and ask: *"Predict the price 30 days out."* Read the reply carefully. Then ask: *"What's the current setup, what's the bull/bear balance, and what would invalidate the read?"* Compare the two replies side by side. The first deflects to a range; the second answers in full. That gap is the whole lesson.

## Takeaway

AMI describes process and probabilities, names invalidation, and stops there. Point-price predictions are the thing it deliberately does not sell — and that absence is the feature. AMI is a training simulator. The Verdict is a practice artifact for evaluation, not investment advice.
