# The 60-minute rule after a loss

The next trade after a closed loss is, statistically, your worst trade. The 60-minute rule — no new entries in the first 60 minutes after a stop-out — is the simplest process change that materially changes a trader's distribution of outcomes. It is not a <Term id="ami_mandate" /> parameter — AMI does not have a field for it and does not block an order during a cooldown window. Building on <Lesson id="047_revenge_trading"/>, this lesson explains *why* 60 minutes and not 5 or 240, gives you the falsifiable test for whether your cooldown is real or a suggestion, and shows you how to write the rule into your own process — tracked in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, checked in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Risk Officer — since the trigger stays in your hand at the exact moment your hand is least trustworthy, and only your own discipline pulls it back.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader's journal, reviewed across 14 months, showed 184 stop-outs. He grouped them by "next entry timing": entries within 0–60 minutes of the stop, 60–240 minutes, and after the next session open. The win rates were 31%, 47%, and 54% respectively. The average size of the next entry was 1.7x his risk_score-derived cap in the 0–60 bucket, 1.1x in the middle bucket, and 1.0x in the next-session bucket. Most striking: the 0–60 bucket was where 78% of his mandate-breach trades (the ones where size also blew through the enforced cap) lived. The same trader on the same strategy generated genuinely different expected value depending only on *when* he placed the next trade after a loss.

He wrote himself a rule the following Monday — no new entries for 60 minutes after a stop-out — and started logging every stop-out plus his next-entry timing in the Decision Journal. Six months later, the 0–60 bucket of his next-trade behaviour was empty — not because AMI blocked those entries (it can't; there's no enforcement mechanism for this), but because the visible, reviewed journal made every near-miss expensive to ignore, and he built the habit of checking the clock before the ticket. Mandate-breach trades (the size cap AMI *does* enforce) fell 71%. Realised drawdown distribution narrowed materially. The strategy did not change; the timing of his weakest hour did.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that the cooldown is a punishment. It is not. The cooldown is a circuit breaker — it separates the trader who *was just stopped out* from the trader who is *about to size the next position*. In the first sixty minutes after a loss, three measurable things are different about you: your urge to size up to "make it back" is elevated, your patience for waiting for a real setup is depleted, and your conviction is correlated to the loss rather than to the setup. None of these are character flaws — they are the predictable response of any trader to a recent loss. The 60-minute rule does not require you to be a different kind of trader. It requires you to be the *same* trader an hour from now, and to let the same-trader-an-hour-from-now make the decision instead of the just-stopped-out version.

## Try it

Write down your own cooldown length — 60 minutes, or 90 if you want a stricter floor (AMI has no field for this; you're the one holding it). Then open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, filter to your last 20 stop-outs, and tag each according to "next entry timing" — within 60 minutes, 60–240 minutes, or next session. Compute the win rate of each bucket. Take the screen to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Risk Officer and ask: "What does my own data say my cooldown duration should be?" The empirical answer is almost always longer than you would have guessed.

## Takeaway

The cooldown is not a punishment — it is the rule that lets the same-trader-an-hour-from-now make the decision instead of the just-stopped-out version. AMI will hold your position size to your risk_score; the clock is yours to write down once and enforce on yourself absolutely.
