# Capstone: stress-testing a strategy claim

You now hold the full M21 toolkit: what a backtest can and can't prove, how too many parameters relative to trade count curve-fits to noise, why only untouched out-of-sample data is an honest test, how Monte Carlo simulation reveals the tail risk one historical path might hide, and how survivorship bias inflates returns when a backtest's universe quietly excludes what failed. This capstone runs one plausible-looking vendor claim through all five checks at once — the discipline of stress-testing any strategy claim before trusting it.

## One scenario, every M21 concept

A vendor claims a strategy backtested on S&P 500 constituents from 2015-2024 (300 trades) shows a **68% win rate**, averaging **+$220** on wins and **-$150** on losses, using **14** tunable filters. First, the backtesting check: in-sample EV = (0.68 × $220) + (0.32 × -$150) = $149.60 - $48 = **+$101.60 per trade** — a real number, describing only this exact historical data. Second, the overfitting check: 300 trades ÷ 14 parameters = **≈21.4 trades per parameter** — thin, well below the 50-per-parameter ratio the module's simpler example used, meaning a meaningful share of that +$101.60 could be curve-fit to this specific history rather than a repeatable pattern.

Third, the out-of-sample check: walk the same unmodified rule forward onto 2025 data it wasn't tuned on, and the win rate drops to **45%**: EV = (0.45 × $220) + (0.55 × -$150) = $99 - $82.50 = **+$16.50 per trade** — a decay of ($101.60 - $16.50) ÷ $101.60 ≈ **84%** from the in-sample figure. Fourth, the Monte Carlo check: even taking that surviving +$16.50 EV at face value, with a 55% per-trade loss probability, the odds of five consecutive losses = 0.55⁵ ≈ **5.03%** — about 1 in 20 five-trade stretches produces a losing streak that size, a real and sizeable tail risk sitting underneath a still-positive average. Fifth, the survivorship check: the backtest's universe was *today's* S&P 500 constituents — meaning any company that was delisted, went bankrupt, or was removed from the index between 2015 and 2024 never appears in the tested data at all, which (per the survivorship-bias lesson) means even the surviving +$16.50 out-of-sample figure is probably still overstated relative to what an investor choosing among the full 2015 universe would actually have experienced.

Five checks, one claim: a headline 68% win rate and +$101.60 in-sample EV shrinks to a thin, survivorship-inflated, tail-risk-bearing +$16.50 once actually stress-tested — still possibly a real, if modest, edge, but nothing like the number the vendor led with.

## The steelman

The strongest case against this five-check gauntlet: "No claim can survive this much scrutiny — you could sink any strategy, however genuine, by demanding perfect trades-per-parameter ratios, undecayed out-of-sample performance, zero tail risk, and a perfectly survivorship-free universe all at once." That's a fair caution against an impossible standard. The falsifier: the gauntlet isn't a pass/fail gate demanding perfection on all five — it's a discipline for sizing conviction to what actually survives. This scenario's strategy didn't fail every check; it kept a positive, if much smaller, out-of-sample EV even after all five stress tests. The right conclusion isn't "reject every strategy that isn't flawless" — it's "size your conviction and your position to the +$16.50 that survived stress-testing, not the +$101.60 headline that didn't."

## Try it

Take a strategy claim you've encountered — a vendor pitch, a screener result, or a Room verdict — and run it through all five M21 checks by hand: trades-per-parameter, an out-of-sample split, a Monte Carlo losing-streak estimate, and a check on whether its universe excludes delisted or failed names. Log what survives in your Decision Journal. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

A strategy claim's headline win rate is the least-tested number in the pitch — run it through the trades-per-parameter check, an untouched out-of-sample test, a Monte Carlo tail-risk estimate, and a survivorship check before sizing any conviction on it at all.
