# Covered call & protective put

Options aren't only standalone bets — combined with shares you already hold, they become structures for reshaping risk. A covered call sells away some of your upside in exchange for income collected today. A protective put pays a cost today in exchange for a defined floor under your downside. Neither eliminates risk; each trades one kind of risk for another, on purpose. After this lesson you'll be able to explain what each structure gives up and what it buys.

## Example

Consider a trader holding 100 shares of a stock, illustratively priced near $205, who sells (writes) a call against those shares: a $210-strike short call, collecting a $4.00 premium per share upfront. This is a covered call — "covered" because the trader already owns the shares that would need to be delivered if the option is exercised. If the stock is at $215 at expiry, the short call's intrinsic value is $5.00 — meaning the shares get called away at $210, capping the trader's upside there, but the $4.00 premium collected upfront is kept regardless of what happens next. The trade converts unlimited upside above $210 into fixed income plus a capped gain.

The protective put runs the opposite trade: a trader holding shares near $195 buys a $190-strike put for a $5.00 premium, paying for a floor under their position. The break-even for that put leg alone is $185.00 (strike minus premium) — below $185, the put's payoff keeps growing, offsetting further share losses. If the stock falls to $170, the protective put's payoff (intrinsic value) is $20.00 per share — $190 strike minus $170 — and net of the $5.00 premium paid, that's a $15.00 benefit: without the put, that decline would hit the shares uncushioned; with it, the loss is capped near the strike, minus what was paid for the insurance. The put itself cost $5.00 upfront, the same way a fixed insurance premium is paid whether or not the claim is ever used.

## The trap

Beginners think a covered call is close to free money — harvest the premium, no real cost. They miss that the cost is the capped upside, which can be a large opportunity loss if the stock rallies hard past the strike. The mirror mistake: treating a protective put as free insurance. It isn't — the premium is a guaranteed cost that drags on returns in every scenario where the crash never happens, the same way home insurance costs money every year the house doesn't burn down. Both structures have a real, upfront price; the trap is noticing the benefit and forgetting to price the trade-off.

## The steelman

The strongest case against routinely selling covered calls: doing it repeatedly means systematically capping your best-performing positions right as they run — you get called away on your winners and keep riding your losers, the opposite of what a disciplined process should do. The falsifier is measurable in a portfolio log: track how often shares get called away during your biggest winning stretches versus your losing ones. If covered-call writing is quietly amputating your winners, the pattern shows up as a lower average gain on called-away positions than on the ones you kept fully.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal and find a position you're tracking, or pick a watchlist ticker. Ask yourself: would you rather cap this position's upside for income today (covered call) or pay for a floor under it (protective put)? Both structures are simulated in AMI Trade, but only once you turn on derivatives in your mandate — until you do, AMI will not propose or open one, and this stays a thought exercise in trade-off reasoning. That default is deliberate: decide the trade-off on paper first.

## Takeaway

A covered call sells away your ceiling for income today; a protective put buys you a floor for a cost today — both are structures for shaping risk, not ways to eliminate it.

*Where this comes from: the CBOE's standard equity-option hedging structures — the covered call and protective put are the two textbook ways to combine stock with a single option.*
