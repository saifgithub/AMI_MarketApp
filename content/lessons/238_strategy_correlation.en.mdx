# Strategy correlation — measuring it honestly

The diversification lesson (236) said the benefit comes from low cross-strategy correlation. This lesson is how you actually measure it, because the correlation number you compute is misleading in two specific ways: it averages across regimes (hiding the times when correlations spike to +0.9), and it depends heavily on the time-window length you choose. After this lesson you can compute strategy correlation across multiple rolling windows, identify "stress correlation" (the correlation that emerges in crisis regimes), and size your strategy combination using stress correlation — not average correlation — because that's what determines drawdown in the regime that matters.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take trend-following on SPY and a long-only momentum top-decile strategy on the S&P 500 over 2010-2024.

Average correlation of monthly returns: +0.34. Looks decent — not perfectly diversified but well below +1.0. Now segment.

- **Low-vol regime (VIX < 18)**: correlation +0.21. Diversified.
- **Mid-vol regime (VIX 18-26)**: correlation +0.41. Still diversified.
- **High-vol regime (VIX > 26)**: correlation +0.78. Crisis.
- **Worst 10 months by SPY drawdown**: correlation +0.86. Effectively the same strategy.

In Q1 2020, both strategies lost simultaneously — trend-following was whipsawed by the speed of the drop (the 50/200 didn't have time to flip before the crash), and momentum took a 14% loss as factor leadership rotated. The "average +0.34 correlation" implied a meaningful diversification benefit; the actual experience was correlation near 1 in the regime where the diversification was supposed to matter most.

Honest measurement: compute correlation in three buckets — full sample, bottom-decile of market returns ("stress"), and top-decile of market returns. Use the stress correlation for sizing. A pair of strategies with average correlation +0.3 but stress correlation +0.85 should be sized as if it were a single strategy in your risk model, because that's how it will behave when you most need diversification. The pair with average +0.3 AND stress +0.4 is actually diversified.

## The trap

The beginner mistake is using the full-sample correlation as if it were a constant. Correlations are regime-dependent, and they spike toward +1 in crisis — which is the regime where diversification is supposed to save you. The correct mental model: "stress correlation" is the only correlation that matters for risk management. Average correlation tells you about typical days; stress correlation tells you about the day you'll lose the most. The second trap is computing correlation on the wrong frequency. Daily correlations are noisy and over-state diversification (intraday noise washes out); weekly or monthly correlations are more reliable. The third trap is using a recent short window (e.g., last 12 months) — if the window doesn't contain a stress regime, your stress-correlation estimate is unmeasurable. Use the longest window that includes at least one crisis episode (2008, 2020, 2022 at minimum).

## Takeaway

Average correlation flatters; stress correlation reveals. Size your portfolio for the correlation that emerges in the regime where you'll lose the most, not the regime where everything is calm.
