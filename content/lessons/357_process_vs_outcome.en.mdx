# Process vs outcome

A decision and its outcome are two different objects, and markets are noisy enough that they come apart routinely. A well-sized position with a written thesis can lose money; a reckless one can pay. Poker's name for scoring the first by the second is *resulting* — the term Annie Duke reports from the table and built a book around — and it is the most common error people make reviewing their own record. This lesson gives you the alternative: grade a decision on what was knowable at the moment it was made — thesis, size, invalidation — and treat the outcome as one noisy sample about it.

## Example

Two decisions from one quarter in a $100,000 practice portfolio. Both are invented, deliberately — no real ticker, no real prices.

**Decision A.** A hypothetical mid-cap industrial. You write a thesis and an invalidation before entering, size it at 2% ($2,000), and set a stop 15% below entry, capping the loss at $300 — 0.3% of the portfolio. Treat it as a repeatable setup with a hypothetical 45% hit rate paying 3R on winners and 1R on losers, where R = $300: 0.45 × $900 + 0.55 × (−$300) = **+$240 expected per repetition**. This time an announcement nobody had breaks the thesis, price trades down through the level and the stop fills. Realised: **−$300**.

**Decision B.** A hypothetical speculative small-cap, entered on a forum tip. No written thesis, no invalidation, no stop, sized at 25% ($25,000). Assume the honest odds on that kind of setup are a 40% chance of +40% and a 60% chance of −40%: 0.4 × $10,000 + 0.6 × (−$10,000) = **−$2,000 expected per repetition**. This time it runs +40%. Realised: **+$10,000**.

The outcome column ranks B ahead by $10,300. The process column ranks A ahead by $2,240 per repetition. Only one of those columns tells you what to do again next quarter — and it is not the one your account balance shows you.

## The trap

Resulting runs in both directions, and beginners mostly notice the painful one: a loss lands, the process gets torn up, and a positive-expectancy setup is abandoned after four bad samples. The costlier direction is the pleasant one. Decision B paid $10,000, so 25% position sizes on forum tips get filed as validated, and the next one is $30,000. Nothing in that outcome touched the fact that the setup was worth −$2,000 a repetition. The correct mental model: outcomes update your estimate of the odds slowly, across many samples; they do not grade the decision. Grade the decision from what was on the page when you made it. That is the CEO-seat job in <Lesson id="279_you_are_the_ceo"/>, pointed backwards at your own record.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal, pick a closed entry, and cover the P&L before you read anything else. From the thesis and invalidation you wrote at the time, grade the decision 1–5 and write the number down. Then uncover the result and grade the same decision again, now knowing how it ended. The gap between the two grades is one reading of your resulting habit — one entry is an anecdote, so do it across several before you believe the size of it. The blind grade is the honest one; the second is what your memory would have handed you at review. Neither direction should move the grade: a decision that ended badly is not thereby worse, and one that paid is not thereby better — and it is the second, pleasant direction that slips past you. Then ask the Research Manager how it would score the same entry from the pre-outcome text alone — its answer is a training artifact for you to evaluate, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Grade the decision by what was knowable when you made it; the outcome is one noisy sample, not the report card. A good decision that lost money is still the one to repeat.
