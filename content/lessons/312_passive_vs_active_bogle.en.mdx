# Passive vs active — the Bogle case

John Bogle's argument for indexing isn't "active managers are bad at their jobs" — it's arithmetic. Before costs, the average actively managed dollar and the average passively managed dollar in the same market must earn the *same* gross return, because together they are the market. After costs, the passive dollar wins on average, because it pays less to get there. After this lesson you'll be able to state the case precisely enough to know what would actually prove it wrong.

## Example

Every year, S&P Dow Jones publishes the SPIVA scorecard, comparing actively managed funds against their benchmark indices. The consistent finding across two decades of data: the majority of actively managed US large-cap funds underperform the S&P 500 in a typical year, and the underperformance rate rises the longer the measurement window — over 15-to-20-year horizons it has repeatedly run well above 80%. This isn't a claim that no manager ever beats the market in a given year; some do, every year. It's a claim about the odds, compounding: a fund that beats its benchmark 6 years out of 10 but trails badly in the other 4 can still lag over the full decade, especially once its higher expense ratio (see the prior lesson's $13,844.08 fee-drag example) is subtracted every single year, win or lose.

The mechanism is Bogle's "cost matters hypothesis": pre-cost, active and passive management are a zero-sum split of the market's return, because active managers collectively hold something close to the market portfolio. Post-cost, passive wins the average, because trading costs, research costs, and management fees are a direct subtraction that indexing mostly avoids.

## The trap

Beginners hear "most active funds underperform" and conclude "therefore no active manager is worth paying for," which overshoots the evidence. It feels justified because the SPIVA numbers are so lopsided. The correct mental model: the claim is about the *average*, not every individual manager, and it says nothing about whether you personally can identify the above-average manager *in advance* — which is a much harder problem than noticing, after the fact, who won.

## The steelman

The strongest case for active management: markets aren't perfectly efficient everywhere. In less-covered corners — small-cap stocks, emerging markets, distressed debt, illiquid situations — fewer analysts are pricing the security, so skilled research has more room to find a mispricing before it closes. Some categories of active funds (certain small-cap and fixed-income strategies) show meaningfully better relative SPIVA results than large-cap US equity active funds do. Passive investing also has a structural dependency problem: it works because enough active participants are still doing the price-discovery work that makes an index's prices meaningful in the first place — if everyone indexed, someone would need to still be analyzing companies for stock prices to reflect anything real.

The falsifier for "active adds value in inefficient corners": track a specific active fund's post-fee, risk-adjusted return against its actual benchmark (not a broad index it doesn't resemble) over a full market cycle, not a hot 2-year stretch. If the outperformance survives fees and full cycles, the case holds for that fund; if it evaporates once both are applied, the "inefficient corner" argument was really a "got lucky in one regime" story wearing a stronger costume.

## Takeaway

The case for indexing isn't that active managers are unskilled — it's that skill, even where real, has to clear a cost hurdle every single year to show up in your account.

*Where this comes from: Bogle's cost matters hypothesis — Common Sense on Mutual Funds (1999) — and the SPIVA scorecard methodology it predicted decades before the data existed to test it.*
