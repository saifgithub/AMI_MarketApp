# Operating, investing, and financing cash flow

Lesson 035 introduced free cash flow as the corrective to earnings. This lesson opens the cash flow statement and reads each of its three sections separately. Cash flow from *operations* (CFO) shows whether the business itself generates cash. Cash flow from *investing* (CFI) shows what the company is spending on long-term assets — capex, acquisitions, divestitures. Cash flow from *financing* (CFF) shows how the capital structure is moving — debt issued or repaid, equity issued or bought back, dividends paid. The *pattern* across the three sections is one of the most diagnostic single screens in fundamental analysis. By the end of this lesson you should be able to read the three-section signature of a healthy mature company, a growing company, and a struggling company at a glance.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at three patterns side by side:

**1. Healthy mature company — AAPL fiscal 2024.** CFO roughly +$118B. CFI roughly +$2B (modest net investment after offsetting marketable-securities movements). CFF roughly –$122B (debt repaid, dividends paid, and significant buybacks). The signature: strong operating cash inflow, modest net investing, large financing outflow returning cash to shareholders. This is what a cash-generative mature business looks like — the operating section funds everything.

**2. Healthy growing company — let's use a hypothetical software firm at scale.** CFO +$2B. CFI –$1.5B (heavy capex on data centres and a small acquisition). CFF +$0.5B (modest debt issued to fund part of the expansion). Total cash position roughly flat. The signature: operating cash flow positive, investing cash flow significantly negative (the company is reinvesting), financing cash flow modestly positive or near zero. The business is funding most of its own growth.

**3. Struggling company — illustrative.** CFO –$200M (the operating business is burning cash). CFI –$50M (modest investment). CFF +$300M (heavy debt issuance and equity raises to plug the gap). Total cash position roughly flat. The signature: operating cash flow negative, financing cash flow heavily positive to compensate. This is the textbook capital-raise treadmill — the business cannot fund itself, so it issues debt or equity to bridge each period.

The pattern matters more than the levels. A company where CFO funds CFI with surplus left over for CFF (returning capital) is mature and healthy. A company where CFO partially funds CFI and CFF tops up is growing within its means. A company where CFO is negative and CFF is providing all the cash is on the capital-raise treadmill — sustainable only as long as capital markets remain open.

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK's cash flow profile reflects its bank business model (where the cash flow statement is harder to interpret for financials — concentrate on net interest income and capital ratios instead). TENAGA's signature is closer to a utility: strong CFO, large CFI (capex on transmission and generation), and CFF that ranges from positive (debt issuance for capex) to negative (debt repayment and dividends) across the regulatory cycle.

The falsifiable claim: a company with negative CFO and positive CFF in any single year is in a temporary capital-raise mode that the next 2–3 years must reverse. Persistently negative CFO funded by financing for 3+ consecutive years (without a clear cyclical-trough or heavy-growth-capex justification) is the signature that historically precedes equity dilution at distressed multiples or distressed debt restructuring.

## The trap

The trap is looking only at the total change in cash position. A beginner sees "cash balance up $50M" and concludes the company is fine. They miss that the $50M came from $400M of debt issuance offsetting $350M of operating cash burn. The total cash position is a derived number; the *components* are the story. The correct mental model: read CFO first (did the business generate cash?), then CFI (did it spend that cash productively?), then CFF (where did the rest come from or go to?). Three healthy patterns exist — mature returning capital, growing reinvesting, and turnaround paying down debt — and three unhealthy ones (operating cash burn funded by financing, financing-funded capex with no underlying cash generation, asset sales funding shortfalls). The patterns are visible at a glance once you know what to look for.

## Takeaway

Cash flow has three sections, and the pattern across them tells you what kind of company you're looking at. The total change in cash is the derived number; the three components are the diagnosis.
