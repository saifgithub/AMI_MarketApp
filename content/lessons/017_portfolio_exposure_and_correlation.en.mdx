# Portfolio exposure and correlation

Per-trade risk is only half the picture. If you hold five "different" positions that all move together when the market sneezes, your real risk is not 5 × 1% — it is closer to 1 × 5%. Correlation eats diversification when you most need it. This lesson teaches you to count independent bets, not tickers.

## How this plays out in real markets

Suppose you hold five positions, each sized at 1% account risk: NVDA, AMD, MSFT, GOOGL, META. On paper you have $5,000 of risk on a $100,000 account — 5%. In reality, in a sharp tech sell-off these five names regularly post correlations of 0.7 to 0.9 to each other.

On a single risk-off Tuesday in Q1 2025, the Nasdaq-100 dropped 3.4% intraday and every one of those five names fell between 4% and 7%. Your "diversified" five-position book lost roughly 4.5% in a single session — closer to one big tech bet than five independent ones.

Compare to a book of NVDA + JPM + XOM + KO + IHH on Bursa Malaysia (semiconductor + bank + energy + staples + healthcare). The same Nasdaq-down-3.4% day, JPM closed flat, XOM was up 1.1%, KO down 0.4%, IHH up 0.2%. Independent factors absorbed the tech shock. Same five 1% positions, very different real-world exposure.

Falsifiable test: if your top 3 holdings have moved together (same sign, similar magnitude) on more than 70% of red days in the last quarter, you do not hold three positions — you hold one position three times.

AMI's safety floor enforces your single-name position cap and account drawdown limit automatically; single-factor exposure across correlated names is not (yet) something the app tracks for you — the Chief Investment Officer will walk through it in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> if you ask, but noticing the cluster is on you.

## The trap

The trap is counting positions instead of counting bets. A beginner with NVDA, AMD, and TSM thinks they own three stocks; the PM sees one bet on the semiconductor cycle, sized at 3% account risk plus whatever sector beta amplification kicks in during a sell-off.

The correct mental model: ask "if my central thesis on these is wrong, do all of them get hit at once?" If yes, treat the cluster as one position for sizing purposes — if your mandate caps single-trade risk at 1%, the cluster aggregate caps at 1%, not 3%.

The other half of the trap is the reverse: avoiding correlated bets so aggressively that you end up holding 15 micro positions you cannot track. Three to seven genuinely independent bets is the working range.

## Try it

Pick a single-factor exposure ceiling for yourself — 5% is a reasonable starting point — and write it down as a personal rule (AMI doesn't have a field for this yet).

Then in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, look at your current open positions and ask the Chief Investment Officer in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "Across these positions, what is my real single-factor exposure?" If the PM flags a cluster you didn't notice, you've just learned the difference between position count and bet count.

## Takeaway

Count bets, not tickers — if your top holdings all bleed on the same day, you own one position in different costumes.
