# Portfolio exposure and correlation

Per-trade risk is only half the picture. If you hold five "different" positions that all move together when the market sneezes, your real risk is not 5 × 1% — it is closer to 1 × 5%. Correlation eats diversification when you most need it. This lesson teaches you to think in terms of total open risk, sector clusters, and the difference between holding many tickers and holding many independent bets. The PM tracks this for you in the background and will block trades that push a single factor exposure past your mandate ceiling.

## How this plays out in real markets

Suppose you hold five positions, each sized at 1% account risk: NVDA, AMD, MSFT, GOOGL, META. On paper you have $5,000 of risk on a $100,000 account — 5%. In reality, in a sharp tech sell-off these five names regularly post correlations of 0.7 to 0.9 to each other (they move down together when rates jump, when a chip headline breaks, when the Nasdaq has a bad day). On a single risk-off Tuesday in Q1 2025, the Nasdaq-100 dropped 3.4% intraday and every one of those five names fell between 4% and 7%. Your "diversified" five-position book lost roughly 4.5% in a single session — closer to one big tech bet than five independent ones.

Compare to a book of NVDA + JPM + XOM + KO + IHH on Bursa Malaysia (semiconductor + bank + energy + staples + healthcare). The same Nasdaq-down-3.4% day, JPM closed flat, XOM was up 1.1%, KO down 0.4%, IHH up 0.2%. The cluster of independent factors absorbed the tech shock. Same five 1% positions, very different real-world exposure. Falsifiable test: if your top 3 holdings have moved together (same sign, similar magnitude) on more than 70% of red days in the last quarter, you do not hold three positions — you hold one position three times.

## The trap

The trap is counting positions instead of counting bets. A beginner with NVDA, AMD, and TSM thinks they own three stocks; the PM sees one bet on the semiconductor cycle, sized at 3% account risk plus whatever sector beta amplification kicks in during a sell-off. The correct mental model: ask "if my central thesis on these is wrong, do all of them get hit at once?" If yes, treat the cluster as one position for sizing purposes — meaning if your mandate caps single-trade risk at 1%, the cluster aggregate caps at 1%, not 3%. The other half of the trap is the reverse: avoiding correlated bets so aggressively that you end up holding 15 micro positions you cannot track. Three to seven genuinely independent bets is the working range.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_mandate" /> editor and set `max_single_factor_exposure_pct` to 5. Then in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, look at your current open positions and ask the Portfolio Manager in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "Across these positions, what is my real single-factor exposure?" If the PM flags a cluster you didn't notice, you've just learned the difference between position count and bet count.

## Takeaway

Count bets, not tickers — if your top holdings all bleed on the same day, you own one position in different costumes.
