# Profit and margins

Profit is what's left of revenue after costs. Margins are profit expressed as a percentage of revenue — they tell you how much of every dollar of sales the company actually keeps. There are three margins worth knowing: gross (after direct cost of goods), operating (after running the business), and net (after everything including tax and interest). By the end of this lesson you should know what each margin reveals, why the same revenue figure can hide very different businesses, and the precise margin behaviour that flips a "growth story" into a "value trap".

## Example

Look at AAPL and AMZN in fiscal 2024. AAPL reported roughly $391B in revenue with a gross margin around 46%, an operating margin around 31%, and a net margin around 26%. That means for every dollar of iPhone or Mac sold, about 46 cents survived direct manufacturing cost, 31 cents survived running the company, and 26 cents reached shareholders after tax. AMZN reported about $638B in revenue — bigger top line — but with a gross margin around 49%, an operating margin around 11%, and a net margin around 9%. AMZN earns more cents on the dollar at the *gross* line because much of its revenue mix is high-margin AWS and ads, but its retail and logistics costs eat most of that back at the operating line. Same "good company" label; very different shape of profit.

Compare to TSLA in fiscal 2024: revenue around $97.7B, gross margin around 18%, operating margin around 7%. A 1-percentage-point swing in gross margin moves operating profit meaningfully because there isn't much cushion. That's why TSLA's stock reacts so violently to gross-margin headlines — a slip from 18% to 15% is a 17% relative collapse in the gross-profit dollar.

On Bursa Malaysia: PETRONAS Chemicals has historically run gross margins in the 25%–35% range depending on the crude cycle. When oil rallies and feedstock costs rise faster than product prices, margins compress — and the whole business looks worse despite stable revenue. The income statement looks fine at the top; the damage shows up in the middle.

## The trap

The trap is comparing margins across industries as if they were on the same scale. A beginner sees a software company at 30% net margin and a grocer at 2% net margin and concludes the grocer is "bad". They're not — grocers run on inventory turnover; software runs on per-unit licence economics. The two will never converge. The other version of the trap: ignoring margin direction. A company with shrinking gross margins quarter-over-quarter is telling you costs are rising faster than prices, or that customers are pushing back on price. That trend almost always shows up in margins before it shows up in revenue. The correct mental model: compare a company's margins to its own history and to its direct peers, never across sectors. And watch the *direction* — declining margins on rising revenue is one of the most reliable early warnings on the income statement.

## Takeaway

Margins tell you what kind of business it is and which way the income statement is leaning — compare to the company's own history and its peers, and let the direction speak louder than the headline.
