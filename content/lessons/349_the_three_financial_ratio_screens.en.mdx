# The three financial-ratio screens

A company that clears the business-activity screen still has to clear three quantitative ratios, each checked against a cap under the AAOIFI-style methodology: interest-bearing debt as a percent of market cap (cap 33%), cash plus interest-bearing securities as a percent of market cap (cap 33%), and non-compliant (interest) income as a percent of total revenue (cap 5%). All three must pass — failing any one fails the whole screen. AMI computes these three ratios exactly, via the same screening logic that runs behind the halal Mandate flag, never by hand. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read a real balance sheet through all three ratios and know why a company can pass the business screen and still fail here.

## Example

Take a hypothetical balance sheet built like a heavily-levered real-economy company — think of the debt load a legacy telecom carrier such as **AT&T (T)** has historically carried: interest-bearing debt of **$138,000M**, market cap of **$128,000M**, cash plus interest-bearing securities of **$4,200M**, non-compliant (interest) income of **$900M**, and total revenue of **$122,000M**. Run these through AMI's screening function (`sharia_screen`) and the exact output is: **debt ratio 107.8%** (cap 33% — **fails**), **liquidity ratio 3.3%** (cap 33% — **passes**), **income ratio 0.7%** (cap 5% — **passes**). Overall: **fails**, on the debt ratio alone, even though the other two ratios are comfortably clean. This is the realistic shape of a real-economy-business failure — not a casino, not a brewery, just ordinary telecom-scale leverage that happens to exceed a third of market cap. Compare that to the capstone lesson later in this module, where a lightly-levered tech balance sheet clears all three comfortably.

## The trap

Beginners assume that once a company clears the business-activity screen (<Lesson id="348_the_business_activity_screen"/>), it's "basically halal" — treating the ratio screen as a formality. The AT&T-shaped example above shows why that's wrong: a telecom carrier has an entirely permissible core business and still fails outright on leverage alone. The correct mental model: the ratio screen is not a rubber stamp on companies that already passed the qualitative check — it independently disqualifies genuinely permissible businesses that have simply borrowed too much relative to their market value. Debt level, not business type, is the more common reason an otherwise-ordinary company fails the overall screen.

## The steelman

A skeptic might argue: "A 33% debt-to-market-cap cap is arbitrary — why not 25% or 40%? Market cap itself swings daily on sentiment, so a stock can flip compliant-to-non-compliant on a price move alone, with the company's actual balance sheet unchanged." That's a fair critique, and it's exactly why <Lesson id="350_standards_differ_why_the_same_stock_flips"/> covers standards disagreement — AAOIFI, Bursa SAC, and the major index providers don't all use identical thresholds or the identical denominator (some use trailing 12-month average market cap instead of a single-day snapshot, precisely to dampen this volatility). The falsifier: if the 33% line were purely arbitrary noise, every standard-setter would have converged on wildly different numbers; instead, AAOIFI, Dow Jones Islamic, S&P Shariah, and MSCI Islamic all cluster near a third — evidence the threshold reflects a real judgment about "how much of the enterprise's value is effectively financed by interest-bearing debt," not an arbitrary round number.

## Takeaway

Three independent ratios — debt/market-cap, liquidity/market-cap, and income/revenue — each capped and each computed exactly; failing any one fails the whole screen, business activity notwithstanding. These are computed exactly as a real compliance analyst would, applying a published methodology that is taught here for training, not issued as a religious determination.

*Where this comes from: AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 21's financial-ratio screening thresholds, mirrored in the Dow Jones Islamic Market Index methodology's 33%/33%/5% criteria.*
