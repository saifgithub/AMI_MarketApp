# Shiller CAPE — normalising P/E across a cycle

<Lesson id="039_the_pe_ratio"/> framed P/E against sector and own-history; <Lesson id="177_pe_in_cyclicals_peak_earnings_trap"/> showed how cyclical peaks distort the denominator. The Shiller CAPE — cyclically adjusted P/E, sometimes called P/E10 — is the standard fix: replace trailing-twelve-month EPS with the ten-year inflation-adjusted average. The arithmetic is simple; the discipline is rare. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute CAPE conceptually, know what its current level signals about long-run return expectations, and recognise the specific reason CAPE has been a poor short-term timing tool despite being a useful long-run anchor.

## How this plays out in real markets

The US market in mid-2026 trades on a trailing P/E around 22x. The Shiller CAPE for the S&P 500 sits near 32x. The gap is informative: trailing P/E is benefiting from a recent run of high profit margins; CAPE smooths the last decade of EPS and adjusts for inflation, so it shows the multiple you're paying for a normalised stream of earnings. The long-run CAPE average since 1900 is roughly 17x. A reading of 32x is in the top decile historically — comparable to the late 1990s tech-boom levels and well above 2007 peaks.

What does that mean for a buyer today? Long-run academic work — most famously Robert Shiller's — shows that starting CAPE has been a reasonable predictor of 10-year forward annualised real returns. Top-decile starting CAPE (>30x) has historically been followed by real annualised returns near 0–3% over the next ten years; bottom-decile starting CAPE (<10x) has been followed by 8–12% real annualised returns. So a buyer today, at a 32x CAPE, should plan around modest long-run returns from beta alone — alpha has to come from selection, sizing, and timing the cycle.

Falsifiable claim: CAPE is a useful 10-year forecasting tool, not a 1-year timing tool. The CAPE was already above 25x in late 1996; the US market doubled before the 2000 peak. A trader who shorted on "elevated CAPE" in 1996 was destroyed before being right. On Bursa: the KLCI typically trades at a lower CAPE than the S&P (mid-teens to high-teens) because its index composition is more cyclical and dividend-heavy; a 20x CAPE on KLCI would be an outlier worth investigating, not a routine reading.

## The trap

The trap is treating CAPE as a market-timing signal. CAPE can stay elevated for years — the late-1990s episode lasted four years above 30x before the bust, and the 2017–2022 episode lasted five years above 25x with strong forward returns. The correct mental model: CAPE sets a posterior on long-run beta returns, not a timing call. Use it to *size* your expectations — at 32x CAPE, plan for 3–5% real from index exposure over a decade, not 8% — and let valuation inform your asset-allocation choices rather than your entry timing. What invalidates the signal: structural shifts in earnings quality (higher sustainable margins from a higher-margin sector mix, e.g. tech share of S&P market cap rising from 10% to 30%) can justify a permanently higher CAPE baseline. Critics argue today's 32x is closer to a historical 24x once you adjust for sector mix and accounting changes. The honest read is that CAPE is informative but the long-run mean itself may have drifted.

## Takeaway

CAPE is a long-run expectation-setter, not a market-timing tool. Use it to size your return assumptions; use trailing and forward P/E to evaluate individual names.
