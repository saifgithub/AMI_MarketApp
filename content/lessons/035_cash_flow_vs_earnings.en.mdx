# Cash flow vs earnings

Earnings (net income) and cash flow look similar but measure different things. Earnings follow accounting rules — revenue can be recognised before cash arrives, and expenses can be spread across years. Cash flow tracks actual money moving in and out of bank accounts. Over a long enough horizon the two converge, but in any single year they can diverge meaningfully — and the gap is one of the most diagnostic numbers on the entire financial statement. By the end of this lesson you should know what free cash flow is, why it matters more than earnings to a long-term investor, and the exact multi-year pattern that flags accounting earnings as suspect.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at MSFT in fiscal 2024. MSFT reported net income of roughly $88.1B and operating cash flow of about $118.5B. After ~$44.5B in capital expenditure (data centres, AI infrastructure), free cash flow (FCF) came in around $74B. Three numbers, three slightly different stories: earnings ($88B) showed accounting profit; operating cash flow ($118B) showed how much actual cash the business produced; FCF ($74B) showed what was left after the cost of keeping the business running and growing. The fact that operating cash flow exceeded net income is normal and healthy — depreciation (a non-cash expense) reduces net income but not cash, so cash flow is typically higher.

Now look at the reverse pattern. Several high-growth companies in the 2021–2022 cycle reported positive accounting earnings while burning cash. When a company posts net income of, say, +$300M while FCF runs at –$800M for the same year, the gap is paying for itself in some combination of: receivables (revenue booked but not yet collected), inventory build-up, capitalised software costs (treated as assets, not expenses), or stock-based compensation that flatters the cash line but dilutes shareholders. None of those are crimes on their own; in combination, persistently, they are a warning.

On Bursa Malaysia: SIME Darby Plantation has historically reported earnings that swing with palm-oil price recognition timing, while its cash flow follows the actual harvest and shipment cycle. A trader watching only quarterly EPS misses the underlying cash rhythm of the business.

The falsifiable claim: if FCF stays meaningfully below reported net income for 3+ consecutive years without a clear capex-cycle explanation, the earnings figure is suspect and warrants forensic scrutiny.

## The trap

The trap is treating reported earnings — the EPS headline — as the truth about a business. A beginner sees "Q3 EPS beat by $0.05" and moves on. Earnings are an accounting construct, and several legitimate accounting choices (revenue recognition timing, capitalisation policies, working-capital management) can shift the headline meaningfully without changing the cash reality. The correct mental model: earnings tell you what management's accountants say the business made; cash flow tells you what hit the bank account. When the two move together, life is simple. When they diverge persistently and in the same direction, ask why — and don't let an earnings beat from a non-cash adjustment carry the same weight as one backed by genuine cash conversion.

## Takeaway

Earnings tell you what the accountants say; cash flow tells you what the bank account did. When the two disagree for years in a row, trust the bank account.
