# How to actually file a report with regulators

Module 11 has taught the patterns. This lesson teaches the action that follows: the concrete portals and procedures for filing with the regulators and law-enforcement bodies that have jurisdiction over investment fraud. It also covers what regulators can and cannot do — so expectations are calibrated — and why private naming-and-shaming on social media is risky for the victim. Reports filed early, with documentation, are the inputs that build the public alert lists that protect the next victim.

## How this plays out in real markets

Each regulator publishes its own complaint channel. The reader should bookmark the relevant ones for their jurisdiction:

- **US securities and investment fraud:** SEC Tips, Complaints, and Referrals — sec.gov/tcr. FINRA Investor Complaint Center — finra.org/investors. CFTC complaint portal for futures and FX — cftc.gov.
- **US cyber / wire fraud:** FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) — ic3.gov. State attorney-general offices accept parallel complaints.
- **UK:** FCA reporting — fca.org.uk/consumers/report-scam-unauthorised-firm. Action Fraud for criminal reporting — actionfraud.police.uk.
- **Australia:** ASIC reportascam pages — asic.gov.au. Scamwatch run by the ACCC — scamwatch.gov.au.
- **Singapore:** MAS investor-alert and complaint channels — mas.gov.sg.
- **Malaysia:** SC Malaysia complaints — sc.com.my. BNM TELELINK and Financial Consumer Alert reporting — bnm.gov.my. Police Commercial Crime Investigation Department for criminal reporting.
- **Hong Kong:** SFC complaints — sfc.hk.

What regulators *can* do: open an investigation, freeze assets they have legal access to, publish warnings adding the operator to the public alert list, refer the case for criminal prosecution, coordinate with foreign counterparts. What they generally *cannot* do unilaterally: recover funds that have already moved offshore through layered crypto wallets or shell companies, prosecute operators in jurisdictions without extradition treaties, or guarantee restitution. The published alert list itself is one of their most effective tools — it interrupts future victim recruitment.

The filing itself, in practice, requires:

- The operator's full name, any aliases, the platform URL, and the registration claims they made.
- The dates, amounts, and methods of any payments — bank transfer details, crypto wallet addresses, transaction hashes.
- Screenshots of the chat history, the platform dashboard, all written communications.
- A clear chronological narrative of what happened.

File in your home jurisdiction first; file in the operator's claimed jurisdiction second if it differs.

## The trap

The trap is twofold. First, expectation mismatch: the victim files expecting personal restitution and is disappointed when the regulator responds with a warning-list entry rather than a cheque. The reframe is that the filing is a public-good action that protects others, and is also a prerequisite for any subsequent civil action. Second, the public-naming temptation: posting the operator's identity on social media or in community groups feels cathartic, but creates legal exposure (defamation in some jurisdictions) and tips the operator off before regulators can act. The correct path is regulator-first, then any media engagement only on the regulator's confirmed alert-list entry, which is a public record and safe to amplify.

## Takeaway

File reports with regulators and police in your home jurisdiction first, then in parallel with foreign authorities relevant to the operator. Expect a public alert-list entry as the most common short-term outcome; treat any unsolicited "recovery service" that arrives after filing as the second wave.
