# Keeping a decision journal

An un-journalled decision cannot be honestly reviewed, because the version you remember is not the version you made. Once the outcome exists, memory quietly rewrites the thesis to fit it — you recall being more certain than you were, or having anticipated the thing that actually happened. The fix is mechanical rather than psychological: write the thesis, the confidence, and the invalidation condition *before* the outcome exists. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to write an entry that survives its own outcome uncorrupted.

## Example

Take a hypothetical mid-cap industrial — no real ticker, because what matters here is the shape of the entry, not the company. You open the position at $40, sized at 4% of a simulated portfolio, and before anything resolves you write three lines:

- **Thesis:** gross margin expands as the new plant comes online; the market is still pricing the old cost base.
- **Confidence:** 60%.
- **Invalidation:** two consecutive quarters of negative free cash flow, or a weekly close below $34.

Twelve weeks later it trades at $48 — up $8, or 20% on the position, which is 0.8% at portfolio level. Comfortable. But the entry is still sitting there, and it says margin expansion. Margins contracted. The move came from a takeover rumour that the thesis never mentioned.

Without those three lines, the honest reconstruction is simply unavailable: you would remember a 20% winner and file the thesis as confirmed. With them, the review is unambiguous — the stated reason failed, the outcome was good, and those are two separate facts about the same trade. The invalidation line is doing work too: at $34 you exit on a rule written when you had nothing yet to defend, which is the only moment such a rule is cheap to write.

## The trap

The trap is journalling after the fact — opening the entry when the position closes, or "remembering" the thesis at review time. It feels equivalent; you were there, you know what you thought. You don't. Hindsight bias is not forgetting, it's a silent edit: the outcome becomes part of the recalled reasoning, and recalled reasoning is always better calibrated than the real thing was. A retrospective entry is a story about a result, not a record of a decision.

The correct mental model is that the entry is evidence, and evidence collected after the verdict is worthless. Write it while you can still be wrong — that is the entire mechanism. Anything written later is commentary.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal and pick a position that hasn't resolved yet. Add three lines before it does: the thesis in one sentence, confidence as a number, and the specific condition that would make you wrong. Then read it against the trail described in <Lesson id="274_the_safety_floors_audit_trail"/> — the PM records what the rules did; only you can record what you believed. AMI is a training simulator, so the entry is practice material, not advice.

## Takeaway

Write the thesis, the confidence, and the invalidation before the outcome exists — afterwards, your memory is a witness that has already read the verdict.
