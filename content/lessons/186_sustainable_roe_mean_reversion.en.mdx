# Sustainable ROE — the mean-reversion limit

<Lesson id="041_return_on_equity"/> talked about "sustainable" high ROE driven by margin and turnover; this lesson nails down what sustainability actually means in numbers. Empirically, very high ROEs revert. The cross-sectional distribution of US corporate ROE has a long-run median around 11–13% and a wide tail of "extraordinary" businesses earning 25%+ for many years. But the *base rate* is mean reversion — a company earning 30% ROE today has, on long-run averages, a meaningfully lower probability of earning 30% in ten years than the market often assumes. By the end you should be able to read an ROE in the context of its peer distribution, recognise which moats actually defer reversion, and name the specific signals of reversion that show up before the headline number breaks.

## How this plays out in real markets

The classic study — Bartov, Fama, Nissim and others — shows that the cross-sectional ROE distribution mean-reverts over five-to-ten-year windows. Companies in the top decile of ROE (above ~30%) tend to drift down toward the 18–22% range over a decade; companies in the bottom decile (negative or low single digits) tend to drift up toward the 8–12% range, often through industry consolidation or exit. The persistent outliers — the ones that hold 25%+ ROE for fifteen-plus years — share specific features: brand pricing power, network effects, switching costs, scale advantages, or regulatory moats.

Concrete examples:
- **AAPL** has run ROE above 40% for over a decade (now ~150% on buyback-shrunk equity, but underlying operating ROIC remained 40%+). The moat: brand, ecosystem lock-in, supply-chain scale. This is the rare durable outlier.
- **NFLX** ran ROE around 25%+ in 2018–2020, dropped to ~15% in 2022–2023 as streaming competition intensified, recovered as Netflix tightened. The moat exists but isn't as deep as AAPL's; reversion happened partially.
- **A randomly-picked top-decile ROE name from 2010** had, on average, dropped 8–12 ROE points by 2020 — not zero, but meaningful compression. The single number to internalise: high ROE *reverts unless explicitly defended by a moat*.

The market often prices high ROE as if it will persist forever; the empirical base rate says it will compress. A 30%-ROE business priced at a high multiple has a built-in expectation of moat durability that, statistically, most don't deliver. On Bursa: PUBLIC BANK has held 13–14% ROE for over a decade — at the upper end of sustainable for Asian banks. Names that briefly hit 20%+ ROE on a cycle reverted within 3–5 years to sector medians.

Falsifiable claim: an ROE above 25% in a non-financial business is sustainable for more than ten years only when paired with at least one of: (a) brand pricing power demonstrated through cycle (price increases stick during inflation, price holds during recession), (b) network effects with measurable user-base growth, (c) high switching costs visible in net retention or customer tenure, (d) scale-driven cost advantage at least 20% below the nearest competitor. Absent all four, the base case is reversion toward the sector median over a decade.

## The trap

The trap is the "wide moat" narrative without measurable evidence. A bull writeup says "company X has a wonderful brand and pricing power that will sustain 30% ROE for decades." The honest test is: has the brand survived an inflation episode without volume losses? Has the company raised prices in real terms over the last decade? Does net retention exceed 100%? Have competitors who entered failed? If the answer to any of these is no, the "wonderful moat" is a story, and the base-rate reversion will start showing up in the next 3–7 years. The correct mental model: treat every high-ROE business as reversion-bound by default, and require explicit, measurable moat evidence to override the prior. What invalidates a reversion thesis: when a company's high ROE has *already* survived a major competitive challenge — a deep recession, a new entrant with deep pockets, a regulatory shock — and held, the base-rate prior weakens. AAPL has survived such challenges; many "wonderful businesses" haven't yet been tested.

## Takeaway

High ROE reverts by default; only measurable moat evidence buys persistence. Pay for the ROE the company has proven through a cycle, not the ROE the bull case extrapolates.
