# Trailing vs forward P/E — and the analyst-estimate trap

Lesson 039 introduced P/E as a within-sector, within-history ranking tool. This lesson sharpens the tool. There are two P/Es printed on every quote page — trailing (price / last-twelve-months EPS) and forward (price / next-twelve-months consensus EPS) — and they disagree most when it matters most. By the end you should be able to choose the right one for the situation, recognise when the forward number is a story analysts are telling rather than a fact, and name the precise condition under which the forward P/E is a trap.

## How this plays out in real markets

NVDA in mid-2026 trades near $135. Trailing EPS is $3.20, so trailing P/E ≈ 42x. Consensus forward EPS — the analyst-mean estimate for the next four quarters — is $4.80. Forward P/E ≈ 28x. The headline 42x is the past; the 28x assumes 50% earnings growth over the next year. Both are facts about the price, but only one of them is also a fact about the company.

Now contrast with INTC during a typical down-cycle quarter. Stock at $32, trailing EPS at $0.40 (a depressed-cycle number), trailing P/E ≈ 80x — looks brutally expensive. Consensus forward EPS at $1.80 as analysts model a recovery, forward P/E ≈ 18x — looks cheap. Same stock, same day, two stories. The truth depends on whether the forward $1.80 actually materialises. Through the 2022–2024 PC slump analyst forward EPS for INTC was cut roughly six times in eighteen months; each cut moved the "cheap" forward P/E higher after the fact.

Falsifiable claim: forward P/E is more useful than trailing only when (a) the consensus estimate range is tight (top vs bottom analyst within ~15%) AND (b) revisions over the last 90 days are flat or up. Wide ranges or a falling revision trend mean the forward number is a moving target — use trailing P/E plus a normalised earnings figure instead. On Bursa: TENAGA forward P/E often sits 10–15% below trailing because analysts model tariff resets that may not arrive on schedule; readers who anchor on the forward number get an artificially cheap-looking utility.

## The trap

The trap is treating consensus forward EPS as data when it is, in practice, a forecast that systematically drifts. Analysts anchor on company guidance; companies guide to beatable numbers; numbers get revised through the year as reality intrudes. A forward P/E built on a forecast that hasn't been tested yet is a price divided by hope. The dangerous version is the "cheap on forward" screen: a stock looks expensive on trailing P/E because last year was weak, then looks cheap on forward because analysts model a snap-back. Buyers treat the forward number as a fact and find out two quarters later that estimates were cut 30%, the price followed, and the "cheap" multiple was never real. The correct mental model: when trailing and forward P/E diverge by more than ~25%, you're not looking at a valuation — you're looking at a forecast. Read the assumptions before you trust the multiple. What invalidates the signal: a negative 90-day revision trend on consensus EPS. If estimates are being cut into the print, the forward P/E in your screener is already stale.

## Takeaway

Forward P/E is only a valuation when the underlying estimate is stable. If revisions are moving or the analyst range is wide, the forward multiple is a forecast wearing the costume of a fact — fall back to trailing or normalised EPS.
