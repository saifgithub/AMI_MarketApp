# Net debt / EBITDA — the credit-cycle ratio

Lesson 042 introduced debt-to-equity as the headline leverage gauge — and flagged that the ratio can hide debt structure. Net debt / EBITDA is the ratio credit analysts and rating agencies actually use, because it answers a more useful question: how many years of operating cash earnings would it take to repay the debt stack? Formula: (total debt minus cash and equivalents) divided by trailing EBITDA. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute it, know the rough thresholds rating agencies use, and name the precise gap between this number and the cash-flow reality it tries to approximate.

## How this plays out in real markets

AAPL in fiscal 2024 had roughly $100B in total debt, $65B in cash and marketable securities, and trailing EBITDA near $135B. Net debt = 100 − 65 = $35B. Net debt / EBITDA = 35 / 135 ≈ 0.3x. That's a number a rating agency calls "fortress balance sheet" — under one year of operating cash to clear the entire debt stack. AAPL retains its AA+ rating largely on this single ratio.

Compare a more levered name. T (AT&T) in 2024 carried roughly $140B in total debt against $7B in cash, with EBITDA around $42B. Net debt = 133, divided by 42 = 3.2x. That's near the upper end of investment-grade — rating agencies typically treat 2.5–3.5x as the borderline above which a non-cyclical name drifts toward BBB-/BB+. T's bond spreads and equity multiple both reflect this.

The classic stress example: a cyclical industrial entering a downturn with net debt / EBITDA at 3.0x sees EBITDA fall 30% in a recession year — ratio jumps to 4.3x without any new borrowing. The covenant on the bonds may trigger at 4.0x. Suddenly the company is in technical default, forced to renegotiate or issue equity at a depressed price. The ratio looked safe at the cycle peak; the cycle did the damage, not new debt.

On Bursa Malaysia: TENAGA, the regulated utility, runs net debt / EBITDA in the 3.5–4.5x range — high for a non-financial but normal for regulated utilities because cash flow is predictable. PETRONAS-linked names see the ratio swing wildly with Brent, sometimes from 1.0x to 3.0x in a single year on no operating change — pure commodity passthrough.

Falsifiable claim: for a non-cyclical, non-utility business, net debt / EBITDA sustained above 4.0x is a credit-risk warning regardless of D/E or interest coverage. The signal invalidates if the company is in a regulated industry with mandated returns (utility, tower, pipeline) where the higher number is structurally normal — but even then, watch for the cycle.

## The trap

The trap is reading EBITDA as if it were cash. EBITDA strips out depreciation and amortisation, which are non-cash charges — but the underlying capex they represent is real and recurring. A company with net debt / EBITDA of 3.0x and capex equal to 80% of EBITDA actually has very little free cash to service debt. The correct mental model: net debt / EBITDA is a screen, not a verdict. The follow-up question is always net debt / (EBITDA − capex), which is closer to net debt / free cash flow. What invalidates the headline: a company growing through capex-heavy expansion will look fine on net debt / EBITDA and terrible on net debt / FCF — the gap is the tell that EBITDA is overstating cash generation.

## Takeaway

Net debt / EBITDA is the screen credit analysts use; net debt / (EBITDA − capex) is the truth. The first looks at operating earnings; the second looks at the cash that's actually available to repay debt after the business has been reinvested.
