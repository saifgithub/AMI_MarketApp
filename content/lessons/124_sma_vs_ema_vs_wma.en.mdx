# SMA vs EMA vs WMA — when each matters

Lesson 026 introduced the simple moving average and named the EMA in passing. The three common variants — Simple (SMA), Exponential (EMA), and Weighted (WMA) — all compute an average of the last N bars, but they distribute weight differently. SMA gives every bar equal weight. WMA gives the most recent bar the highest linear weight, then steps down. EMA applies an exponential decay so the newest bar dominates and older bars fade smoothly. Same lookback period; three different speeds. Pick the one whose lag matches the timeframe you trade — and know the tradeoff before you switch.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at QQQ from October 2025 through January 2026. Using a 20-period lookback on the daily chart:

- The SMA20 turned bullish on October 28 at 487 — three full sessions after the local low.
- The WMA20 turned bullish on October 24 at 484 — one session after the low.
- The EMA20 turned on October 23 at 483 — coincident with the low bar's close.

EMA caught the turn earliest. Same chart, different lags. But on November 14, when QQQ printed a one-day -2.8% shakeout to 495, all three averages held above price by the next session — except the EMA20, which flipped to declining for two bars before re-flattening. The EMA's responsiveness cuts both ways: faster turns, more whips.

The trade-off is mechanical. EMA(N) gives roughly half the lag of SMA(N) but produces about 30–40% more direction changes across a typical six-month sample, which translates into more whipsaw entries if you trade off the cross. WMA sits between the two on both axes.

On Bursa Malaysia: PUBLIC BANK during 2025 — SMA50 crossed bullish on March 17, EMA50 on March 11. The six-day head start was real, but EMA50 also gave one false flip in mid-April that SMA50 ignored entirely.

## The trap

The trap is assuming "faster is better." Beginners read that EMA reacts quicker and switch every MA on their chart to exponential, then watch their cross signals double in frequency and their win rate collapse. The opposite mistake is just as common — defaulting to SMA on a 5-minute scalping chart where a 30-bar lag is the entire trade.

The correct mental model: match the MA's lag to the holding period of the trade. For multi-week swings off the daily chart, SMA's slower response filters noise you don't care about. For shorter-term momentum entries — daily breakouts you'll exit in a week — EMA's earlier turn is worth the extra whips. WMA is the compromise when you want some recency emphasis without exponential overshoot. The choice is a tuning parameter, not a preference.

SMA, EMA, and WMA trade lag for whipsaws on a sliding scale — match the variant to your holding period, not to whichever one feels faster.
