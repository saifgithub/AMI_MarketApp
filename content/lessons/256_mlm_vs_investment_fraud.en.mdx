# MLM vs investment fraud: where the line is

Multi-level marketing is legal in most jurisdictions. Investment fraud — including pyramid schemes and Ponzis — is illegal in all of them. The two structures look superficially similar because both rely on recruitment, but the line between them is sharp and falsifiable: where does the money actually come from? In a legitimate MLM, retail customers outside the network buy a real product at a market price. In a pyramid or Ponzi, the only meaningful inflow is new recruits' deposits. This lesson teaches the three questions that separate the two — and one warning: MLMs can drift across the line as they age.

## How this plays out in real markets

The US FTC and SEC have published joint guidance on the legal test, summarised on ftc.gov and sec.gov. The structure that fails the test usually carries one or more of these traits:

- The "product" exists but is priced far above any retail equivalent — a USD 600 monthly "nutrition pack" that retails for USD 80 of equivalent goods.
- The bulk of distributor income comes from recruiting new distributors, not from outside-the-network product sales.
- Distributors are required to purchase "starter kits" or monthly inventory in dollar amounts that exceed plausible retail sales.
- Income disclosures (where regulators force their publication) show 95%+ of distributors earning negative net income after costs.

The SEC's Investor.gov publishes case studies of operations that began as plausible MLMs and were later prosecuted as pyramid or investment-fraud schemes. The trigger is almost always the same: the operator quietly removes the retail product or rebrands the "membership" as an "investment" promising a return. SC Malaysia and BNM have both flagged schemes that crossed this line — wellness or e-commerce branding wrapping a deposit-and-yield product.

The legal test isn't about the wrapper, it is about the cash flow. The FTC summarises it as: if every distributor disappeared except those who never sold to a non-distributor, would there still be a real business? In a real MLM, yes — products are bought by people outside the network. In a pyramid, no.

## The trap

The trap is the brand-name fallacy: "Brand X is publicly traded and decades old, so this related opportunity must be legitimate." Recognisable MLM brands are legal entities — but local franchises, splinter groups, or "next-generation" offerings under a similar name may not be. The reader is shown the parent brand's public listing and skips the question that matters: are the products in *this specific opportunity* being bought by retail customers outside the recruitment chain? The fix is to ask for the income disclosure and look at the median distributor's net income — not the gross sales figures the recruiter quotes.

## Takeaway

The legal MLM test is cash-flow, not branding: real revenue from real customers outside the recruitment chain. A deposit-for-monthly-return product bolted onto an MLM is an unlicensed investment scheme, regardless of the wellness or e-commerce wrapper.
