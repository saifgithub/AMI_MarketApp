# Standardising a signal before you combine it

Two signals measured on different scales cannot simply be added together. Before anything can be combined, each one has to be put on a common footing — and then compared against its peers on the same day. This lesson walks the four operations that do that, and shows why cross-sectional demeaning changes the claim you are making.

## Example

Three openly hypothetical names. Each has a four-month history of monthly gains, and each behaves differently.

| Name | Four-month history | Mean |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 2, 6, 2, 6 | 4 |
| Beta | 0, 10, 0, 10 | 5 |
| Gamma | 7, 9, 7, 9 | 8 |

**Subtract the mean.** Alpha's readings become −2, +2, −2, +2. The drift is gone; what is left is movement around its own centre.

**Measure the spread.** Squaring those deviations and averaging gives the variance, and its square root is the standard deviation: **2** for Alpha, **5** for Beta, **1** for Gamma. Beta swings five times as widely as Gamma.

**Divide by it.** This month Alpha gains 12, Beta 10, Gamma 9. Dividing each by its own spread converts a raw gain into a count of typical moves:

| Name | This month | Standardised |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 12 | (12 − 4) ÷ 2 = **4** |
| Beta | 10 | (10 − 5) ÷ 5 = **1** |
| Gamma | 9 | (9 − 8) ÷ 1 = **1** |

Beta's 10 and Gamma's 9 look similar in raw terms. Standardised, both are an ordinary month — one typical move. Alpha's is four.

**Subtract the cross-sectional mean.** Now compare across names on the same day. The average standardised reading is (4 + 1 + 1) ÷ 3 = **2**. Subtracting it gives Alpha **+2**, Beta **−1**, Gamma **−1**, and those sum to exactly zero.

Read what just happened. All three names rose this month. After the last step, two of them are *negative*. The number no longer says "this name went up" — it says "this name went up more than its peers did", and that is a different claim about a different thing.

## The trap

The trap is stopping after the third step and believing you have found stock-selection skill. A standardised signal that is positive for every name on a rising day is telling you the market rose. It will look impressive in a backtest, it will be genuinely predictive of something, and it will not help you choose between two names — because it says the same thing about both.

The correct model: standardising against a name's own history makes signals *comparable to each other*, and subtracting the cross-sectional mean makes them *comparable to the market*. Only cross-sectional demeaning isolates what is specific to the name. A signal that survives the first step but not the second was measuring the market all along, and the cost of finding that out later is an entire strategy built on it.

## Try it

Take two holdings in your simulated Portfolio and write down each one's last four monthly moves. Compute each mean, each standard deviation, and this month's standardised reading by hand. Then subtract the average of the two standardised readings from each. Notice which holding stays positive. Ask the Research Manager which of your holdings is genuinely outperforming and check its reasoning against your own arithmetic — AMI's answers are training artifacts for you to grade, never advice.

## Takeaway

Standardising a signal makes it comparable to other signals; subtracting the cross-sectional mean makes it comparable to the market — and only after that second step does a positive number mean the name beat its peers rather than simply rose.
