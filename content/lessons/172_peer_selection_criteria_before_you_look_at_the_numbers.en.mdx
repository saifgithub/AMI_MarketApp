# Peer selection criteria: before you look at the numbers

Lesson 038 said comps are only as honest as the peer set, and that the rules for inclusion must be written down before you look at the multiples. This lesson hardens those rules into a checklist: same primary industry, similar revenue scale, similar geography, similar business-model mix, similar growth profile. Apply the checklist, accept the resulting peer set, and the median multiple it produces becomes a defensible anchor. Skip the checklist and the comp set drifts toward whatever supports your prior. By the end of this lesson you should have a five-criterion checklist and a specific size-band rule that prevents the most common peer-selection mistakes.

## How this plays out in real markets

Imagine you're valuing UAL (United Airlines), a large US legacy airline. Run the five-criterion checklist:

1. **Same primary industry:** passenger airlines, not aerospace manufacturers, not airline-services companies. So DAL, AAL, LUV pass. BA fails (manufacturer). Cuts: keep DAL/AAL/LUV.
2. **Similar revenue scale:** UAL revenue ~$55B. Use a 0.3x–3.0x band — peers within $16B–$165B. DAL ~$60B (pass), AAL ~$52B (pass), LUV ~$26B (pass), JBLU ~$9B (fail — too small to be a comparable peer despite being in the industry). Cuts: JBLU.
3. **Similar geography:** US-focused with meaningful international exposure. DAL/AAL/LUV all fit. Foreign carriers like Lufthansa (LHA.DE) are excluded — different regulatory regime, different cost base, different currency exposure.
4. **Similar business-model mix:** legacy hub-and-spoke (UAL, DAL, AAL) vs point-to-point low-cost (LUV). LUV is borderline — same industry, similar size, similar geography, different business model. Decision: include with a note ("model mix differs; will compare with and without LUV").
5. **Similar growth profile:** all four large US airlines have similar growth profiles post-pandemic recovery. No further cuts.

Final peer set: DAL, AAL, LUV with a model-mix note. Now look at the multiples (EV/EBITDA is the typical airline multiple given high capex and varying tax). If UAL trades at 5.5x EV/EBITDA against a peer median of 5.8x, it's slightly below peers but inside the noise band — neither a buy nor a sell signal on comp grounds alone.

Now an example that fails the size criterion. Comparing NVDA ($3T market cap) to a $200M-market-cap small-cap semiconductor design firm violates criterion 2 — the 15,000x size ratio means access to capital, customer relationships, and operating economics differ structurally, regardless of "same industry." A defensible NVDA peer set is AMD, AVGO, QCOM, MRVL, maybe TXN — all with revenues in the same order of magnitude.

On Bursa Malaysia: comparing MAYBANK ($25B+ market cap) to a small Bursa-listed micro-cap commercial bank violates the size band. The defensible peer set is CIMB, PUBLIC, RHB, HLBK — the top-tier Malaysian banks.

The falsifiable claim: if your peer set is defensible on all five criteria (industry, size band 0.3x–3.0x, geography, business model, growth profile), the median multiple becomes a credible anchor. If you've stretched any criterion to include or exclude a specific peer, the comp set is rigged.

## The trap

The trap is data-availability bias — including peers because their data is easy to find, not because they're the right comparables. A beginner valuing a mid-cap stock includes the megacaps because they have analyst coverage and clean financials, even though the megacaps trade at very different multiples for structural reasons (institutional ownership, index inclusion, scale advantages). The correct mental model: the peer set should be chosen by whether the peers face similar competitive economics, not by whose 10-K is easiest to read. The second version of the trap: keeping the peer set fixed even as the target company evolves. A small-cap that grows into a mid-cap needs a new peer set; an industrial that pivots to software needs a new peer set. Re-run the criteria checklist annually, especially after any large acquisition or strategic shift.

## Try it

Pick a stock you're researching. Before you open the financials, write down in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />: the five-criterion checklist applied to that stock — what industry, what revenue band, what geography, what business model, what growth profile. Then list the 3–5 peers that pass all five criteria. Only then ask the Fundamentals Analyst: "Show me EV/EBITDA, P/E, and EV/Sales for [target] vs these specific peers." This sequence — criteria first, peers second, multiples third — is the discipline that keeps comps honest.

## Takeaway

Define the peer-set rules before you see the numbers — industry, size band, geography, business model, growth profile — and accept the peer median that results, including the cases where it tells you the answer you didn't want.
