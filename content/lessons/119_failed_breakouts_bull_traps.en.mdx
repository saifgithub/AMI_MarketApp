# Failed breakouts and bull traps

This assumes you've completed <Lesson id="024_breakouts"/> (breakouts). Here we go deeper on the worst version of a breakout: the failed one. A failed breakout — also called a bull trap when it triggers long entries that then reverse — is a candle that clears resistance on weak evidence, attracts buying, then closes back inside the range. The fingerprints are usually visible before the fail: thin volume, late-session pumping, distance from the level on the close. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify the volume and price-action signatures that distinguish a real breakout from a trap, and know exactly when to flip from "breakout buyer" to "trap seller".

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at TSLA on the daily chart on 14 and 15 March 2026. TSLA had ranged $245–$275 since late January. On 14 March, TSLA closed at $277.40 — a close $2.40 above the $275 resistance. The headline read like a breakout. But three things stuck out: (1) the day's volume was 8.2 million shares, *below* the 14-million-share 20-day average — a 0.6x volume breakout where <Lesson id="024_breakouts"/> wants to see 1.5x or higher; (2) the close was only 0.9% above the level (anything under ~1% is in the noise band for TSLA's typical daily range); (3) the candle had a $4.20 upper wick — buyers pushed to $282.60 intraday but couldn't hold.

The next session, 15 March: TSLA opened at $276.10 and closed at $268.40 — a daily close back inside the prior range. By 18 March TSLA was at $258. A trader who bought the breakout at $277 with a stop "below the level" at $274 got stopped out in one session. A trader who bought thinking $275 would flip to support held into a 7% drawdown before being forced out.

In the same window, compare META on 27 March (<Lesson id="024_breakouts"/>'s example): close 1.6% above the level on 2.3x volume, no upper wick of note, followed by a hold session. That's what a real breakout looks like next to a fake one.

The trap invalidates as a short setup the moment price prints a new daily close above the failed breakout's high — for TSLA, a close above $282.60. That close didn't come, and the failed breakout converted into a clean short.

## The trap

The trap is reading "close above resistance" as a binary signal regardless of volume and candle shape. A close $0.05 above a level on 0.4x volume with a wick rejection has nothing in common — statistically — with a close 1.5% above on 2x volume with no upper wick, except the label "breakout". The first sets up a trap; the second sets up a trend. The correct mental model: the breakout is the *combination* of the close beyond the level + the volume signature + the candle shape. Miss any one and the probability of a fake rises sharply. A useful heuristic: if you couldn't honestly tell a friend "the volume was at least 1.5x average and the close was a full 1% beyond the level with no major upper wick", treat the breakout as unconfirmed — wait for a hold session or step aside entirely.

## Try it

Find a recent breakout day on any ticker. Check three things from this lesson: close-distance from the level (in %), volume relative to the 20-day average, and upper-wick size relative to the body. Ask the Technical Strategist in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />: "Did [ticker]'s breakout on [date] satisfy all three fingerprints — close > 1% above level, volume > 1.5x average, no major upper wick? What happened in the following five sessions?" Log the answer in your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> with a tag: fake or real.

## Takeaway

A real breakout has a clean close beyond the level on expanded volume with no rejection wick — anything weaker is probably a trap, and a close back inside the range is the explicit invalidation regardless of where your protective stop sits.
