# Disclosure and transparency

Markets can only price what they can see, so every serious market builds a disclosure regime: material events filed promptly, insider trades reported, selective whispering banned. Three US pieces worth knowing by name: **Regulation FD** (2000) bans disclosing material information to favoured analysts or funds before everyone else; **Form 4** requires insiders to report their own trades within two business days; the **8-K** requires material corporate events to be filed within four business days. This lesson teaches you to read disclosure two ways — the content, and the *cadence*. The cadence is the underrated one: how a company discloses when it doesn't want to is a fundamental.

## How this plays out in real markets

Wirecard was a DAX-30 member, payments darling of German tech, audited by a Big Four firm. In June 2020 it admitted that €1.9 billion of cash on its balance sheet — roughly a quarter of it — likely never existed. The shares went from around €104 to under €3 within about a week, a loss of roughly 98%. The scandal's sting: the information had been available for years. Financial Times journalists had reported accounting irregularities since 2015; Wirecard's response was to attack the reporting, sue, and lobby regulators to investigate the *journalists*. The failure that destroyed shareholders wasn't a business collapse — it was a disclosure collapse, visible in the cadence long before the confession: delayed annual reports, an auditor unable to confirm balances, aggression toward anyone asking for evidence.

That's the transparency lesson in one case: opacity defended loudly is not neutral. It is information, and the market eventually prices it all at once.

## The trap

Beginners read "no news" as "nothing happening" — the stock is quiet, filings are late, management stopped guiding, and the absence feels like calm. Silence from a disclosure regime is never neutral: filings have deadlines, so a delayed annual report, a postponed audit sign-off, or a resigned auditor is a *breach of cadence* — a leading indicator with an excellent track record. The correct model: track the disclosure calendar the way you track earnings. What was due? Did it arrive on time? Is each explanation for delay checkable, or is it aimed at the people asking?

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the News Analyst, pick a ticker from your watchlist and ask what the company's most recent *filed* disclosure was — not the most recent headline. Practising the distinction between what a company files under deadline and what it says in press releases is the fastest way to start reading cadence.

## Takeaway

Judge a company by what it files when it doesn't want to. Content tells you what happened; cadence tells you whether you're being told — and cadence breaks first.
