# The dividend discount model — and the one input that breaks it

A share is a claim on the cash a company hands its owners. The dividend discount model takes that literally: a stock is worth the present value of every dividend it will ever pay. In its standard form that collapses to one line of arithmetic — next year's dividend divided by the gap between your required return and the dividend's perpetual growth rate. The arithmetic is trivial. The judgment is not, because the whole valuation hangs on a denominator that can be a very small number, and small numbers move violently.

## Example

Take a hypothetical regulated water utility. It will pay **$2.00** per share over the next year. You want a **8%** annual return to hold it, and you believe the dividend can grow **3%** a year forever — roughly the pace of the economy it serves.

    value = 2.00 / (0.08 − 0.03) = 2.00 / 0.05 = $40.00

Now change only the growth assumption, and watch what the denominator does:

| perpetual growth | denominator | value | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | 0.05 | $40.00 | — |
| 4% | 0.04 | $50.00 | +25% |
| 5% | 0.03 | $66.67 | +67% |

One percentage point of assumed growth — a number nobody can observe — moved the valuation 25%. A second point moved it 67%. Now hold growth at 3% and raise your required return to 9% instead: the denominator becomes 0.06 and the value falls to **$33.33**, down 17%.

Nothing about the company changed in any of those rows. Only your two opinions did.

## The trap

The trap is running the model on a company it does not describe, because the arithmetic never complains. Plug a fast-growing software firm into this formula and you meet the failure immediately: if you set growth at 12% and your required return at 9%, the denominator goes negative and the model returns a negative price. Set growth to 8.9% against a 9% return and it returns a value larger than the company's entire market. Neither output is a signal — both mean *this model does not apply here*.

The perpetual growth rate is not a forecast of the next few years. It is what you believe the dividend will do **forever**, which is why it cannot exceed the long-run growth of the economy for long. A company growing faster than its economy indefinitely eventually becomes the economy.

## Steelman

The DDM's reputation as a toy is unearned, and the reason is the same as its weakness. Because the model has only three inputs, you cannot hide behind it. A discounted-cash-flow model with a fifteen-row projection lets you bury an aggressive assumption in row nine. Here there is nowhere to put it: you must say out loud what return you demand and what you believe about growth forever, and the sensitivity table above shows the reader exactly what those opinions are worth. For a mature dividend payer — a utility, a regulated telecom, a large consumer staple — that honesty is a feature.

## Try it

Open 1-on-1 with the Fundamentals Analyst and name a dividend-paying company you already hold or follow. Ask what perpetual growth rate its current price implies at a 9% required return, then ask whether that rate is above or below the long-run growth of the economy it operates in. The answer is the whole lesson: you are no longer asking "is it cheap", you are asking "is this assumption believable".

## Takeaway

The dividend discount model is one division, and the denominator is a gap between two opinions — so before you trust the price it prints, move the growth rate by one point and see how much of your answer was arithmetic and how much was you.

*Where this comes from: the perpetual-growth form of the dividend discount model, and the constraint that perpetual growth cannot exceed the discount rate, as set out in Damodaran's valuation work.*
