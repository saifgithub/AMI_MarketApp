# What the data actually says about day trading

You've decided to <Term id="day_trading"/>, or you're seriously testing the idea. Good — that's a hypothesis, and hypotheses are testable. Before you test it with money, real or simulated, it's worth knowing what the largest dataset ever assembled on this exact question actually found. Not a guru's opinion, not a forum consensus — the matched record of every buy and sell order routed through the Taiwan Stock Exchange from 1992 to 2006, 3.7 billion transactions, cross-referenced against who placed them. Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean spent over a decade mining it. This lesson is the headline numbers, without editorial. What you do with them is the rest of this track.

## How this plays out in real markets

| Question | What the Taiwan data found |
|---|---|
| How many people day trade? | About 450,000 individuals in an average year, 1992–2006 |
| How many make money in a given year? | About 18.6% of the actively-trading subgroup (>NT$600,000/yr, ~277,000 people) — 81.4% lose money |
| How many are *reliably* profitable net of fees, year after year? | Under 1% — roughly 4,000 of ~450,000 |
| Still day trading after 1 / 2 / 3 years? | 44% / 24% / 15% |

Read the middle two rows together and the shape of the problem appears. Almost one in five active day traders turns a profit in a given year — that's not nothing, and it's the number a trader who just had a good year will point to. But the researchers didn't stop at "made money once." They ranked every trader by their prior year's results and checked whether the top group stayed on top the following year. Only about 4,000 people — under 1% of the full day-trading population — cleared that bar: reliably positive returns net of fees, not a single lucky stretch. The gap between 18.6% and under 1% is the gap between "made money" and "there's a real edge here, repeatable."

And most people don't stay in the population long enough to find out which group they're in. Among traders with at least 10 days of experience, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis put the share still day trading at 44% after one year, 24% after two, 15% after three.

## The trap

There are two ways to misread this table, and they're mirror images of each other. The first: "I'm not most people" — dismissing the base rate before testing yourself against it. The second: "the odds are hopeless, why bother" — treating a population statistic as a verdict on an individual you haven't measured yet. Neither is what the data says. The data says the population base rate is brutal, and the population itself mostly doesn't find out which group it's in, because most people stop generating a track record before it means anything.

The honest response to a table like this one isn't confidence or despair — it's a measurement plan. The <1% figure exists specifically because the researchers refused to count a good year as proof; they demanded it repeat, out of sample, before they'd call it skill. That's the same discipline available to you starting now, and it's cheaper here than anywhere else: a simulator has no minimum capital, no real losses, and no limit on how many times you can test the hypothesis before it costs you anything but time.

## Try it

You can't get fourteen years of Taiwan-Exchange data on yourself, but you can start building the one number that matters here: a track record. Place a handful of <Term id="simulated_trade"/>s over the next few sessions and let the <Term id="ami_decision_journal"/> keep the log — every entry, every exit, the losers included. In a month, take it to the Conservative Risk Officer in <Term id="ami_one_on_one"/> and ask it to look at your win rate and average hold time against the pattern in this lesson. It won't be able to tell you which group you're in yet — a month isn't a year — but it can tell you whether you're keeping the kind of record that could ever answer the question.

## Takeaway

The Taiwan data is the largest test of this exact hypothesis ever run, and the honest reading is narrow: most active day traders lose money in a given year, fewer than 1% do it reliably enough to call it skill, and most people stop trading before they'd find out which group they were in. None of that tells you what group you're in. Only a measured record does — and that's the rest of this track.
