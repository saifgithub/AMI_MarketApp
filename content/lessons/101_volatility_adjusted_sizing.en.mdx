# Volatility-adjusted position sizing

This lesson assumes you've completed lesson 014 (Position sizing basics); here we go deeper on how to keep risk constant across instruments that move very differently. A fixed 1% of account is the right rule, but if you apply the same dollar-stop distance to TSLA and to KO, you've effectively taken a 4× bigger bet on TSLA — same dollar risk, but TSLA's daily range eats the stop in hours while KO's takes weeks. Volatility-adjusted sizing uses the instrument's recent range (typically ATR — Average True Range) to set the stop distance, so a 1% account risk produces the same odds of stop-out across every ticker on your <Term id="ami_watchlist" />.

## Example

Your simulated account is $50,000, max 1% risk per trade = $500 risk budget. TSLA is at $240, 14-day ATR = $9. KO is at $62, 14-day ATR = $0.75. You like both setups long.

For TSLA, set the stop at 1.5 ATR below entry: $240 − $13.50 = $226.50. Per-share risk = $13.50. Shares = $500 ÷ $13.50 = 37 shares. Notional = $8,880 (17.8% of account). For KO, same 1.5 ATR stop: $62 − $1.125 = $60.875. Per-share risk = $1.125. Shares = $500 ÷ $1.125 = 444 shares. Notional = $27,528 (55.1% of account). Different notionals, identical risk, identical odds of getting stopped out on normal noise — because the stop scales to each name's volatility instead of being a flat percentage or dollar amount.

On Bursa Malaysia: AIRASIA at RM2.40 with a 14-day ATR of RM0.08 vs MAYBANK at RM10.20 with a 14-day ATR of RM0.12. Same RM500 risk budget, 1.5 ATR stops. AIRASIA: stop RM2.28, per-share risk RM0.12, 4,166 shares. MAYBANK: stop RM10.02, per-share risk RM0.18, 2,777 shares. Falsifiable test: if you size two trades at the same dollar risk without adjusting for ATR, the higher-volatility name will hit its stop more than twice as often on noise alone within the first five trading days.

## The trap

The trap is using a flat percentage stop (5% below entry, say) across every ticker. On low-volatility names like KO or JPM, a 5% stop is well outside normal noise — fine, but it forces a very small share count, so the position barely contributes to portfolio outcomes. On high-volatility names like TSLA or NVDA, 5% can be inside a normal day's range — you get stopped out by noise that says nothing about the thesis. The correct mental model: dollar risk is constant (the 1% rule), but stop distance scales with the instrument's recent range. ATR is the cleanest measure; 1.5× to 2× ATR is the working range for swing trades. Anything tighter than 1× ATR is asking to be shaken out; anything looser than 3× ATR makes the stop more of a hope than an invalidation.

## Try it

Open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Trader, pick any two tickers from your watchlist with very different volatility (e.g., TSLA and JPM), and ask "What's the 14-day ATR for each, and what would 1.5 ATR stop distances look like at current price?" Then run the position-sizing math at 1% account risk for both — note how different the share counts and notionals are.

## Takeaway

Risk is the constant; stop distance is the variable — let volatility, not a round percentage, decide where the stop sits.
