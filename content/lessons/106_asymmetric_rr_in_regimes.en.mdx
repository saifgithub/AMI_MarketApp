# Asymmetric R:R across trending and ranging markets

This lesson assumes you've completed <Lesson id="016_risk_reward_ratio"/> (Risk/reward ratio); here we go deeper on the fact that the R:R you can realistically achieve depends on what kind of market you're in. In a strong trend, targets resolve cleanly and runners can deliver 3:1 or 5:1; in a tight range, the same setup tops out at 1.2:1 because the price can't get past the range high before reversing. Using the same minimum R:R threshold in both regimes is a category error — you over-trade ranges (because most setups fail the threshold but you take them anyway) or under-trade trends (because you take profits too early at the threshold instead of letting runners deliver). This lesson teaches how to calibrate the threshold to the regime.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at GOOGL across two regimes. In the trending Q2 2024 stretch, GOOGL ran from $148 to $190 with three pullback entries along the way. A typical entry at $155 with a $150 stop and a target at the next round number ($170) showed paper R:R = 3.0. But the trend kept extending — runners trailed with a 2× ATR chandelier rode to $180+ for realised R:R closer to 5:1. The minimum threshold that "made sense" in that regime was 3:1 with a willingness to trail past it.

Compare the ranging Q3 2024 stretch in GOOGL, which oscillated between $160 and $172. The same kind of entry — $163 long, stop $159 (risk $4), target $172 (reward $9) — showed paper R:R = 2.25. Looks good on paper. But the target sits at the range top, where supply re-enters. Price tagged $171.50 and reversed to $164 over two days. Realised R:R = (171.50 − 163) ÷ (163 − 159) = 2.13. Not bad, but no runner — the range high is a ceiling, not a launchpad. Demanding 3:1 in this regime would have rejected every valid setup. On Bursa Malaysia: same logic on MAYBANK in a slow RM10.10–RM10.70 range — a 2:1 setup is the structural ceiling, not the floor. Falsifiable test: if you take 10 trades demanding 3:1 R:R inside a range that's 5% wide, more than half fail at the range top before reaching target.

## The trap

The trap is using a single R:R floor regardless of regime. The beginner sets `min_risk_reward_ratio` to 2.0 and applies it to every trade. In a strong trend they exit at 2R while the move extends to 5R — leaving most of the trade's value on the table. In a tight range they keep waiting for a 2R setup that doesn't exist and either skip the regime entirely or, more often, take a forced 1.4R setup and call it 2R by extending the target outside the range. The correct mental model: regime determines both the achievable R:R and the appropriate threshold. Trending markets — demand 2:1 minimum but trail runners. Ranging markets — accept 1.5:1 to 2:1 fixed-target setups and don't extend targets past the range. The Technical Strategist can flag the regime in <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />; use that flag to pick the threshold.

## Try it

Open a 1-on-1 with the Execution Desk, pick a ticker from your <Term id="ami_watchlist" />, and ask "Is this currently trending or ranging, and what R:R is structurally achievable here?" Then look at your last 10 trades on the same ticker in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> — were you demanding 3:1 in a range, or letting 5:1 runners get cut at 2:1 in a trend? Calibrate accordingly.

## Takeaway

In trends, demand 2:1 but trail runners; in ranges, accept 1.5:1 to 2:1 and take the target at the structural ceiling — never extend a target past the regime.
