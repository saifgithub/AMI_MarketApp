# The pre-mortem

A pre-mortem inverts the usual risk question. Instead of asking what could go wrong, you assume the decision has already failed — the position is closed, the thesis is dead — and you write the story of how it happened. Gary Klein's method works because a concrete causal story recruits different reasoning than an open-ended list does: the prospective-hindsight research Klein cites found that imagining an event has already occurred raises the number of reasons people generate for it by roughly 30%. (Klein's own gloss in HBR reads "correctly identify reasons"; the underlying 1989 study counted the reasons produced and never scored them for correctness. More causes surfaced is the finding — a useful one, and a narrower claim than it is usually quoted as.) By the end of this lesson you can run one on a Room verdict before you commit, and turn each failure story into a tripwire you can actually check.

## Example

Take a hypothetical mid-cap industrial — invented figures, no real ticker, so read the numbers as the shape of the problem rather than a market claim. The simulated Room returns a BUY: entry $50, stop $44, 8% of the simulated portfolio, six-month horizon. The planned worst case is arithmetic: a 12% move against you on an 8% weight is a 0.96% portfolio hit.

Now run the pre-mortem. It is six months later, the position is closed, the thesis is dead. Three stories, all past tense:

1. The margin expansion the Bull Researcher leaned on was a one-off input-cost benefit. It reversed by the third quarter and the multiple re-rated down with it.
2. The single customer supplying roughly a third of revenue did not renew. You never checked the contract calendar.
3. The miss arrived at earnings, the open gapped straight through $44, and the fill landed near $41 — an 18% position loss, 1.44% of the portfolio, half again the 0.96% you had budgeted.

Each story ends in a tripwire: the cost-line detail in the next filing, the disclosed customer concentration, the earnings date sitting inside your holding period. None of the three shows up on a "what could go wrong" list, because that list produces categories — margin risk, customer risk — with no check attached to them.

## The trap

The beginner move is to ask what could go wrong and answer with nouns: macro risk, competition, valuation. It feels rigorous, and it changes nothing, because a category has no observable attached to it. The second failure mode is timing — run the pre-mortem after you have already committed to the position and the exercise stops being a search and becomes a justification (Klein's method runs on a fully specified plan — sized, priced, stop set — but strictly before commitment). It is also not the same as arguing the other side (<Lesson id="360_steelmanning_the_other_side"/>): the steelman argues the case against on today's evidence, while the pre-mortem stipulates the loss as an accomplished fact and works backwards to whatever caused it — a chain that often ends with the bull premise having been false all along, as story 1 above does. The correct model is to assume failure as a fact, write it in past tense before you commit, and make every story name the one thing you could check that would show it starting.

## Try it

Open a Room verdict you have not accepted yet. In the Decision Journal, write one paragraph dated six months out that starts "This position was closed at a loss because" and finish it in past tense. Then name the single disclosure, date, or price level that would show that story starting. Ask the Research Manager which part of the Room's argument your story contradicts. AMI's verdict is a training artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Assume it already failed and write the story: a specific cause you can check beats a risk category you cannot. If your failure story does not end in a tripwire, the pre-mortem is not finished.
