# The round-number trap

Round numbers — $100, $500, $1,000 a share, "the trillion-dollar market cap" — are not technical levels. They are psychological anchors that pull both targets and stops toward them, against the math of the chart. After this lesson you will be able to recognise when a round number is silently editing your plan, name the anchoring bias driving it (extending <Lesson id="045_greed"/> on greed), and replace the anchor with a level the chart actually drew. The cost of trading round numbers is paid in two places: holding a winner past its real exit because "$500 feels like the round target" and selling a loser early because "below $100 feels broken".

## How this plays out in real markets

In November 2024, a trader entered NVDA around $138 with a written target of $172 (the prior measured-move extension) and a stop at $128. As NVDA approached $150 — the first round-number magnet — the trader felt the urge to take "half off at the round number". He didn't write that into the plan; he just felt it. He scaled half at $150. NVDA closed the day at $148.50, ran to $152.40 the next session, then resumed the trend straight to $173 over the following four weeks. The half he scaled at $150 finished at +8.7%. The half he held to $172 finished at +24.6%. The difference between the two halves was not strategy. It was a round number whispering louder than the measured-move math.

A counter-example, same trader, GME 2024: he had bought GME at $22 with a stop at $19.40 (a real structural low). As GME slid to $20.10, the round $20 mark started to feel like "if it breaks 20 it's broken". He moved his stop up to $20.05 — tighter, but on no structural basis. GME printed $19.98 in the next session, knocked him out, and closed back above $21 within forty minutes. His real stop at $19.40 would have held the position; the round-number stop did not.

## The trap

The trap is that round numbers look like levels because crowds congregate at them — there is real order flow at $100 and $500. But the *direction* of that flow is unknowable from the round number alone. Anchoring on round numbers as exits (whether targets or stops) replaces a level the chart drew with a level your brain drew. The correct mental model: round numbers are *zones of expected noise*, not zones of expected reversal. Your target is wherever the measured move, the resistance line, the fibonacci extension, or the volatility band says — and if those happen to coincide with a round number, fine, the math agrees. But the round number itself is never the reason. If you cannot point to a chart-drawn level under the round number, the round number is anchoring, not analysis.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and filter to closed trades where your exit price was within $0.50 of a round number. For each, ask: was the round number near a chart-drawn level (measured move, prior high/low, MA, fib)? If less than half of them had a real level within $2 of the round number, you have a documented round-number anchoring pattern. Take one example to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Technical Strategist and ask: "Where was the actual chart level for this trade?" The gap between that and where you actually exited is your anchoring cost.

## Takeaway

Round numbers are zones of attention, not zones of reversal. If you cannot point to a level the chart drew, the round number is anchoring — and anchoring is greed and fear wearing the same disguise.
