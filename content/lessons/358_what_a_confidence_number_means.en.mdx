# What a confidence number actually means

A confidence number only means something if it can be scored. Calibration is that test: if you say 70% and you are well calibrated, then across all the times you say 70%, about 70% of those calls resolve the way you said. A single call proves nothing — calibration is a property of a track record, not of one number. AMI's confidence figure is the Room's weighted lean rather than a calibrated probability, which <Lesson id="077_decision_support_vs_prediction"/> covers. This lesson covers the other half: what calibration would actually require, and how a number becomes a claim you can be scored on.

## Example

Suppose you log ten calls over a quarter, each one written down at 70% confidence — the kind of call where you record "I think this thesis holds" and mark it 0.70. Five resolve as you called them; five don't.

Now score it. The standard scoring rule is the Brier score: for each call take (stated probability − outcome), where outcome is 1 if it happened and 0 if it didn't, square it, then average across all calls. Zero is perfect, 1 is the worst possible, lower is better. (That is the single-event convention used throughout this lesson. Tetlock's *Superforecasting* reports Brier scores on a 0–2 scale, where 0.5 is chance rather than 0.25 — the same ranking, every figure doubled. Compare scores only within one convention.)

Your five hits contribute (0.70 − 1)² = 0.09 each, so 5 × 0.09 = 0.45. Your five misses contribute (0.70 − 0)² = 0.49 each, so 5 × 0.49 = 2.45. Total 2.90 across ten calls → **Brier 0.290**.

Then the benchmark. Had you written 0.50 on all ten and claimed nothing at all, every term would be (0.50 − outcome)² = 0.25, for a Brier of **0.250**. Your confident calls scored worse than making no claim.

Run the same arithmetic on a 7-of-10 quarter: 7 × 0.09 = 0.63, 3 × 0.49 = 1.47, total 2.10 → **Brier 0.210**, which beats the 0.250 benchmark. Identical stated confidence, different resolution rate — and the score, not the feeling, is what separates them.

One caution before you read too much into ten calls. If you really are calibrated at 70%, five-or-fewer hits in ten still happens about **15%** of the time — roughly one quarter in seven, from luck alone. The 0.290 is a fact about this sample; it is not yet evidence the 70% was unearned. Sustain the same rate to forty calls — twenty-or-fewer hits — and the luck explanation drops under **1%**. That is when the number is genuinely in trouble. <Lesson id="363_scoring_yourself_over_time"/> covers how many samples a verdict on yourself actually takes.

## The trap

The trap is treating a confidence number as a description of how strongly you feel rather than a claim you have agreed to be scored on. It feels right, because the number does encode something real: 0.75 genuinely feels surer than 0.55. But a feeling cannot be wrong, and a claim can. Calibration only exists where three things are present — many calls, a resolution criterion fixed before the outcome exists, and a scoring rule applied to every call rather than the memorable ones. Drop any one of the three and the number reverts to vocabulary. The correct mental model: a confidence figure is an entry in a ledger you will settle later. If you never settle it, you never had a probability. You had an adjective wearing a decimal point.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal and take three entries that have **not** resolved yet — if your recent ones have all closed, use the next three you open. Write a probability on each and the resolution criterion that would settle it, both while the outcome still does not exist. That order is the whole exercise: a probability written after the fact is a description, and only one written before can be scored. Then ask the Research Manager how the Room arrived at the confidence figure on a recent verdict. You are holding two different objects side by side: a weighted lean, and a claim you have agreed to score. Remember that AMI's verdicts are practice artifacts, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

A confidence number means nothing until it has been scored against many resolutions — say 70% often enough and roughly 70% should happen, or the number is decoration. AMI's confidence is the Room's weighted lean; the numbers you can actually calibrate are your own.
