# Why bonds exist — coupon, par, maturity

A stock makes you an owner; a bond makes you a lender. When a company or government needs cash, it can sell a piece of itself (equity) or promise to pay the cash back with interest on a schedule (debt). A bond is that promise in security form: you hand over money today, and in return you get fixed interest payments plus your money back at a set date. After this lesson you'll be able to name the three numbers that define any bond and explain why lenders and owners sit on opposite sides of the same capital stack.

## Example

Take a hypothetical corporate bond — the kind JPM or MSFT might issue to fund operations. Face value (also called **par**) is $1,000: the amount you're repaid at maturity. The **coupon rate** might be 4% annually, meaning the issuer pays you $40 a year (often split into two $20 semiannual payments) for as long as you hold the bond. **Maturity** is the date the issuer returns your $1,000 — say, 10 years from issuance. Those three numbers — par, coupon, maturity — are the entire contract. Compare that to owning MSFT stock: no promised payment schedule, no repayment date, no ceiling on the upside, and no floor on the downside. Bondholders get paid before stockholders if the company runs into trouble, but they never share in the upside beyond their fixed coupon. On Bursa Malaysia, the Islamic-finance-compliant version of this same idea is the **sukuk** — structured to avoid interest (riba) by tying returns to an underlying asset or venture instead, but functionally still a fixed-income instrument with a payment schedule and a maturity.

## The trap

Beginners assume "bonds are boring, skip to stocks" because bonds don't have the lottery-ticket upside of a hot ticker. That instinct skips half the capital stack. The correct mental model: bonds and stocks aren't competitors, they're two different claims on the same company's future cash flows — one fixed and senior (bonds), one residual and junior (stock). A portfolio that only understands stocks doesn't understand what determines a stock's discount rate, its bankruptcy risk ranking, or why the whole market moves when a central bank changes rates. Fixed income literacy isn't a detour from equity investing — it's the other half of reading it.

## Try it

Open the Fundamentals Analyst chat and ask about a company on your watchlist: "Does this company carry debt, and what would that mean for bondholders versus shareholders if earnings dropped sharply?" Notice how the answer forces you to think about the capital stack, not just the stock price.

## Takeaway

A bond is a fixed, senior claim defined by three numbers — par, coupon, maturity — while a stock is a residual, junior claim with no ceiling and no floor. Know which one you're holding and why it sits where it does in the capital stack.
