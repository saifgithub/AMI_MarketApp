# Capstone: thinking in probabilities (Bayesian updating)

You now hold the full M20 toolkit: expected value as the probability-weighted average of a decision, base rates as the background frequency a claim must clear before evidence can move it, fat tails as a reminder that extreme outcomes are more common than clean models suggest, correlation-versus-causation as a check on what a relationship actually means, and sample size as a check on how much any one result should move your belief. Bayesian updating is the formula that ties all of them together: it takes a prior probability (informed by the base rate), updates it with new evidence (whose strength depends on the sample size behind it), and produces a posterior probability you can then run back through an expected-value calculation.

## One scenario, every M20 concept

A vendor claims a new trading strategy has a real edge. Before looking at any evidence, what's the **prior** probability that a randomly claimed "edge" like this is genuinely real? Per the base-rate lesson: most claimed edges are noise, so start with a prior of **10%** real. Now the evidence arrives: a backtest over **20 trades** shows a **70% win rate** — the exact scenario from the sample-size lesson, where a two-standard-error bar (≈20.5 points) only *just* fails to clear the observed 20-point edge. Because that evidence is weak (not because it's fake), say it would show up 80% of the time if the edge were genuinely real, but it would *also* show up 20% of the time by pure chance even with no real edge at all — a likelihood ratio of 4-to-1 in favor of "real," not overwhelming.

Bayes' rule: posterior = [P(evidence | real) × P(real)] ÷ [P(evidence | real) × P(real) + P(evidence | not real) × P(not real)] = (0.8 × 0.10) ÷ (0.8 × 0.10 + 0.2 × 0.90) = 0.08 ÷ (0.08 + 0.18) = 0.08 ÷ 0.26 ≈ **30.8%**. The weak evidence moved belief from a 10% prior to a 30.8% posterior — real movement, but nowhere near "proven." Now run that posterior back through expected value: if the edge is real, assume it's worth +$1,000; if it isn't, assume acting on it costs -$1,000. EV = (0.308 × $1,000) + (0.692 × -$1,000) = $308 - $692 ≈ **-$385**. Even after updating upward from 10% to 30.8% on real evidence, the expected value of trusting this claim is still firmly negative.

## The steelman

The strongest case against this whole exercise: "You made up the prior (10%), the likelihood ratio (4-to-1), and the assumed payoffs (±$1,000) — precise-looking math built on guessed inputs is false precision, not rigor." That's a fair caution — Bayesian updating is only as good as its inputs, and dressing a guess in decimal points doesn't make it less of a guess. The falsifier: even *rough* versions of these inputs (a low prior because most claimed edges fail, a modest likelihood ratio because 20 trades is weak evidence per the sample-size lesson) point to the same directional conclusion — a 20-trade backtest alone should shift belief only modestly, not to conviction. The discipline isn't "trust the decimal point"; it's "update in the right direction by roughly the right amount, and don't let a small, weak sample jump you straight to certainty."

## Try it

Pick a strategy claim you've heard about (a screener result, a Room verdict, a tip). Write down your own prior, a rough likelihood ratio for the evidence you have, compute the Bayesian posterior by hand, and then run that posterior through an expected-value calculation before deciding what to do with it. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Don't just ask "is this evidence convincing?" — start from a realistic base rate, update it by exactly as much as the evidence's strength justifies, and only then compute whether the resulting probability makes the bet worth taking.

*Where this comes from: Bayes' theorem for updating probability with new evidence — Thomas Bayes, An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances (1763).*
