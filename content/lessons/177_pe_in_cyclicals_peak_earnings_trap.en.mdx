# P/E in cyclicals — when a low P/E means peak earnings

Lesson 039 said a low P/E is only a discount if earnings aren't declining. This lesson nails the most expensive corner of that rule: cyclical businesses. For steel mills, oil majors, semiconductors, autos, homebuilders, shipping, and chemicals, the P/E ratio is *inverted* relative to ordinary intuition — the cheapest-looking multiple appears precisely when the cycle is about to roll over. By the end of this lesson you should be able to recognise a cycle peak, name the specific earnings-quality marker that warns you, and resist the strongest single trap in value investing.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at X (US Steel) historically near a steel-cycle peak. Hot-rolled coil at $1,500/ton, capacity utilisation in the high 80s, EPS for the trailing twelve months at $10. Stock trades at $40, so trailing P/E ≈ 4x. Sector median trailing P/E is 12x, S&P is 22x. A naive screener flags X as "deep value — 80% discount to market." Now run the cycle forward two years: hot-rolled coil drops to $750/ton, utilisation falls to the low 70s, EPS collapses to $1. The stock at $40 is suddenly trading at 40x earnings. Worse, the stock didn't sit at $40 through this — it dropped to $20. The 4x P/E was real on the print and a lie about the future.

Now contrast with INTC at a cycle trough: stock at $25, trailing EPS at $0.20 (depressed), trailing P/E = 125x — looks brutal. Two years later utilisation recovers, EPS reaches $2.50, and the stock at $40 is at 16x. The high P/E at the trough was the buy signal; the low P/E at the peak was the sell signal. Cyclicals trade exactly backwards from how P/E intuition works for compounders.

Falsifiable claim: for a cyclical, a trailing P/E meaningfully below long-run median (say, below 6x for steel/oil/shipping, or below 10x for chemicals/autos) combined with capacity utilisation in the top quartile of the last decade is a peak-earnings signal, not a value signal. The condition that invalidates the signal: utilisation in the bottom quartile of the cycle (a trough). On Bursa: PETRONAS-related counters often look optically cheap (P/E 6–8x) at $90+ oil; after a cyclical retracement to $55 oil those same names trade at 20x on slashed EPS — the stock had already adjusted before the multiple did.

## The trap

The trap is the deep-value screener trap: ranking the market by trailing P/E ascending, picking the cheapest names, and finding yourself loaded into cyclicals at the top of their cycle. Every name you bought screens cheap because the *denominator* — trailing earnings — is mechanically inflated by a transient cycle peak. The correct mental model: for any cyclical, replace trailing EPS with mid-cycle EPS (an estimate of earnings averaged across a full cycle) before computing P/E. Then ask: at mid-cycle EPS, what's the multiple? A steel name at $40 with mid-cycle EPS of $4 is trading at 10x mid-cycle — fair, not cheap. The trailing 4x was just the cycle handing you a number. What invalidates the signal: a long-term shift in industry structure (consolidation that lifts the floor, regulation that caps supply) can permanently raise mid-cycle EPS and make a low P/E genuinely cheap — but those shifts take years to confirm, not quarters.

## Takeaway

For cyclicals, trailing P/E inverts: the cheapest-looking number signals the peak. Always recompute the multiple on mid-cycle EPS before treating a low P/E as value.
