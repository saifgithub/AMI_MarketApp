# What Is a Stock?

A stock is a piece of a company. When you own a share of Apple, you own a slice of Apple — fractional, but real. Today there are roughly 15 billion Apple shares outstanding, so if you hold one, you own a 1 / 15-billionth slice of the entire business.

That tiny slice gives you two things:

1. **A claim on future profits.** If Apple makes money, some of it can be paid out to shareholders as dividends. Most companies retain most of their profits to grow, but the *legal claim* is yours.
2. **A vote.** Shareholders get to vote on company decisions — who sits on the board, major acquisitions, etc. One share, one vote. (In practice retail shareholders rarely vote. Big institutional holders do.)

## Why do companies sell stock?

Companies sell stock to **raise money** without taking on debt. They IPO ("initial public offering"), and instead of borrowing $1B from a bank, they sell $1B worth of new shares to the public. The cash funds factories, R&D, acquisitions, hiring.

In exchange, the founders give up some control — every new share dilutes the existing ownership pool. Going public is a trade: capital now, in exchange for a permanent piece of the company.

## Why do *you* buy stock?

Two paths:

- **Capital appreciation.** You buy at $100, the business grows, the stock goes to $150. You sell, you profit. The most common reason individual investors buy.
- **Income.** Some companies pay regular dividends. If you hold $100,000 of a 3% dividend stock, that's $3,000/year in your pocket, regardless of price movement. This is the path of most retirees and income-focused investors.

## The catch

A stock's price isn't tied to the *current* value of the business. It's tied to what investors **think** the future will look like. If the market collectively thinks Apple's next 10 years will be worse than expected, the stock falls — even if today's Apple is doing fine.

That gap between current reality and future expectations is where every trade lives. Your Fundamentals Analyst's whole job is to answer one question: **what's actually likely to happen?**

## In your sim

You can't lose real money in AMI Trade. Every position you take is paper. But the prices you see are real. When you buy NVDA at $152 in your sim, that's the actual market price. When NVDA goes up tomorrow, your sim gains the same percentage a real-money position would have gained.

This matters because **good thinking transfers**. The reasoning that produces a good sim trade is the same reasoning that produces a good real trade. We're not teaching you how to push buttons. We're teaching you how to think.
