# Operating leverage in cyclicals

<Lesson id="033_profit_and_margins"/> explained margins; this lesson explains why margins move so much *more* than revenue in certain businesses. Operating leverage is the relationship between fixed and variable costs: when a high-fixed-cost business adds revenue, most of the incremental dollar drops straight to operating profit. When it loses revenue, the same fixed costs eat profit at a brutal rate. Cyclicals — semiconductors, airlines, miners, automakers, steelmakers, shipping — exhibit the highest operating leverage. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify operating-leverage exposure, compute the rough amplification factor, and know the specific trap that catches investors at peak-cycle margins.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at semiconductor capital-equipment maker AMAT (Applied Materials) across cycles. In a downcycle year revenue might drop 15% but operating profit drops 35–45% because the R&D and facility costs don't shrink proportionally. In an upcycle the same lever runs in reverse: revenue up 15% can drive operating profit up 35–45%. The "amplification ratio" — change in operating profit divided by change in revenue — is typically 2–3x at high-operating-leverage businesses, vs ~1x at low-leverage ones (retailers, where COGS scales nearly proportionally).

Real numerical example: in fiscal 2024, INTC reported revenue around $53B and operating income around $0.9B (effectively breakeven at the operating line excluding asset impairments). That same business at fiscal 2021 peak generated roughly $79B revenue and ~$20B operating income. A 33% revenue decline produced a >95% operating-income decline. The fixed-cost base — fabs, R&D, headcount — barely moved. That ratio (~3x operating-profit decline per unit of revenue decline) is the operating-leverage signature of capital-intensive cyclicals.

The reverse lever, when it works: airlines in 2022–2023. UAL revenue rose roughly 80% from 2021 to 2022 as travel recovered; operating margin swung from –7% to +5%. That's not a 7%-to-12% gradual move; it's a 12-percentage-point operating margin swing on a revenue base that was largely the same set of planes, gates, and pilots already paid for.

On Bursa Malaysia: AIRASIA's operating margin pre-COVID, during COVID, and post-COVID swings from mid-single digits positive to deeply negative and back — the underlying cost base (aircraft leases, maintenance, ground operations) barely flexes with passenger volume. The same applies to PETRONAS Chemicals across feedstock cycles.

The falsifiable claim: a business with a fixed-cost ratio (operating expenses that don't scale with revenue) above 60% will exhibit an operating-leverage amplification of 2x or more in both directions. The amplification doesn't dissipate at peak margins — it just runs in reverse when revenue rolls over.

## The trap

The trap is anchoring on peak-cycle margins as the new normal. A beginner sees a cyclical company at peak operating margin (say 30% vs a through-cycle average of 12%) and forecasts forward at the peak rate. They compute a forward P/E that looks cheap because the EPS estimate is based on peak earnings. Then revenue rolls over 15%, operating profit drops 40% via the same leverage that drove it up, and the "cheap" P/E was actually expensive on normalised earnings. The correct mental model: at high operating leverage, margins are amplified, not earned. Always check what the through-cycle average operating margin looks like over a full 5–10 year period — and assume mid-cycle is the right number to use when discounting forward earnings, not the most recent quarter.

Pick a cyclical name from your <Term id="ami_watchlist" /> (a semi, automaker, miner, or airline). In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, ask: "What is [ticker]'s 10-year through-cycle average operating margin? Where is the current quarter's margin relative to that average? Estimate the normalised earnings if margins reverted to the through-cycle level." Save the normalised P/E next to the trailing P/E in your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />.

## Takeaway

Operating leverage amplifies revenue moves into much bigger profit moves — in both directions. At peak margins, trailing P/E is the most misleading number on the page. Normalise before you buy.
