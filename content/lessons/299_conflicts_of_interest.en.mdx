# Conflicts of interest: follow the money

A conflict of interest is structural, not moral: it exists whenever the person advising you is paid in a way that can pull against your interest — before anyone behaves badly, and even if they never do. That framing matters because it changes what you look for. You don't audit character; you **map the payment flow**. This lesson trains the map: find who pays, what behaviour the payment rewards, and then decide how much weight the advice deserves.

## How this plays out in real markets

Robinhood advertised commission-free trading. In December 2020 the SEC fined it $65 million for failing to properly disclose that its real revenue was **payment for order flow** — market makers paying Robinhood to execute its customers' orders. The SEC found customers received inferior execution prices costing them $34.1 million *even after accounting for the zero commissions*. The service wasn't free; the fee was hidden inside the fills, and the broker's paying customer was the market maker, not you.

Bigger and older: the 2003 Global Research Analyst Settlement. Ten Wall Street firms paid $1.4 billion after regulators showed their "independent" research was marketing for their investment-banking clients — analysts publicly rating stocks a buy while privately deriding them, because the covered companies paid the firm for banking services. Retail investors read the ratings as analysis; they were priced as advertising. Same map in both cases: the advice flowed to you, the money flowed from someone whose interest wasn't yours.

## The trap

The beginner's shortcut: "it was disclosed, so it's handled." Disclosure feels like a solved problem — the conflict is in the open, everyone can adjust. But disclosure removes the *secrecy*, not the *incentive*: the analyst's pay still depends on the banking relationship after the footnote is printed. Behavioural research adds a nastier wrinkle — advisers can lean harder into biased advice after disclosing, feeling licensed by their own honesty. The correct model: treat disclosure as the *starting* signal. Read it to build the payment map, then weight the advice as if the incentive works — because it usually does.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the PM, ask: "What incentive does AMI have in whether I take a simulated trade?" Then build the same map for a real-world platform you know: who pays it, per what behaviour? AMI's answer — a training subscription that earns the same whether you trade or not — is the map you should demand from anything that touches real money.

## Takeaway

Every piece of advice arrives with an incentive attached. Find who pays before you weigh what's said — and treat disclosure as where the analysis starts, not where it ends.
