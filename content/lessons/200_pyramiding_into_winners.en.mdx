# Pyramiding into winners — when it works and when it's greed

Pyramiding is adding to a position that is already in profit. Done one way, it lets a thesis compound at a falling average cost in risk-units. Done the other way, it is the textbook last act of greed — turning a winner into the trade that owes you the year back. Building on lesson 045 (greed) and lesson 014 (position sizing), this lesson gives you a falsifiable test that separates planned pyramiding from emotional pyramiding, and a deterministic rule you can wire into your <Term id="ami_mandate" /> so the urge to "add to a winner" cannot quietly outrun your written risk.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader entered NVDA in October 2023 at $42 (post-split-adjusted) with 1% account risk and a written plan: first add only if the position closed at or above +12% with a higher swing-low printed, second add only above +25% with another higher swing-low, third add never. At $48 he added — clean. At $55 he added again — clean. Total committed risk after two adds was 1.7% of account, because each add used a tightened stop based on the new swing-low, not a fresh 1% stop on the new shares. The position ran to $74 by January 2024 and he closed it on his original target rule.

Six months later, on AAPL, the same trader pyramided three times *without* the swing-low confirmations because "the trend is obvious." Each new tranche was sized 1% on a wide stop. By the third add, his real risk on AAPL was 3.4% of account against a 1% mandate. AAPL gapped down 6% on a guidance cut. The single-position loss was 4.1% of account — four times the planned exposure. The strategy had not failed. The discipline around the strategy had.

## The trap

The trap is treating an add as a fresh entry rather than an extension of the original trade. If you size each pyramiding tranche as 1% account risk on a new wide stop, you have not built a position — you have stacked three independent 1% trades on the same ticker, which is concentration risk pretending to be conviction. Healthy pyramiding has two non-negotiable features: each add references a *new structural level* (higher swing-low, breakout-retest, fresh consolidation), and each add tightens the stop on the whole stack so total committed risk does not exceed your original mandate. If you cannot point to the new level, or if you cannot show the math that keeps total risk capped, the add is greed wearing a strategy hat.

## Try it

Open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> and find your last three positions where you added more than once. For each, compute total committed risk (entry-to-stop distance times shares, summed across all tranches, divided by account size at the time of each add). If any one is above your mandate per-trade risk, take the screen to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and ask: "Show me the structural level each add referenced." The pattern usually answers itself within the first response.

## Takeaway

A pyramid works only if every add references a new structural level AND total committed risk stays inside the mandate. If either part is missing, the add is greed with a justification attached.
