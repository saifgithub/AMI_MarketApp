# Sukuk vs conventional bonds

A conventional bond is a loan: the issuer owes you principal plus interest, full stop, regardless of what the money was used for. A sukuk is structured as **partial ownership of a real, underlying asset or venture** — the return you earn is rental income, profit-share, or a sale-and-leaseback markup on something real, not interest on money lent. This lesson extends the fixed-income module (303-309) you already covered: the pricing mechanics of a sukuk trade like a bond's do, but the legal claim underneath is fundamentally different. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name the structural difference and state the strongest critique against it.

## Example

The two lessons you already have on bond mechanics (303-309: pricing, yield-to-maturity, duration, credit spreads) still apply to sukuk in terms of *how the market prices the cash flow stream* — a sukuk's price still moves opposite to yield, the way lesson 304 showed for a conventional bond. What differs is the legal structure generating that cash flow. A common form, **sukuk al-ijarah**, works like this: the issuer sells a real asset (say, an office tower) to a special-purpose vehicle, which leases it back to the issuer; sukuk holders own a share of that SPV and receive rental payments as their return, and at maturity the issuer buys the asset back at a pre-agreed price. Malaysia is the world's largest sukuk market by outstanding volume, with Bursa Malaysia and the government issuing extensively in this format; GCC sovereigns (Saudi Arabia, UAE) are also major sukuk issuers, often using a similar asset-backed lease structure for infrastructure financing. The economic return can land close to a conventional bond's coupon in a stable rate environment — but the underlying legal claim (rental income on a real, owned asset) is what makes it non-riba, not the size of the payment.

## The trap

Beginners assume sukuk is "just a bond with a different name" because the pricing behavior and the return size often look similar. That's the mechanics, not the substance. The correct mental model: what makes a sukuk non-riba is the legal claim — ownership of (or a beneficial interest in) a real asset generating real rental or profit income — not merely that it's labeled "Islamic" or that its yield resembles a conventional bond's. A sukuk that is structured so the "asset" is nominal and the cash flows are engineered to replicate a conventional loan in substance would fail Sharia compliance regardless of the label on the prospectus.

## The steelman

The strongest critique of sukuk, made by serious Islamic-finance scholars themselves (not just outside critics): "many sukuk structures use a purchase-undertaking mechanism that guarantees the issuer will buy back the underlying asset at a fixed, pre-agreed price at maturity — which, in economic substance, reproduces a guaranteed principal-plus-return exactly like a conventional bond, with the 'asset ownership' functioning as a legal formality rather than genuine risk-sharing." That's a real, ongoing debate inside Islamic finance, not a strawman — it's the "is it really asset-backed?" critique named in this module's plan. The falsifier: compare a sukuk al-ijarah with a genuine, tradable, income-producing underlying asset (a real office tower with market-rate rent that could rise or fall) against a sukuk where the "asset" is a thin formality wrapped around what is functionally a fixed-return loan — AAOIFI's own standards (Shariah Standard No. 17) have tightened over time specifically to push back on the latter pattern, which tells you the critique landed and the standard-setters are actively responding to it, not ignoring it.

## Takeaway

A sukuk's return can look like a bond's coupon, but what makes it non-riba is real asset ownership generating the payment — not the payment's size, and not the label on the prospectus.
