# Leveraged & inverse ETFs — the decay trap

A 2x leveraged ETF doesn't promise double the index's return over any period you choose — it promises double the index's return *for one single day*, then resets. That reset is the whole story: over multiple days, compounding a daily-reset multiple against a volatile, choppy market produces a result that can diverge sharply, and negatively, from "2x the index's actual multi-day move." After this lesson you'll be able to compute why a leveraged ETF can lose money even when its underlying index ends flat or even up.

## Example

Suppose an index starts at 100. Day 1 it rises 10% to 110. Day 2 it falls 10% (from the new base) back to 99 — down 1% from where it started over two days, a realistic round trip. A 2x leveraged ETF tracking that index resets daily: Day 1 it gains 20% (2 × 10%), Day 2 it loses 20% (2 × -10%). Starting at $100: Day 1 → $120. Day 2 → $120 × (1 − 0.20) = $96. The underlying index lost 1% over the two days; the "2x" ETF lost 4% — four times the loss, not two times, purely from daily compounding through a reversal. Run that same choppy back-and-forth pattern for weeks and a leveraged fund can bleed value steadily even while the underlying index goes nowhere. This is volatility decay (also called "beta slippage"): the more a market chops sideways with volatility, the worse daily-reset leverage performs relative to its stated multiple, independent of any long-term direction.

Real products carry explicit warnings about this: leveraged and inverse ETFs (tickers like TQQQ for 3x Nasdaq-100 exposure, or SQQQ for -3x) disclose in their prospectuses that they are designed to meet their stated multiple only over a single trading day, and that holding periods beyond that carry compounding risk that can produce outcomes far from what a naive "multiply the index return" calculation would suggest.

## The trap

Beginners see "2x S&P 500 ETF" and mentally model it as "if the S&P 500 is up 8% this year, I made 16%." It feels like simple multiplication should work. The correct mental model: the 2x multiple resets every single day, and daily-reset leverage compounds — in a choppy or declining market it compounds against you faster than the index moves, and even in a smoothly trending market the multi-day result is close to but not exactly double. These products are built and disclosed as short-term tactical or hedging tools, not buy-and-hold positions.

## The steelman

The case for using leveraged ETFs deliberately: a trader with a strong, short-term directional view — hours to a few days, not months — can use a leveraged ETF to express that view without the margin account, borrowing costs, or liquidation risk of an actual leveraged position. In a smoothly trending (low-volatility, one-direction) market over a short holding period, the daily-reset multiple tracks its target reasonably well — the decay problem is specifically a volatility-and-holding-period problem, not a leverage-is-always-broken problem. The falsifier: compare a leveraged ETF's realized multi-day return against its naive multiple, across both a smooth trending period and a choppy sideways period for the same underlying index. If the tracking holds up in the trend and breaks down in the chop, the steelman is right about *when* leverage works — which is precisely why these are short-holding-period tools, never long-term ones.

## Takeaway

A leveraged ETF's multiple is a promise about one day, not your holding period — the longer and choppier the hold, the further the result drifts from "just multiply the index."
