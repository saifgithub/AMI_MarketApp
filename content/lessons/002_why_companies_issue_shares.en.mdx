# Why companies issue shares

Companies sell shares for one reason: they need cash and they don't want to borrow it. Borrowing money creates debt you have to repay on a schedule, with interest, even in bad years. Selling shares is different — the company raises cash by giving permanent slices of itself to investors, and never has to pay that money back. The trade is straightforward: capital now, in exchange for handing over future ownership and some say in how the company is run. Knowing why a company issued shares tells you what kind of business you're buying into.

## How this plays out in real markets

When Amazon (AMZN) went public in May 1997, it sold 3 million shares at $18 each — raising $54 million in one day. That cash funded warehouses, hiring, and the technology that built the rest of the company. Amazon was unprofitable for years afterward. With debt, the bank would have demanded repayment. With equity, Amazon's shareholders waited. By 2026, AMZN trades around $180 (split-adjusted from $18 in 1997 with two stock splits). The early shareholders were paid in price appreciation, not interest.

The mechanism repeats today. When a company "issues shares," it can be doing one of three things: a fresh IPO (its first public sale), a secondary offering (selling new shares after it's already listed), or stock-based compensation (paying employees in shares rather than cash). Each one dilutes existing owners — there are now more total slices, so each one is a smaller piece of the pie.

On Bursa Malaysia: when MR D.I.Y. (MRDIY) listed in October 2020, it raised about RM1.5 billion to fund new stores and pay down debt. Same mechanic, ringgit instead of dollars.

## The trap

The common beginner mistake is assuming that when a company sells new shares, the stock price should automatically go up — "the company got more money, so it's worth more." It usually goes the other way. A secondary offering means there are now more shares chasing the same pile of future profits. If a company doubles its share count and nothing else changes, each share is worth roughly half as much. The cash raised has to produce *more* future value than the dilution costs to leave existing shareholders better off. Always ask: what is the company going to do with this money, and is that worth the slice of ownership they just gave away?

## Takeaway

Companies sell shares to raise cash they never have to repay. Every new share issued dilutes the existing owners, so always ask whether the cash will produce more value than the ownership given away.
