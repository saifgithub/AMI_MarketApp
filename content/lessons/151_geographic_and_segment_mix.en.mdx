# Geographic and segment revenue mix

Lesson 032 introduced segment concentration. This lesson extends it across two axes: *segments* (product lines, divisions) and *geographies* (regions, currencies). The same $400B revenue can carry very different risk depending on whether 60% comes from China or 60% comes from regulated US healthcare. Mix tells you which macro variables move the company — FX rates, tariffs, regional consumer demand, regulatory regimes. By the end of this lesson you should be able to break a company's revenue into mix components, identify the two or three macro factors it is most exposed to, and read a "currency-neutral" growth disclosure correctly.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at AAPL's fiscal 2024 revenue by geography: Americas ~$167B (43%), Europe ~$101B (26%), Greater China ~$67B (17%), Japan ~$26B (7%), Rest of Asia-Pacific ~$30B (7%). The Greater China line is the headline-sensitive one — a single news cycle about Chinese smartphone competition can move the stock 4–6% on a 17%-of-revenue concern. AAPL also reports revenue by segment: Products (~$295B, mostly iPhone) and Services (~$96B, much higher margin). The Services line has roughly doubled the gross margin of Products — so as Services grows from 20% to 25% of total revenue, the overall margin expands mechanically even if both segments individually do nothing. That's "mix shift" working in shareholders' favour.

The opposite happens at companies with declining high-margin segments. Look at any consumer-staples firm where regional emerging-market growth is faster than developed-market growth but EM margins are 600–800bps lower. The top line grows nicely in reported dollars; the blended margin compresses simply because the mix shifted toward the lower-margin region.

FX matters more than most beginners think. When a US multinational reports "revenue +3%, currency-neutral revenue +8%", the difference (5 percentage points) is FX drag from a stronger dollar. The same company will report a tailwind of equal magnitude when the dollar weakens. Constant-currency growth is the real economic comparison; reported growth includes a translation effect that has nothing to do with whether the business is healthier.

On Bursa Malaysia: GENTING's revenue mix straddles Malaysia, Singapore, the US (Resorts World Las Vegas, NY), and the UK. A weak USD/MYR rate inflates the reported MYR revenue from US operations without unit volume changing. Read constant-currency disclosure where available.

The falsifiable claim: when reported revenue and constant-currency revenue diverge by more than 300bps, the gap is FX and should be removed before comparing growth rates across years or peers.

## The trap

The trap is reading the consolidated revenue line as if the company were a single homogeneous business. A beginner sees "revenue +7%" and stops. They miss that one segment grew +25%, another shrank –10%, one geography boomed because the local currency strengthened, and another stalled because of a tariff shift. The correct mental model: revenue is a sum, and a sum hides which components moved. Always pull the segment table and the geographic table from the latest 10-K or 10-Q. Look at: (1) which segment is growing fastest and what % of revenue it represents now vs three years ago — that's the structural shift; (2) which geography exposes the company to the most volatile macro factor; (3) the difference between reported and constant-currency growth — that's how much of "growth" is just FX.

## Takeaway

Revenue is a sum that hides what moved. The mix between segments and geographies — and the difference between reported and constant-currency growth — is where the real business signal lives.
