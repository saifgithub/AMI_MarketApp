# Regime changes in real time vs in hindsight: false signals

Lessons 059–064 each defined a regime with a detection rule. In hindsight, every regime change looks obvious. In real time, every regime-change signal is a coin toss between "this is it" and "this is another head fake". This lesson teaches the synthesis: how to weigh confirmations, what false signals look like, and how to size while uncertain. After this lesson you will be able to name the three most common false-regime signals, name what distinguishes a real regime change from a head fake, and state the position-sizing rule for ambiguous regimes.

## How this plays out in real markets

Three high-profile false regime signals from recent history:

1) The August 2015 "regime change that wasn't": SPX closed below its 200-day SMA on 21 August 2015 at 1,971, the 50-day crossed below the 200-day on 28 August 2015, and the China-devaluation news cycle insisted "the bull is over". Real time, the bear-regime checklist had two of three markers firing (price below 200-day, near-death cross). In hindsight: SPX reclaimed the 200-day SMA on weekly basis by November 2015 and the bull resumed; the "bear" was a 12% correction inside a continuing trend.

2) The June 2016 Brexit head fake: SPX dropped 5.3% in 2 sessions after the 23 June 2016 vote. VIX spiked to 25.8. Headlines said "regime change". Real time: only one of three bear markers fired (no death cross, 200-day SMA not broken on weekly close). In hindsight: SPX bottomed 27 June 2016 and made new highs by 11 July 2016 — a 14-trading-day false bear.

3) The October 2018 bear that almost was: SPX closed below its 200-day SMA on 11 October 2018, the 50-day crossed below the 200-day on 7 December 2018, and the bear-regime checklist confirmed by mid-December 2018. In hindsight: SPX bottomed 24 December 2018 and reclaimed the 200-day SMA on 30 January 2019. Was it a "real" bear or a head fake? It depends on the timeframe — a 19.8% intraday drawdown that hit every bear marker still feels like a regime change but reversed inside 90 days.

Detection rule for distinguishing real-time regime change from false signal: a regime change is *more likely real* when (a) the detection cluster from the relevant lesson is fully confirmed (3-of-3, not 2-of-3), (b) breadth confirms (per lesson 064 — % of stocks above 200-day SMA moves in the regime-consistent direction by >10 points), (c) the signal survives 4 weeks without invalidation. Head fakes typically fail 1 or 2 of those three filters.

The honest invalidation rule: a regime call is INVALIDATED if the original detection cluster reverses (e.g., 200-day reclaimed on weekly basis for a bear call). When a head fake happens, you take the small loss from the compressed-mandate size, re-classify, and continue.

## The trap

The hindsight trap is the most damaging cognitive distortion in regime analysis. Reading any historical chart, the regime changes "look obvious" — but a regime-change call is decided in the days before the cluster fully confirms, when the data is genuinely ambiguous. The trader who studies 2008 charts and concludes "I would have caught it" usually under-estimates how the 2015, 2016, and 2018 false signals would have shaken them out.

The correct model: regime calls in real time are probabilistic, not binary. The right behaviour is to size positions for the *uncertainty*, not for the regime-call confidence. Two-of-three markers firing = compressed mandate sizing; three-of-three confirmed = regime-appropriate sizing; survives 4 weeks = full regime conviction. Accept that some calls will be head fakes; the discipline is in the sizing, not the calling.

## Takeaway

In real time, regime calls are probabilistic. Size for the cluster — two markers = compressed <Term id="ami_mandate" />, three confirmed = full regime sizing, surviving four weeks = full conviction — and accept head fakes as the cost of acting before hindsight clarifies the picture.
