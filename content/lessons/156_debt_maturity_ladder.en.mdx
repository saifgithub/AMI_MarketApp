# Debt maturity ladder and refinancing risk

Lesson 034 introduced debt-to-EBITDA and interest coverage. This lesson adds the *time dimension*. Two companies with identical debt levels can carry very different risk profiles depending on *when* the debt matures and at *what* coupon. Debt due next year at 4% becomes debt due next year at 8% if refinanced into a high-rate environment — and the resulting jump in interest expense can crush an otherwise healthy company. By the end of this lesson you should know how to read a debt maturity schedule, identify a refinancing wall, and quantify the interest-expense risk it represents.

## How this plays out in real markets

Every 10-K includes a debt maturity table in the notes — typically titled "Long-term debt" or "Contractual obligations". It shows the principal coming due each year for the next five years and "thereafter". A healthy ladder is *staggered* — roughly equal amounts maturing each year, so the company refinances small tranches at a time. A risky ladder is *bunched* — a cliff of maturities in a single year that requires bulk refinancing.

Real example: in 2023–2024, several commercial real estate companies faced "maturity walls" where 30–40% of total debt was scheduled to refinance within a 24-month window. The debt had been issued at 3–4% coupons in the 2020–2021 ultra-low-rate era; rolling it at then-prevailing 7–9% commercial mortgage rates roughly doubled the interest expense on the rolled tranche. For a company with debt-to-EBITDA around 5x and interest coverage around 2x at the old rate, refinancing the same principal at double the coupon mathematically pushes interest coverage below 1x — the textbook stress configuration from lesson 034.

For a healthy ladder: MSFT's long-term debt of ~$42B in fiscal 2024 was spread across maturities running out beyond 2050, with weighted-average coupon under 4% and no single year representing more than 8% of total debt outstanding. The refinancing risk is essentially zero — the company can let any given tranche mature and either refinance or repay from cash.

The interest-rate sensitivity calculation: if a company has $5B of debt maturing in the next 12 months at an existing 4% coupon and the new market rate for similar credit is 7.5%, the incremental annual interest expense is $5B × (7.5% – 4%) = $175M. Compare that $175M to the company's operating income to see how much of EPS is at risk before the refinancing happens.

On Bursa Malaysia: REITs on Bursa typically disclose detailed maturity schedules; KLCC and IGB REIT both stagger their financing across multiple instruments and tenors specifically to avoid wall risk. The disclosures are in the annual report notes — required reading before sizing a position.

The falsifiable claim: a company with >25% of total debt maturing in any single 12-month window during a high-rate environment will see its forward interest coverage compress materially within two reporting cycles. The compression is predictable arithmetic, not a guess.

## The trap

The trap is reading "total debt $5B" without reading the maturity schedule. A beginner checks debt-to-EBITDA, sees a comfortable 3x, and stops. They miss that $2B of the $5B matures in the next 18 months at a coupon that may have to double on refinancing. The same debt-to-EBITDA ratio that looked fine carries a hidden +30% jump in interest expense baked in to the forward year. The correct mental model: debt has two co-equal dimensions — *amount* (which lesson 034 covered) and *timing* (which is this lesson). Both must be assessed. A company with a clean staggered ladder can carry meaningfully more debt than a peer with a wall; a peer with a wall can be stressed at a much smaller absolute level.

Identify the largest single-year maturity in the next 5 years as a percentage of total debt. Estimate the new interest expense if that tranche refinances at current market rates." ## Takeaway

Debt has an amount and a calendar. Read both before you trust the ratios — a clean leverage number with a maturity wall behind it is more dangerous than a higher number that's staggered out.
