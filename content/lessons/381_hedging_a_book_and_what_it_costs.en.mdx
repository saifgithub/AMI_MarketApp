# Hedging a book, and what a hedge actually costs

A hedge is not protection you buy; it is a second position whose risks are different from the first one's rather than absent. After this lesson you will be able to size a market hedge from your portfolio's beta, and — more usefully — compute in advance how much of your risk such a hedge can possibly remove, which for many books is far less than the word "hedged" implies.

## Example

An openly hypothetical simulated book of **$100,000** with a portfolio beta of **1.20**. Portfolio beta is the weighted average of the holdings' betas, so this is a book that has typically moved 1.2% for each 1% of the market.

**Size the hedge.** Beta 1.20 on $100,000 is an index-equivalent exposure of **$120,000**. A 10% index decline therefore implies roughly **−12%**, or **−$12,000**, on the book. To neutralise the market exposure entirely you would need a short index position of **$120,000**; to bring the book's beta down to 0.85 you would remove 0.35 of beta, which is **$35,000** of index-equivalent short. That is the whole sizing calculation, and it is the part people find hard and shouldn't.

**Now the part people find easy and shouldn't.** A market hedge removes only the market-linked portion of your risk, which is exactly the quantity R² measures in <Lesson id="378_beta_and_capm_what_beta_is_not"/>. Perfectly hedging the market leaves residual volatility of σ × √(1 − R²). Using the two holdings from that lesson:

| holding | R² | volatility | after a perfect market hedge | risk removed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steady | 0.90 | 2.68% | **0.84%** | **68.8%** |
| Story | 0.09 | 7.08% | **6.75%** | **4.6%** |

Read the Story row twice. A textbook-perfect beta hedge, executed with no slippage, on the more volatile of the two holdings, removes **4.6%** of its risk. The other 95% was never market risk, so nothing done to the market position could touch it. Hedging the wrong risk perfectly is still hedging the wrong risk.

**And the cost.** A market hedge is symmetric: the same short that removes 12% of downside removes 12% of upside. There is no version in which you keep the gains. On top of that comes **basis risk** — the index you can short is not your book, so the hedge and the holdings diverge — plus financing and borrow costs, plus the possibility of being right about direction and losing on the difference between the two.

**In AMI specifically.** If your Mandate has `long_only` on — which is the default — the Chief Investment Officer's safety floor blocks a sell against a position you do not hold, because that opens a short. So the hedge described above cannot be placed in the simulator with that flag set, and AMI has no options or futures to place it with instead. That is deliberate: this is a training simulator, and hedging is taught here as literacy so you can read and evaluate a hedged book, not because there is a hedge to execute.

## The trap

The trap is treating "hedged" as a state rather than as a second position with its own profit and loss. The word does most of the damage: a hedged book sounds like a book with the risk taken out, so the natural next move is to size the underlying position more aggressively — which is how a hedge that removes 4.6% of the risk ends up funding a doubling of the exposure.

The correct mental model: you now hold two positions and your risk is whatever the pair does together. Some of the original risk is gone, some is untouched, and a new risk — the difference between your holdings and the instrument you hedged with — has been added. Whether that trade is worthwhile is a question with a numerical answer, and the number to compute before placing it is R². If your book's R² against the hedging instrument is low, the honest description of the hedge is that it is a market bet sitting alongside your holdings, not protection for them.

## The steelman

The strongest case for hedging anyway, even at low R²: risk is not the only thing being managed. A holder who would sell everything in a 20% drawdown, at the bottom, does better with an imperfect hedge that keeps them in their positions than with an unhedged book they abandon — the hedge is bought against a behavioural failure, not a statistical one, and 4.6% of variance is not the metric that decides it. That is a real argument and it is the honest reason many hedges exist. What it does not do is license the mislabelling: the position should be described as what it is, so the cost is priced against the right benefit. The falsifier is available from your own Decision Journal rather than from theory. Look at what you actually did in previous drawdowns. If you held your positions through them, the behavioural case does not apply to you and the R² arithmetic is the whole story. If you sold at the lows, the case applies, and the hedge's job is to change that behaviour — in which case the thing to track afterwards is whether it did, not whether it made money.

## Try it

Estimate your simulated book's beta by taking a weighted average of your holdings' betas — ask the Technical Strategist in a 1-on-1 for each one. Then ask the harder question about the same holdings: how much of their movement is market-linked at all? Multiply your book's value by its beta to see the index-equivalent exposure a hedge would have to cover, then use the R² figures to estimate how much of your risk such a hedge could reach. If the answer is a small fraction, you have learned something about your book that no hedge would have taught you. Note that with `long_only` set in your Mandate the trade itself is blocked by the safety floor — the exercise is the arithmetic, not the order.

## Takeaway

Size a hedge with beta, but decide whether to place it with R² — a perfect hedge of a risk your book does not mostly carry is an expensive way to hold a market view.

*Where this comes from: the systematic-versus-specific risk split at the centre of Sharpe's CAPM — Capital Asset Prices (1964).*
