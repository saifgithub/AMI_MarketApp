# Expected value — the core of every decision

Expected value (EV) is the probability-weighted average of every possible outcome of a decision, and it is the single number every other quant tool in this module builds on. EV = Σ pᵢ·xᵢ — multiply each outcome by its probability, then add the products. A trade, a bet, or a strategy is only "good" in the long-run sense if its EV is positive; a high win rate alone tells you nothing without knowing what you win and lose each time. Here's the part that trips people up: a positive-EV decision can still lose money on any single try — EV describes the long-run average, not any one outcome.

## Example

Say a setup wins 60% of the time for +$500, and loses the other 40% of the time for -$300. EV = (0.6 × $500) + (0.4 × -$300) = $300 - $120 = **+$180 per trade**. Take this setup 100 times and the *average* outcome per trade converges toward +$180 — roughly +$18,000 total, though any individual run will scatter around that number.

Now look at what EV does NOT promise: on any single instance of this trade, there's still a **40% chance of losing $300** — that's the p=0.4 term in the same equation, not a separate risk lurking outside it. A trader who takes this setup once and loses is not "unlucky in a way that disproves the edge" — a 40% loss rate is priced into the +$180 EV from the start. Convene the Room on a name like NVDA and look at the Trader's proposed size next to the Bull and Bear cases: a well-run size assumes the loss scenario is a normal, expected outcome of a positive-EV process, not a failure of the process.

## The trap

The trap is judging a single outcome instead of the process that produced it. A trader takes the +$180-EV setup once, loses the $300, and concludes "that edge doesn't work" — abandoning a genuinely positive-EV process because one draw from a 40%-probability branch happened to land. The correct mental model: EV is a claim about the *average* over many repetitions, not a prediction about the next one. Judge a decision by whether its EV was positive going in, not by whether that particular instance won.

## Takeaway

A decision's quality is set by its expected value — the probability-weighted average of every outcome — not by whether any single instance of it happened to win or lose.
