# Interest coverage — can EBIT pay the rent on the debt?

Lesson 042 flagged interest coverage as one of the two follow-up checks after debt-to-equity. This lesson goes deep. Interest coverage = EBIT / interest expense. It answers a more direct question than net debt / EBITDA: how many times can current operating earnings pay the interest bill before the company misses a payment? A ratio of 1.0x means every dollar of EBIT goes straight to interest; 10.0x means there's a fat cushion. By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute it, name the rating-agency bands, and recognise the precise condition that turns a "comfortable" coverage number into a fragile one.

## How this plays out in real markets

MSFT in fiscal 2024 reported EBIT around $109B against interest expense near $2.9B. Interest coverage = 109 / 2.9 ≈ 38x. That number is functionally infinite — any plausible EBIT decline still leaves a comfortable interest cushion. AAPL: EBIT ~$120B, interest expense ~$3.8B, coverage ~32x. Both are AAA-tier on this metric alone.

Compare a heavily indebted name. Whirlpool (WHR) in 2024 reported EBIT near $1.3B against interest expense near $370M. Coverage = 1.3 / 0.37 ≈ 3.5x. That's the lower end of investment-grade — rating agencies typically call sub-3.0x speculative-grade territory, and Whirlpool's bonds trade at corresponding spreads. The same business in a recession year where EBIT falls 40% would see coverage drop to 0.78 / 0.37 ≈ 2.1x, well into junk territory without any new debt being issued. The denominator was stable; the numerator did the damage.

The classic distress example: a company with coverage of 2.0x and floating-rate debt sees the central-bank policy rate rise 200 basis points. Interest expense rises proportionally on the floating portion; if 50% of debt is floating, the interest line jumps about 25%. Coverage drops from 2.0x to 1.6x without the business changing at all — the macro cycle moved the ratio toward default. That's why interest coverage and rate exposure (lesson 191 on fixed vs floating) are joint-not-separate concerns.

On Bursa Malaysia: TENAGA's interest coverage runs around 3–4x, normal for a regulated utility with high but predictable debt. AIRASIA's coverage went negative during COVID — EBIT was negative while interest expense stayed positive, the textbook pre-default signature. Coverage below 1.0x for more than two consecutive quarters is rarely survivable without capital injection or restructuring.

Falsifiable claim: a non-financial, non-utility company with interest coverage below 3.0x AND floating-rate debt above 30% of total debt is a rate-cycle vulnerability that should override most equity-side bull cases. Either condition alone might be tolerable; both together is the specific pattern that takes companies from healthy to distressed in a tightening cycle.

## The trap

The trap is using net income or EPS as the numerator instead of EBIT. Net income is *after* interest, so it understates the cushion — and worse, in a near-default scenario where interest expense balloons, net income can go negative while EBIT is still positive, making the ratio uncomputable or absurdly negative. EBIT is the right numerator because it's the cash that exists before debt holders take their share. The correct mental model: interest coverage measures pre-debt-service cushion, not after-tax profit. What invalidates the standard reading: companies with material capitalised interest (e.g., long-construction industries like utilities or REITs) report a lower interest expense on the income statement than they're actually paying — always cross-check with cash interest paid from the cash-flow statement.

## Takeaway

Interest coverage is the rent-payment check on the debt stack. A 3.0x ratio is the rough investment-grade floor — but the meaningful number is the stressed ratio after a plausible rate or earnings shock, not the calm-day headline.
