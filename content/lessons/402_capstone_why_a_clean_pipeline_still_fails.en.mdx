# Capstone: why a clean pipeline still fails

You can now build the whole thing: standardise each signal, subtract the cross-sectional mean, strip what the factors already explain, weight by the inverse of volatility, and normalise so the absolute weights sum to one. Every step is defensible. The pipeline is still, in most hands, disappointing — and the reasons are worth more than the recipe.

## Three reasons a correct pipeline underdelivers

**One: the step that removes the market can remove the signal with it.** Cross-sectional demeaning is the step that turns "this name rose" into "this name rose more than its peers". If a signal's predictive power came from knowing what the whole market was about to do, that power lives entirely in the part the step deletes.

This is not a hypothetical. AMI ran exactly this test on 32 large-cap US equities across 2,891 trading dates — 92,512 observations, walked forward by date. Predicting each name's own direction, the model scored **0.5528** on a scale where 0.5 is a coin flip. Re-run to predict whether a name would beat the cross-sectional median instead, the same features on the same data scored **0.5055**, with a p-value of **0.62** — indistinguishable from chance.

| Question asked | Score | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Will this name rise? | 0.5528 | above chance |
| Will it beat its peers? | 0.5055 | not distinguishable |

The diagnosis was visible in which inputs mattered most: market-wide volatility measures. They describe what every name is doing at once, so they cannot discriminate between two names. That is our result, on that universe, over that window, with those inputs. A different universe, a longer horizon or better data can give a different answer — and the honest reading is that this was a test of our data, not a law about markets.

**Two: the output is far harder to evaluate than it is to build.** For an asset with 20% annualised volatility, the uncertainty in a measured average return is roughly σ divided by the square root of the years observed, while the uncertainty in measured volatility falls much faster:

| Data available | Uncertainty in average return | Uncertainty in volatility |
|---|---|---|
| 3 months | **40.0 pp** | 1.78 pp |
| 1 year | 20.0 pp | 0.89 pp |
| 5 years | 8.9 pp | 0.40 pp |

Three months of data pins down how risky a book is to within about two points and says essentially nothing about what it earns. This is the arithmetic behind <Lesson id="339_sample_size_and_statistical_significance"/>, and it does not improve with better modelling — only with more years.

**Three: a better model on thin data is still thin.** In the same study, ordinary logistic regression matched gradient boosting, and combining models added 0.003. When the simplest method ties the most sophisticated one, the ceiling is in the data, not the algorithm — which is why <Lesson id="342_overfitting_and_curve_fitting"/> matters more than model selection.

## The steelman

The strongest objection: this reads as an argument for giving up, and it is not one. Everything above is a description of what is hard to *measure*, not proof that nothing is there. The pipeline's individual steps remain correct — standardisation really does make signals comparable, orthogonalisation really does prevent double-counting, and inverse-volatility weighting really does stop a noisy name dominating a book. None of that is undone by a disappointing result on one universe.

What changes is the burden of proof. A pipeline that produces a number is not the same as a pipeline that produces an edge, and the gap between them is filled by evidence you have to go and get — out-of-sample, on data the method never saw, over a window long enough for the answer to mean something. The falsifier is clean: if a signal survives cross-sectional demeaning on data it was not built on, it is discriminating between names, and the objection has been answered on its own terms.

## Takeaway

Every step of the standard pipeline is defensible and none of them manufactures an edge — the step that removes the market can remove the signal too, the returns it produces take years to measure while its risk takes months, and a correct construction is a starting point for evidence rather than a substitute for it.

*Where this comes from: the precision-of-performance-measurement treatment in Bodie, Kane & Marcus, Investments, and AMI's own cross-sectional study on 32 US large-caps over 2,891 trading dates.*
