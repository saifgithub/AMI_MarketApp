# Journal review as a discipline forcing function

A <Term id="ami_decision_journal" /> that is only written-to and never read-from is theater, not discipline. The forcing function that actually changes behaviour is the *review* — a scheduled, structured re-reading of your own past trades against your written rules. After this lesson you will be able to design a weekly review cadence that surfaces your specific failure modes, name the three audit lenses every review should run, and configure the journal so the review compounds across months rather than restarting from zero each week. The trader who reviews finishes the year with a different P&L than the trader who only writes — the same trades, different outcomes, because the review changes the next trade.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader instituted a 30-minute Sunday review starting February 2024. Each week he ran three audit lenses across the prior 5 sessions' trades: (1) per-trade risk drift versus <Term id="ami_mandate" />, (2) checklist compliance versus actual entries, (3) timing of entries relative to closed losses (cooldown audit). The first review (week of Feb 4) surfaced 3 mandate-edge trades, 2 unqualified entries, and 1 entry inside the cooldown window. He documented each, set a Mandate parameter to harden the rule that had been bypassed (changed cooldown enforcement from per-ticker to per-trader), and re-read the prior week's journal entry the following Sunday.

By August, the review had compounded: each Sunday's audit referenced not just the prior week but the prior month's pattern, and each new mandate hardening was logged with a date and the trade that triggered it. By October, his unqualified trade count was down 78% week-over-week from the February baseline. His annualised return through October was approximately 22% higher than his prior 12-month average on the same strategy. He had not added any new strategies. He had only stopped repeating his cheapest mistakes — which the review made visible, week by week.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that the journal is a write-only diary. It is not. A journal that is only written-to is a memory aid — useful but slow. A journal that is *reviewed* is a feedback loop, and feedback loops are the only mechanism by which a trader's behaviour materially changes over time. The correct mental model: the journal entry is the data; the review is the analysis; the mandate update is the action. Most traders complete step one and skip steps two and three. The single highest-leverage 30 minutes in a trader's week is not researching the next trade — it is reading the last 5 trades against the rules they were supposed to follow. The Portfolio Manager's role in this is structural: every review should end with a question to the PM in the form "given what this review surfaced, what mandate parameter should change?" If the answer is "none," the review confirmed the rules are working. If the answer is a specific parameter, the review just earned its 30 minutes.

## Try it

Open Settings and schedule a recurring weekly review (Sunday evening or Saturday morning works for most traders). Then open the Decision Journal, filter to the last 5 sessions, and run the three audit lenses: per-trade risk drift versus mandate, checklist compliance versus actual entries, cooldown timing. End the review with one explicit conclusion — either a mandate parameter change with a date and trigger trade, or a "no change required" entry. Then open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Portfolio Manager and ask: "Given this week's audit, what mandate parameter should change?" The answer is the review's output, not its input.

## Takeaway

The journal entry is the data; the review is the analysis; the mandate update is the action. The 30 minutes of weekly review compounds across the year more reliably than any single piece of research.
