# Correlation ≠ causation

Correlation measures how closely two things move together; causation means one of them actually makes the other happen. Two series can move together almost perfectly and still have no causal link between them at all — because a third, unmentioned variable is driving both. Treating "these two things moved together" as "one caused the other" is one of the fastest ways to build a trading thesis on a coincidence.

## Example

Airline stocks and energy-sector names like XOM often show a strong negative correlation over multi-month stretches — when oil prices fall, airline fuel costs drop and airline margins improve, while energy-producer revenue and equity prices fall with the same oil price. It looks, on a chart, like "airlines moving up causes energy stocks to move down" or vice versa. It doesn't: neither stock is causing the other's move. The third variable — the oil price itself — is driving both series in opposite directions at the same time. An investor who buys airlines "because energy is falling" has the right correlation and the wrong mechanism; the trade only works for as long as the oil-price link holds, and breaks the moment airlines and energy move together instead (e.g., during a demand shock that hits both sectors at once).

Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths show a similar pattern seasonally: both rise together every summer, not because ice cream causes drowning, but because hot weather drives more of both swimming and ice-cream buying. The correlation is real and the causal story implied by it is entirely wrong.

## The trap

The trap is spotting a correlation on a chart and immediately building a causal story to explain it, because a causal story is more satisfying than "these two things happen to move together for a reason I haven't identified yet." It feels right because a plausible-sounding mechanism ("airlines and energy move opposite because they're linked") is easy to invent after the fact. The correct habit: before trading a correlation, ask what the actual causal mechanism is and what third variable might be driving both series — if you can't name the mechanism, you're trading a coincidence that can break without warning.

## Try it

Pick two tickers from different sectors that you've noticed moving together on a chart. In 1-on-1 with the Research Manager, ask it to name a plausible third variable that could be driving both, rather than asking whether one causes the other. Remember: AMI's training output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

A strong correlation tells you two things move together; it never tells you why — find the actual mechanism, or a shared third variable, before trading the relationship.
