# Overconfidence is the mechanism

<Lesson id="367_why_activity_costs_you_before_costs"/> showed that activity costs you before your picks even get judged. This lesson is about why people trade that much in the first place. The leading explanation, and the one Barber and Odean built an entire study to test directly rather than assume, is <Term id="overconfidence"/> — not as a personality flaw, but as a measurable excess of trading relative to what a trader's actual information justifies.

## How this plays out in real markets

Using the same discount-brokerage dataset as the turnover study, Barber and Odean partitioned over 35,000 households by gender specifically because psychology research finds men self-report higher confidence in their own ability than women do, on average, even when performance is comparable. If overconfidence drives overtrading, the more overconfident group should trade more and earn less on a risk-adjusted basis. It did: men traded 45% more than women and earned annual risk-adjusted net returns 1.4 percentage points lower. Narrow the sample to single people — removing any "one spouse handles the research" explanation — and the gap widens: single men traded 67% more than single women and earned 2.3 points less per year.

A second, related mechanism shows up in what traders do once they're already in a position. Odean examined 10,000 accounts and 163,000 trades over six years and computed, for every month, the ratio of two numbers: the share of paper gains that got sold (realized) versus the share of paper losses that got sold. For every month except December, that ratio sat around 1.5 — a position sitting on a gain was more than 50% more likely to be sold on any given day than a position sitting on a loss. In December the pattern flips: tax-loss selling pushes more losers out the door than winners. That reversal matters, because it shows investors *can* act rationally on losers when the incentive is strong enough — the other eleven months aren't an inability to sell a loser, they're a preference not to.

Put the two findings together and you get a mechanism, not just a habit: overconfidence explains why you trade so much — you believe your read is sharper than it is — and the disposition effect explains what you then do with each position once you're in it: cut the winner early because it already "worked," hold the loser because admitting it's wrong feels worse than waiting.

## The trap

Overconfidence doesn't feel like overconfidence from the inside — it feels like conviction. The natural response to "I might be overconfident" is to hunt for evidence you're not, rather than run a test that could actually settle it. The disposition effect compounds this: because you sell winners fast and hold losers long, your *closed* trade record looks better than your *open* one — you can feel like a 1.5-to-1 favorite while measuring only the trades you were most eager to lock in. This is the same trap from a different angle as <Lesson id="047_revenge_trading"/>: holding a loser hoping it turns is the mirror image of sizing up into the next trade to make a loss back. Both are refusals to let a closed loss be closed.

## Try it

Pull up your open <Term id="simulated_trade"/> positions right now. For each one sitting on a loss, ask honestly: would you buy it today, at today's price, if you didn't already own it? A no answer means you're holding it for a reason unrelated to its merit as a trade — the disposition effect, live, in your own account. ## Takeaway

Overconfidence and the disposition effect aren't two separate bad habits — they're the same mechanism seen from two angles. One inflates how much you trade; the other distorts which positions you close and when. Both were measured against hundreds of thousands of trades, and both survive the rational-explanation tests the researchers built specifically to rule them out.
