# Steelmanning the other side

A thesis you have never argued against is a thesis you have only rehearsed. Steelmanning is the discipline of building the strongest available case against your own position — the version a sharp opponent would sign their name to — and then checking whether your thesis survives it. The weak version, the one you dismiss in a sentence, teaches you nothing; knocking it down proves only that you can knock down a bad argument. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to construct an opposing case rigorous enough that the other side would endorse it, and name the evidence that would settle the disagreement either way.

## Example

Take a hypothetical mid-cap industrial — no real ticker, invented figures, so what you're grading is the reasoning rather than the numbers. You hold a simulated 6% position entered at $50. Your thesis: a new plant coming online lifts gross margin from 28% to 33% over two years, and the multiple holds.

The strawman bear case: *"it's already run, so it's expensive."* You dispatch that in one line — the move is exactly what a margin story predicts, and price alone is not an argument. You feel tested. You were not tested.

The steelman: the 5-point margin gain is a capacity effect, not a moat. Two competitors have announced capex to match it; if half the gain is competed away inside the two-year window, you get 2.5 points, not 5 — margin lands near 30.5%. And the backlog underwriting the plant is 61% concentrated in two customers whose contracts renew in 18 months, which is before your thesis is due to pay off. Same conclusion as the strawman — *this is worth less than you think* — but now it carries a mechanism, a magnitude, and a clock.

The falsifier makes it usable: the next annual report's customer-concentration disclosure, and the competitors' announced commissioning dates. If concentration drops materially and competitor capacity slips past the full two-year thesis window — not merely past the 18-month renewal date, which would still leave the margin gain competed away inside your holding period — the bear case weakens on its own terms. If it doesn't, yours does. Notice which of the two cases you could argue, and which you could only dismiss.

## The trap

The trap is testing your thesis against the weakest objection available. It feels like rigour and delivers reassurance, which is why it is the most efficient form confirmation bias takes — you did the work of arguing, and arrived exactly where you started. The correct standard is not *"can I answer this objection?"* but *"would a bear read my version of their case and say yes, that's mine"*. If you can't state it that well, you don't hold a thesis; you hold a conclusion plus the reasons you happened to encounter first. Remember also that someone sold you the share. The other side's argument is already in the price — steelmanning is how you find out what it was. <Lesson id="275_when_agents_disagree"/> covers how to read that opposition when the Room supplies it; this lesson is about supplying it yourself, so you can tell whether the Room's version was any good.

## Try it

Open Convene the Room on a watchlist ticker and read only the Bull Researcher's case. Before scrolling to the Bear Researcher's section, write your own bear case in the Decision Journal — mechanism, magnitude, and the evidence that would settle it. Then compare. If the Bear Researcher's version is stronger than yours, you were holding a strawman. Remember: AMI is a training simulator, and the verdict is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

If you cannot state the opposing case well enough that its holders would endorse it, you don't have a thesis — you have a conclusion. Argue the strongest version, then name the evidence that would settle it either way.
