# Revenge trading

Revenge trading is what happens when a loss stops being information and starts being a debt the market owes you. After this lesson you will be able to detect the pattern in your own behaviour using an objective test, understand why the next trade after a loss is statistically your worst, and commit to a self-imposed cooldown — tracked in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, not enforced by the app — that pulls the trigger out of your hand for the window that matters. The market does not owe you anything. The position you are about to put on does not know you just lost.

## How this plays out in real markets

On Bursa Malaysia, a trader stopped out of TENAGA at a -RM1,200 loss on a Monday morning. Within 40 minutes he opened a 2x-sized long on TENAGA again, "to make it back". That position stopped out at -RM2,400 before noon. By 3pm he had a 3x position on GENTING, a name he had not researched, because "TENAGA owes me and so does the whole index". GENTING closed -4.1% on the day. Total loss for the session: RM5,800 from an original risk budget of RM1,200. The next morning, calm again, he reviewed the chart and confirmed his original TENAGA exit was correct. He had spent four hours and an extra RM4,600 disagreeing with a decision he would later agree with.

The falsifiable pattern, written as an objective test: you are revenge-trading if your last three entries were all opened within 60 minutes of a closed loss, were sized at least 1.5x your written per-trade risk, and at least two of them were in the same instrument or sector as the original loser. Two of those three boxes ticked, in one session, is a tilt signature. The chart has nothing to do with it.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that the next trade is independent of the last one. Mechanically it is — your broker does not know you lost. Behaviourally it is not. After a loss, three things go up simultaneously: position size (you want the loss back faster), trade frequency (you stop waiting for setups), and conviction (you confuse urgency with edge). The Conservative Debator's job is to call out exactly this configuration, because every box in the size/frequency/conviction triangle is *correlated to the loss, not to the setup*. The correct mental model: a loss is a tax on the last trade, not a credit toward the next one. The market does not balance ledgers on your timeline. The only way to "make it back" is to not trade for a while.

## Try it

Pick a cooldown length for yourself — 60 minutes, 90 if you can stomach it — and write it down as a rule (AMI doesn't have a field for this; it isn't app-enforced, you enforce it). Then open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, filter to your last five stop-outs, and check what you did in the 60 minutes after each. If three of those windows contain an entry, you have a documented tilt pattern. Take it to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and paste the timestamps — let it tell you what its read is.

## Takeaway

A loss is a tax on the last trade, not a credit toward the next one. The cooldown is the only rule that protects you from the version of yourself the loss just created — even though you're the one who has to hold yourself to it.
