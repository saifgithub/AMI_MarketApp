# Off-balance-sheet liabilities

<Lesson id="034_debt_and_the_balance_sheet"/> covered the debt line on the balance sheet. This lesson covers the obligations that are *not* on it but behave like debt anyway. Operating leases used to live almost entirely off the balance sheet until accounting rules changed in 2019; pension obligations, purchase commitments, contingent liabilities, and certain joint-venture guarantees still hide in the footnotes. A company that looks lightly leveraged on the headline balance sheet can carry obligations equal to or larger than its on-balance-sheet debt if you don't read the footnotes. By the end of this lesson you should be able to find these obligations, estimate their debt-equivalent burden, and recompute debt-to-EBITDA on an adjusted basis.

## How this plays out in real markets

The four categories worth knowing:

**1. Operating leases (now mostly on-balance-sheet post-ASC 842 / IFRS 16).** Under current rules, both finance and operating leases appear as right-of-use assets and lease liabilities. But the *current portion* and lease *commitments* schedule still warrants reading. A large retailer or airline can have lease liabilities equal to or larger than its bonded debt.

Real example: WMT in fiscal 2025 had long-term debt of about $44B and operating lease liabilities of about $14B — roughly a 30% addition to the debt picture. UAL similarly carries aircraft lease obligations of tens of billions that an investor focused only on the "long-term debt" line would underweight.

**2. Pension and other post-employment benefit (OPEB) obligations.** A defined-benefit pension plan with a funded status of "underfunded by $X billion" is effectively long-duration debt the company must service. Look at the projected benefit obligation (PBO) less plan assets. Several legacy industrial and auto companies carry pension underfunding of $5–20B that doesn't appear on the headline debt line.

Real example: F (Ford) historically has carried global pension and OPEB obligations of $30B+. The funded status has swung between underfunded and roughly fully funded over the past decade as interest rates have moved. The economic obligation is real even when not labelled "debt".

**3. Purchase commitments and take-or-pay contracts.** Companies sometimes contract to buy a fixed quantity of supplies (commodities, components, capacity) over multiple years. The notes disclose the commitment schedule. These behave like debt when the commitment is firm and the contracted price is above market.

**4. Contingent liabilities — litigation, environmental, tax disputes.** Disclosed in footnotes with management's estimate of probable loss range. Large environmental remediation obligations at integrated oil and chemical companies routinely run into the billions.

The diagnostic: compute "adjusted debt" = reported debt + operating lease liabilities + unfunded pension/OPEB + probable contingent liabilities. Then divide by EBITDAR (EBITDA + rent expense) for lease-heavy industries, or by standard EBITDA otherwise. The adjusted ratio is often 30–80% higher than the reported one.

On Bursa Malaysia: AIRASIA's pre-COVID aircraft lease commitments and PETRONAS Chemicals' long-term feedstock purchase obligations are illustrative — both substantial obligations that don't appear cleanly on the headline debt line but bind the company economically.

The falsifiable claim: when adjusted debt-to-EBITDA exceeds reported debt-to-EBITDA by more than 1.5 turns, the headline ratio materially understates the company's true leverage profile.

## The trap

The trap is computing debt ratios from the balance sheet headline alone. A beginner pulls "total debt" from a financial database, divides by EBITDA, gets a comfortable 2.5x, and moves on. They miss that an airline has $12B of lease obligations or a retailer has $14B of store-lease commitments or a legacy industrial has $8B of pension underfunding. The correct mental model: every obligation that requires future cash payment behaves like debt for the purpose of leverage analysis, regardless of where the accountants put it. Read the contractual obligations table in the 10-K — it consolidates lease commitments, purchase commitments, debt repayments, and pension contributions on a single page in standardised columns out to five years and beyond.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, ask: "Pull the contractual obligations table from [ticker]'s latest 10-K. Sum total contractual obligations across all categories. Compare to reported total debt. Compute an adjusted debt-to-EBITDA." Save both ratios — reported and adjusted — to your <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />. The gap between them is the size of the hidden leverage at that company.

## Takeaway

If it has to be paid in cash, it behaves like debt — wherever the accountants put it. Read the footnotes and adjust the leverage number before you call a balance sheet clean.
