# Portfolio-wide panic selling

Selling one position out of fear is a single-position mistake. Liquidating the *book* on the same morning is a structural one — and it is the move most likely to convert a survivable drawdown into a year you cannot recover from inside a calendar. After this lesson you will be able to detect the multi-position fear signature, distinguish a real risk-off rotation from a portfolio-wide panic exit, and use the PM's mandate cap as the only authority for liquidation decisions made under stress. The cost of single-stock fear is a stop-out. The cost of portfolio panic is a calendar year.

## How this plays out in real markets

March 12, 2020. A trader held six US large-caps: AAPL, MSFT, GOOGL, NVDA, AMZN, JPM. Total account drawdown over eight sessions: -23%. His mandate max drawdown was 25%. He had no written rule for "what to do at -23%." That morning, with the futures limit-down and a global lockdown headline, he submitted six market sell orders in roughly four minutes. The fills cleared his book at an aggregate -27% account drawdown — past his mandate cap, executed by his own hand, not by the PM. The S&P 500 bottomed nine sessions later. By June, an unchanged book would have been -3%. By December, +18%. The trader had locked in -27% on a single morning and watched, in cash, a 45-percentage-point recovery he had no plan to participate in.

The falsifiable portfolio-panic fingerprint: (1) you closed three or more positions in a window of under sixty minutes; (2) at least half of those closes were below their *individual* written stops; (3) no PM mandate breach had been flagged at the moment you started selling; (4) the closes were executed during a session with a major risk-off headline (macro, geopolitical, pandemic, banking). Three of four ticked is a panic-liquidation session, regardless of where the index goes the next month.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that "everything is correlated in a crash, so I should sell everything." Correlation does spike in crashes — but the *appropriate response* to higher correlation is sizing down at the portfolio level, not zeroing out the book in the same hour. The correct mental model: your portfolio's stop-loss is not the sum of your individual stops fired in one morning — it is your <Term id="ami_mandate" />'s max-drawdown cap, monitored by the PM, with a deterministic action that is *de-risking, not capitulating*. A 50% de-risk (close half of each position, or close the three with the weakest individual theses) is a structural action you can take at any drawdown. A 100% liquidation should only happen when the mandate breaches and the PM authorises it — never on your own panic timer. The PM's <Term id="ami_pm_safety_floor" /> exists precisely because you, mid-crash, are not the same trader who wrote the mandate.

## Try it

Open Settings → Mandate and review what the PM is configured to do at progressive drawdown levels (e.g. at -10%, at -15%, at -20%). If you do not have a written de-risk action at each tier (typically: at -10% pause new entries; at -15% reduce position sizes by 50%; at -20% PM mandates closure), define them now. Then open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Portfolio Manager and ask: "Run my last drawdown event against this tier policy — where would each action have fired?" The gap between what the tiers would have done and what you actually did is the panic-selling tax you have paid.

## Takeaway

A drawdown is what the mandate was built to survive. A panic liquidation is what the mandate was built to prevent. Define de-risk tiers before the headline arrives, and let the PM — not your stomach — pull the lever.
