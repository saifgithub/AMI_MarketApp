# Boredom trading

Boredom trading is opening a position because you have not opened one recently — not because the setup arrived. After this lesson you will be able to name the cost of an unscheduled trade, define what a no-trade day is supposed to look like, and set a single self-imposed trade-count ceiling, tracked in the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, that protects you from the trader who needs to feel like a trader today. Most strategies have an expected number of trades per month. If you took double that, you are not running the strategy — you are running boredom with a chart open.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader's written strategy generated, on average, 4–6 setups per month on US large-caps — long pullbacks to the 20-day moving average on names with a positive 200-day trend. In a quiet September, the market traded sideways for three weeks and produced exactly one qualifying setup, which he took and which closed flat. By the third week his equity curve had not moved. He took a long on JPM "to be in the market", then a short on XOM "to hedge", then a starter on META "because it looked extended", then a swing on AMZN earnings "for the gamma". None of the four were on his strategy's qualification list. By end of month his account was -2.3% in a flat tape — every single dollar lost to trades the strategy did not ask for.

The falsifiable pattern: you are boredom-trading if your trade count for the period (week, month) is at least 1.5x your strategy's documented baseline, and at least half of the extra trades fail the written entry checklist on later review. Two numbers. If you do not have a documented baseline trade count for your strategy, that is the first problem; the boredom is the second.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that being in cash is wasting time. It is not. Being in cash is collecting optionality — the value of having capital available for the *next* qualifying setup. Every unqualified trade you take is a payment from your future-best-trade to your present-bored-trader. The correct mental model: the strategy's edge lives in the average expected value of qualified trades only. Unqualified trades have an expected value of approximately zero minus fees, slippage, and the opportunity cost of the capital they tie up. A no-trade day is not a failed day; it is the day the strategy is *protecting* you from spending size on something it did not ask for. The Conservative Debator's question to ask yourself is the cleanest one in trading: *"Is this trade on my list, or am I just bored?"*

## Try it

Write down a trade-count ceiling for the week — your strategy's documented baseline, or 5 if you don't have one yet (AMI doesn't have a field for this; it's a rule you hold yourself). Then open the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, filter to the last 30 days, and tag each closed trade as either "on checklist" or "off checklist". Take the off-checklist row to a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Conservative Debator and ask it to characterise the pattern. Most off-checklist trades cluster in the same week — the boring one.

## Takeaway

Cash is a position. The no-trade day is the strategy doing its job, not failing at it.
