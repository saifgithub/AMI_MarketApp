# Duration & convexity intuition

Not every bond reacts to a rate move by the same amount — a 2-year bond barely flinches at a 1-point yield swing while a 20-year bond can drop double digits. Duration is the number that tells you how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in yield, and it's the single fastest way to compare interest-rate risk across very different bonds. After this lesson you'll be able to read a duration figure and predict roughly how much a bond's price moves for a given rate move.

## Example

Take the same hypothetical $1,000 face, 4% coupon, semiannual bond from the pricing lesson, priced at a 5% YTM. At 10 years to maturity, its Macaulay duration is **8.26 years** and its modified duration is **8.05**. The heuristic: price moves roughly **-modified duration % per 1-point yield move**. So if yields rise 1 percentage point, this bond's price should fall roughly 8.05%. Now compare it to the same coupon and yield but only 2 years to maturity: Macaulay duration **1.94 years**, modified duration **1.89**. The same 1-point yield rise costs the 2-year bond roughly 1.89% — over four times less pain than the 10-year bond, purely because more of its cash flow arrives sooner. The clearest teaching case is a zero-coupon 10-year bond at 5% YTM: Macaulay duration is exactly **10.0 years** — its maturity. With no coupons along the way, 100% of your cash flow sits at maturity, so duration collapses to exactly the time you wait. That's the anchor case that makes duration intuitive: duration is a cash-flow-weighted average time to get your money back, and coupons pull that average earlier.

## The trap

Beginners treat duration like a fixed, permanent property of a bond — "this bond has 8 years of duration" as if it's stamped on the certificate. It isn't. Duration changes as the bond ages, as yields move, and it's specific to the yield level it was measured at. The bigger trap: treating the duration estimate as exact. Modified duration is a **linear** approximation of a curved relationship — it gets the direction and rough magnitude right but overstates the loss on a rate rise and understates the gain on a rate fall. The correct mental model: duration tells you the slope at one point on a curve, not the whole curve.

## The steelman

A sharp critic could say: "If duration is just an approximation, why not always compute the exact repriced value instead of relying on a linear shortcut?" Fair — and desks with pricing engines do exactly that for a single bond. But duration earns its keep as a **portfolio-level risk yardstick**: you can sum duration times dollar exposure across hundreds of bonds with different coupons and maturities to get one portfolio-level rate-sensitivity number, something you can't do by re-running full repricing models position by position in real time. The falsifier for whether duration is "good enough": compare the linear duration-implied price move against the bond's actual repriced value for a realistic rate move (1-2 points). If the gap is small — which convexity theory predicts it will be for moderate moves — the shortcut earns its use; for large rate shocks the gap widens and you need the convexity correction.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the Fundamentals Analyst, ask: "If I'm comparing a short-dated bond fund to a long-dated bond fund, what does duration tell me about which one is riskier if rates move?" Use the 8.05-vs-1.89 comparison from this lesson to sanity-check the answer.

## Takeaway

Duration tells you roughly how much a bond's price moves per 1-point yield change — longer maturity and lower coupons mean higher duration and more rate sensitivity — but it's a straight-line estimate of a curved relationship, so treat it as a fast approximation, not an exact forecast.
