# Calls & puts — the two building blocks

Every option that trades — on AAPL, NVDA, or a Bursa Malaysia derivatives contract — reduces to one of two building blocks: a call, the right to buy an underlying at a fixed strike price by a fixed expiry, or a put, the right to sell it. Each block has two sides: the buyer, who pays a premium for the right, and the seller, who collects that premium in exchange for an obligation if the buyer chooses to exercise. Every option strategy in this module — payoff diagrams, covered calls, protective puts — is just these two blocks combined differently. After this lesson you'll be able to name all four positions and state, without hesitating, who holds a right and who holds an obligation in each.

## Example

Take AAPL, illustratively trading near $195. A trader who wants upside exposure without committing to 100 shares outright can buy a call: for example, the right to buy 100 AAPL shares at a $200 strike, expiring in a month, for a premium of a few dollars per share (illustrative figure, not a live quote — real premiums move with AAPL's price and implied volatility). That trader is now **long a call** — they hold a right, not an obligation. If AAPL never gets near $200, they simply let the option expire and lose only what they paid. On the other side of that same contract sits whoever sold it — **short the call** — who collected the premium upfront but is now obligated to deliver 100 AAPL shares at $200 if the buyer exercises, no matter how high AAPL has actually gone. The put works as the mirror image: a trader who wants downside protection or a bearish bet can buy a put — the right to sell at a fixed strike — while the put seller is obligated to buy the shares at that strike if exercised. Four positions, two rights, two obligations.

## The trap

Beginners hear "buying a call" and picture buying the stock at a discount — as if the premium is a down payment that gets applied toward the shares. It isn't. The premium is a separate, non-refundable cost for the *right* to transact later; if the stock never reaches the strike, that premium is gone regardless of what happens to the stock afterward. The second beginner mistake is symmetry-blindness: assuming buyer and seller carry equivalent risk because they're on "opposite sides of the same trade." They don't. The option buyer's maximum loss is capped at the premium paid, full stop. The option seller's risk is structurally different — an uncovered (naked) short call on a stock with no ceiling on how high it can run carries a loss that grows with the stock, uncapped. Right and obligation are not symmetric risks wearing different labels.

## Takeaway

A call is the right to buy, a put is the right to sell — the buyer of either pays a premium for a right capped at that cost, while the seller collects the premium for an obligation that isn't capped the same way.
