# The yield curve & what inversion means

Plot the yield of government bonds against how long you'd have to hold them — 3 months, 2 years, 10 years, 30 years — and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes upward: lenders demand more yield to tie up money for longer. When that slope flips and short-term yields pay more than long-term ones, the curve is "inverted" — one of the most closely watched signals in markets, because it has preceded every US recession since the 1960s. After this lesson you'll be able to read a yield curve shape and explain what the market is collectively betting on when it inverts.

## Example

The most-watched version is the "2s10s" spread — the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 2-year Treasury yield. It inverted in 2022, with the 2-year yield trading above the 10-year for an extended stretch into 2023, one of the longest inversions on record; no NBER-dated US recession has followed it so far — the first inversion in the modern record without one, which is itself the live test of the signal. The mechanism: when the market expects the central bank to cut rates in the future because growth is slowing, long-dated bonds price in those future lower rates today, pulling 10-year yields down even while the current policy rate — and therefore short-term yields — stays high. Bank Negara Malaysia's Overnight Policy Rate (OPR) plays an analogous role on Bursa Malaysia: it's the short end anchor that the broader Malaysian government securities curve prices against, and MGS curve shape gets read for the same growth-expectation signal, even though Malaysia's curve has a different history and drivers than the US Treasury curve.

## The trap

Beginners see "yield curve inverted" and treat it as a precise recession-timing tool — "sell everything now." That's wrong on two counts. First, the lag between inversion and recession has historically ranged from about 6 months to over 2 years, so it's a warning, not a countdown clock. Second, inversion doesn't cause a recession; it *reflects* the market's aggregate forecast that one is coming, priced through thousands of independent trades. The correct mental model: the yield curve is a real-time poll of bond-market expectations about future growth and rate policy, not a mechanical trigger. Treat an inversion as raising the probability you assign to a slowdown, not as a fixed date on a calendar.

## The steelman

A sharp skeptic could argue: "The 2s10s curve has also inverted without a recession following closely, or with false-alarm timing bad enough to make the signal unreliable for anyone trading on it." That's a real critique — the curve isn't a perfect predictor, and post-2008 unconventional central bank policy (quantitative easing, forward guidance) has arguably distorted the long end of the curve in ways that weaken the historical pattern. The falsifier: track the realized lag and false-positive rate across multiple cycles, not just one. If inversions increasingly fail to precede recessions as central bank tools evolve, the signal's reliability is genuinely degrading — that's an empirical question worth revisiting each cycle, not a reason to dismiss the signal outright given its multi-decade track record.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the Market Analyst, ask: "What does the current yield curve shape look like, and has any part of it inverted recently?" Notice whether the answer frames it as a probability update rather than a certainty — that's the discipline this lesson is teaching.

## Takeaway

An inverted yield curve is the bond market's aggregate bet that growth is slowing enough to warrant future rate cuts — a real, historically meaningful signal, but a probability update over a wide time window, not a countdown clock.
