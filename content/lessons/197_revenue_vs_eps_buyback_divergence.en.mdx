# Revenue vs EPS growth divergence — the buyback effect

<Lesson id="044_earnings_growth"/> introduced the gap between revenue growth and EPS growth as the signature of financial engineering — buybacks shrinking the denominator while the numerator (net income) grows slowly or not at all. This lesson goes deep on the math, the corporate-finance logic, and the precise condition under which buyback-driven EPS growth is value-additive versus value-destructive. By the end of this lesson you should be able to decompose EPS growth into revenue, margin, and share-count contributions, recognise the leverage involved in financing buybacks with debt, and apply a falsifiable rule for valuing buyback growth.

## How this plays out in real markets

AAPL is the textbook case. Over the trailing decade, revenue has grown roughly 7-8% annually and net income roughly 8-10%. EPS has grown 12-14% annually. The 4-6 percentage point gap is the buyback — the share count fell from over 26B (split-adjusted) to roughly 15B over the period, a reduction of over 40%. AAPL has spent over $700B on buybacks since 2013. A holder of AAPL has compounded EPS faster than the business has compounded, because the same earnings are split across fewer shares. Per-share metrics improved; the business itself grew at a more modest rate.

Compare a company that doesn't buy back: BRK-B (Berkshire Hathaway) — Buffett historically returned cash via reinvestment and selective buybacks only when the stock was below intrinsic value. Revenue and EPS track more closely (when adjusted for unusual items) because share count is roughly stable. The growth you see is the business's growth.

Now an example where buybacks were value-destructive. IBM in the 2010s bought back roughly $88B of stock at prices ranging from $130 to $215, often funded partially by debt. Revenue was shrinking; EPS was held up by the shrinking share count. The buyback-engineered EPS growth masked a deteriorating business. When the engineering ran out of room, the stock fell anyway. Buybacks at a stretched multiple plus operating decline equalled value destruction — a per-share liquidation of a shrinking business.

The math: if a company has $1B of net income, 100M shares, and an EPS of $10, and it buys back 5M shares at $200 (paying $1B in cash), the new EPS is $1B / 95M = $10.53 — a 5.3% lift on no operating change. That accretion is real to the remaining holders. But if the company funded the $1B buyback with debt at a 5% after-tax rate, interest expense rises $50M, dropping net income to $950M and giving EPS of $10. Net zero. The buyback is only accretive when the inverse of the P/E (the earnings yield) exceeds the after-tax cost of the debt funding it.

On Bursa Malaysia: buyback culture is less developed than in the US, with companies more often returning cash via dividends. Public Bank (PBBANK) has done occasional buybacks; many KLCI names hold cash on the balance sheet rather than retire shares.

Falsifiable claim: a buyback is value-accretive when the company's earnings yield (1 / P/E) exceeds the after-tax cost of the capital used to fund it, AND the management team would not see better opportunities by reinvesting the same cash internally at a higher ROIC. Buybacks that fail either test are mechanical EPS engineering, not capital return.

## The trap

The trap is treating EPS growth as a single quality signal. EPS growth of 15% looks identical whether it came from (a) 15% organic revenue growth at flat margins and stable share count, (b) 5% revenue growth, 5% margin expansion, and 5% share-count reduction, or (c) -5% revenue, flat margins, and 20% share-count reduction. The first is high-quality business growth; the third is liquidation dressed up as growth. The correct mental model: decompose EPS growth into (1 + revenue growth) × (1 + margin change) × (1 + share-count reduction) − 1, approximately, and ask which terms are doing the work. What invalidates the surface read: buybacks funded by debt are leveraged equity returns — they amplify ROE (<Lesson id="041_return_on_equity"/>) and EPS mechanically but they also amplify downside. A leverage-engineered EPS growth rate disappears in a recession because the levered balance sheet constrains capital allocation.

## Takeaway

EPS growth is an identity: revenue, margin, and share count. Decompose before paying a growth multiple — only the revenue and margin components reflect the business; share-count reduction reflects capital allocation, and is accretive only when earnings yield beats the cost of funding.
