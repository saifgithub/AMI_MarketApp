# Capex intensity and working capital stress

Lesson 035 defined free cash flow as operating cash flow minus capex. Lesson 160 broke the cash flow statement into three sections. This lesson goes deeper into two specific lines that determine whether reported cash flow is a clean read on business quality: *capex* (split into maintenance and growth) and *working capital changes*. A high-capex business can post lovely operating cash flow numbers but spend nearly all of it just to stay in place — leaving little for shareholders. A business with bloating working capital can disguise operating stress for several quarters. By the end of this lesson you should be able to separate maintenance from growth capex, read the working-capital line in the cash flow statement, and identify the specific configuration that signals operational deterioration disguised by accounting.

## How this plays out in real markets

**Maintenance vs growth capex.** Companies rarely disclose this split cleanly; you estimate it. The rough rule: maintenance capex is the capex required to keep current revenue stable — usually approximated by depreciation expense (which represents the consumption of existing long-lived assets). Growth capex is total capex minus this maintenance estimate. The remainder is what funds expansion.

Real example: MSFT in fiscal 2024 reported capex of about $44.5B and depreciation of about $22B. The implied split: ~$22B maintenance, ~$22B growth — a roughly 50/50 mix, indicating heavy growth investment. By contrast, mature consumer-staples names typically run capex roughly equal to depreciation (capex/depreciation ≈ 1.0x), meaning nearly all capex is replacement, not expansion.

Capex-intense businesses include telecoms (capex 15–20% of revenue), utilities (15–20%), capital-intensive industrials (10–15%), and increasingly hyperscale cloud providers (15%+ of revenue). The free-cash-flow conversion rate at these businesses can be just 30–50% of net income even when everything is going right.

**Working capital changes.** The cash flow statement reconciles net income to operating cash flow by adjusting for changes in working capital — receivables, inventory, and payables. When these changes are favourable (receivables down, inventory down, payables up), they add to CFO. When unfavourable (receivables up, inventory up, payables down), they subtract from CFO.

The diagnostic: when inventory and receivables both build faster than revenue for 2+ consecutive quarters, the company is producing or selling more than it's collecting on. Inventory build can be deliberate (preparing for a known launch) or symptomatic (demand softening). The cash flow statement reveals which when read alongside the income statement: if revenue is decelerating and inventory is building, the build is demand-symptomatic, not strategic.

Real example: in cyclical industrials and consumer hardware, inventory build that runs 30%+ above revenue growth for 2 quarters has preceded most major margin-cut announcements of the last decade. The cash flow statement saw the demand softening before the press release.

On Bursa Malaysia: TOPGLOV in the post-2021 cycle showed inventory build well in excess of revenue change for several quarters, presaging the gross-margin collapse that followed.

The falsifiable claim: when operating cash flow exceeds net income solely because of a working-capital release (collecting old receivables, drawing down inventory), the CFO strength is one-quarter-only and reverses in subsequent periods. Conversely, when inventory and receivables build faster than revenue for 2+ consecutive quarters with stable or decelerating revenue, the trailing CFO understates forward weakness because the working-capital build is a *use* of cash that will continue.

## The trap

The trap is treating CFO as a single metric without decomposing it. A beginner sees "operating cash flow $5B, up 20% YoY" and applauds. They miss that $1.5B of the increase came from extending payables (deferring payments to suppliers) and $1B from drawing down inventory — both of which are one-time releases that don't repeat. The underlying operating cash flow excluding working-capital effects grew only modestly. The correct mental model: every line in the working-capital reconciliation is a separate question. Inventory down → why (better sell-through, or under-producing)? Receivables down → why (better collection, or a customer prepaying)? Payables up → why (stretching terms, or a deliberate working-capital policy change)? The mirror trap applies to capex: a company can post strong FCF by under-investing in maintenance, mortgaging the asset base for one or two years of cash-flow appearance before the asset deterioration shows up in operating results.

## Takeaway

Capex has a maintenance core that depreciation approximates; working capital has a building pressure the cash flow statement quietly shows. Decompose both — what looks like cash-flow strength can be working-capital release, and what looks like cash-flow weakness can be deliberate growth investment.
