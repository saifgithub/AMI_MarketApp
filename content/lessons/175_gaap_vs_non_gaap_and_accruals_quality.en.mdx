# GAAP vs non-GAAP and accruals quality

<Lesson id="035_cash_flow_vs_earnings"/> established that earnings and cash flow can diverge. <Lesson id="174_stock_based_compensation_the_hidden_dilution"/> zoomed in on SBC as one big source of divergence. This lesson generalises the issue: companies report two sets of earnings — GAAP (the auditor-attested standard) and non-GAAP / "adjusted" (management's preferred framing) — and the gap between them is one of the most reliable signals of earnings quality. Companies whose non-GAAP earnings consistently exceed GAAP earnings by 20%+, and whose accruals (earnings minus cash flow) are persistently positive, tend to underperform over multi-year horizons. By the end of this lesson you should know the specific GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap that flags earnings-quality concerns, and the accruals-quality screen that catches aggressive accounting before it shows up in a write-down.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take a representative software company in 2024 reporting "adjusted EPS" of $2.40 against GAAP EPS of $1.10. The gap is $1.30 per share, or 54% of adjusted earnings. The reconciliation typically includes: SBC, acquisition-related amortisation, restructuring charges, one-time tax items. Each individual exclusion may be defensible; collectively, when they exceed 30–40% of reported non-GAAP earnings every year for multiple years, the "one-time" exclusions become recurring by another name.

Compare to a representative industrial. GAAP EPS $5.20 vs non-GAAP EPS $5.40 — a 4% gap. The reconciliation is small and typically includes one-time restructuring or asset-sale gains. This is the clean profile: GAAP and non-GAAP track closely, and management isn't using "adjusted" as a marketing tool.

Now look at accruals quality. Accruals = net income minus operating cash flow. A company reporting $1B in net income but only $300M in operating cash flow has $700M of accruals — meaning $700M of reported earnings hasn't yet converted to cash. Some accruals are normal (timing differences, receivables growth in a healthy growing business). Persistent large accruals are a warning. Academic research (Sloan 1996 and many follow-ups) has shown that companies with high accruals systematically underperform companies with low accruals by 5–10% annually over the following years.

The "accruals ratio" — accruals divided by total assets — is the common screen. Companies in the highest decile of accruals-to-assets tend to be aggressive accounting candidates. Famous historical cases include Enron, WorldCom, and various pre-collapse retail chains.

Look at TSLA in 2022–2024. Operating cash flow tracked roughly to net income (low accruals), but non-GAAP EBITDA excluded substantial SBC. The GAAP-vs-non-GAAP gap was material; the accruals-quality picture was clean. Different signals telling different parts of the story.

On Bursa Malaysia: SERBA DINAMIK's accounting issues surfaced when, in May 2021, its auditor KPMG flagged ~RM2.32b of sales and ~RM652m of trade receivables it could not verify; the company disputed the concerns and KPMG resigned that June.

The falsifiable claim: a non-GAAP-to-GAAP gap of more than 25% of GAAP earnings sustained for 3+ years is the threshold where earnings quality becomes a material thesis risk. Combined with accruals exceeding 5% of total assets, the combination warrants a deep dive into specific accounting policies before any valuation work is meaningful.

## The trap

The trap is using non-GAAP earnings for valuation because they're easier and because that's what consensus estimates and headlines use. A beginner applies a 25x multiple to non-GAAP EPS, gets a fair value, and never asks whether GAAP EPS would produce a very different answer. The correct mental model: compute valuation on BOTH bases, and trust the lower of the two when the gap is wide and persistent. The opposite trap is rejecting all non-GAAP adjustments as marketing. Some adjustments are defensible — a genuine one-time legal settlement, a true acquisition-amortisation that has no real economic cost — and using GAAP unadjusted for those would systematically underprice the business. The discipline is to read the reconciliation footnote and decide adjustment-by-adjustment, not metric-by-metric.

## Try it

In <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Fundamentals Analyst, pick a stock you own or are considering and ask: "Show me GAAP EPS, non-GAAP EPS, and the reconciliation line items for each of the last 5 years. Also show me net income vs operating cash flow and the accruals-to-assets ratio." In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write one sentence for each: "The non-GAAP-to-GAAP gap is X% sustained for Y years. Accruals are Z% of assets. The honest valuation uses [GAAP / adjusted / a middle figure] because [reason]." This exercise calibrates your eye for earnings quality across your portfolio.

## Takeaway

When non-GAAP earnings consistently exceed GAAP by 25%+ on recurring exclusions, the "adjusted" number is marketing — and when accruals exceed 5% of assets, the cash isn't keeping up with the earnings. Use the lower number when the gap is wide and persistent; the historical base rate is on the side of the cash.
