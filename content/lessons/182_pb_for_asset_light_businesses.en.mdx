# P/B for asset-light businesses — why the ratio doesn't work

Lesson 040 flagged that P/B is "near-useless for software and platforms." This lesson explains *why* — and what to use instead. The mechanical reason: GAAP expenses R&D, brand-building, and customer acquisition as they happen, never capitalising them on the balance sheet. The economic reason: in an asset-light business, the productive capital is people, code, and trust — none of which appear at fair value in book. By the end you should be able to recognise an asset-light business at a glance, name the specific ratios that *do* work for it, and resist the strongest mistake a former value investor makes when moving into modern markets.

## How this plays out in real markets

MSFT mid-2026: stock $480, book value per share ~$42, P/B ≈ 11x. The book records the historical cost of buildings, servers, and net working capital. It does not record the value of: Windows, Office, Azure, the GitHub network, the LinkedIn graph, or twenty-five years of accumulated developer relationships. All of those were expensed in the years they were built. Microsoft's economic worth is roughly nothing like its book; the 11x P/B is meaningless on its own.

META: stock $580, book per share ~$78, P/B ≈ 7.4x. The Facebook + Instagram + WhatsApp user graph (3.5B+ MAU across the family), the ad-targeting models, the brand — none of it sits on the balance sheet. NFLX: stock $700, book per share ~$45, P/B ≈ 15.5x. The 270M+ subscribers, the recommendation engine, the original-content library (partially capitalised but at cost) — also mostly off-book. CRM: stock $260, book per share ~$60, P/B ≈ 4.3x. Even this "modest" P/B is dominated by goodwill from acquisitions, not productive working capital.

The right tools for these businesses: EV/Sales (especially for high-growth software), EV/EBITDA, Rule of 40 (revenue growth + free-cash-flow margin), net dollar retention (for SaaS), and DCF. P/B doesn't fail because it's wrong — it fails because the denominator captures the wrong thing for the business model.

On Bursa: a tech-services name like Vitrox or a brand-heavy consumer like MRDIY will show P/B that looks expensive (3x–8x) against banks at 1.3x. That gap is structural, not a comparison: MRDIY's brand and store-density advantage doesn't show up in book value, so P/B reads high in a way that has no information.

Falsifiable claim: P/B is uninformative as a stand-alone valuation metric for any business where intangible assets (brand, IP, network, customer relationships) make up most of the productive capital. Operationally: if R&D plus sales-and-marketing spend is more than 25% of revenue over the last three years, that's an asset-light fingerprint, and P/B should be treated as a *consistency check* on capital structure at most — not a valuation signal.

## The trap

The trap is the migrating-value-investor trap: an investor trained on banks, REITs, and industrials moves into tech, screens on low P/B, finds nothing, and concludes the market is "absurdly overvalued." The right read isn't that the market is absurd — it's that the metric doesn't fit. The correct mental model: match the valuation tool to the asset structure. Asset-heavy financials and industrials → P/B with ROE. Cyclical commodity producers → mid-cycle P/E and asset replacement value. Asset-light platforms → EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA, DCF, plus business-quality ratios (gross margin, net retention, Rule of 40). What invalidates the signal: a software business that *is* genuinely capital-intensive — Snowflake's data warehouse infrastructure, Cloudflare's edge POPs — has more on-book asset weight than pure SaaS, and P/tangible-book can carry some information at the margin. But even there, the dominant value driver is the intangible stack, and P/B is at best a sanity check.

## Takeaway

For asset-light businesses, P/B is a consistency check at best — never a valuation. Match the tool to the asset structure: P/B + ROE for asset-heavy; EV/Sales + Rule of 40 for asset-light.
