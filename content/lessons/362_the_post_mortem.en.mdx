# The post-mortem that isn't a blame hunt

A post-mortem has one job: decide which decisions to repeat. That is not the same job as deciding which positions made money. A journalled position that lost, with its invalidation defined in advance and honoured on the day, is a sound process meeting variance — repeat it. An unjournalled position that won is a warning wearing a win's clothing. And the moment a review turns into a hunt for who to blame, it stops producing that information at all, because the reliable response to blame is to record fewer decisions. This lesson gives you the separation and the one question that keeps it honest.

## Example

Take four closed simulated positions from one quarter of your own journal. The figures are invented here to keep the arithmetic clean — no real ticker, no real price.

- **A** — thesis and invalidation level written before entry; stop honoured exactly as specified: **−1.2%** of portfolio.
- **B** — no journal entry; size doubled on a hunch after entry; stop moved down twice: **+4.1%**.
- **C** — thesis journalled, invalidation defined, played out as written: **+2.3%**.
- **D** — thesis journalled, but closed at the first red candle, well above the invalidation level you had already defined: **−0.4%**.

Net for the quarter: −1.2 + 4.1 + 2.3 − 0.4 = **+4.8%**.

Score by outcome and you have two winners (B, C) and two losers (A, D). Score by process — did you follow what you wrote before the outcome existed, as <Lesson id="359_keeping_a_decision_journal"/> requires? — and you have two sound (A, C) and two broken (B, D). Same two-and-two split, different membership: the scorecards agree on C and D and disagree on A and B.

That disagreement is the whole output of the review. A is a sound process that lost; repeat it. B is a broken process that won — and it produced 4.1 of the 4.8 points, **85% of the quarter's net gain**, from the one sequence of decisions you least want to repeat. D is a process error the P&L barely registers, because cutting before your own invalidation is still not following your plan.

## The trap

The trap is opening the review by asking "what went wrong?" and stopping at the biggest loser. That feels like accountability — someone is being held to account, a lesson is apparently being extracted. What it actually does is teach the reviewer (you, or an analyst on your team) that written-down decisions are ammunition. The next thesis gets journalled vaguely, or after the fact, or not at all, and the record a post-mortem depends on quietly disappears. The correct opening question is process-first and outcome-blind: *given only what was on the page before entry, was this decision one I would make again?* Answer that, then look at the P&L — never the reverse.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal, pick three closed positions, and label each one twice — process sound or broken, outcome positive or negative — before you look at the P&L column. Any position where the two labels disagree is where the lesson is. Then ask the Research Manager to argue the opposite label on your most profitable trade. Remember: AMI is a training simulator, and its output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

Grade the decision on what was written before the outcome existed: a journalled loss is variance and gets repeated, an unjournalled win is a warning. The review that hunts for blame stops getting decisions written down at all.
