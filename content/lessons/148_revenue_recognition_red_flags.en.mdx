# Revenue recognition red flags

Lesson 032 said revenue tells you the *size* of a business, not its *health* — and the cleanest way to verify the headline is real is to interrogate when and how it was recognised. Accountants book revenue when an obligation is satisfied, not necessarily when cash arrives. That gap is where the games happen: pre-shipping to distributors at quarter-end, booking long-term contracts upfront, recognising bundled deals on the wrong line. By the end of this lesson you should be able to spot three classic recognition red flags from the income statement and balance sheet alone, and know the falsifiable trigger that promotes "watch list" to "do not own".

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at the days-sales-outstanding (DSO) line — average days between a sale and cash collection — calculated as `(Accounts Receivable / Revenue) × 90` for a quarter. A healthy mature business runs a stable DSO; software firms typically 60–90 days, retailers near zero. When DSO drifts up quarter after quarter without a stated reason, the company is either booking revenue it isn't collecting yet or extending unusually generous terms to push deals over the line.

Real example: in fiscal 2024, NVDA reported quarterly revenue around $30B with accounts receivable around $14B — DSO near 42 days. Stable. By contrast, when a hardware peer reports a DSO jumping from 65 to 95 days across two quarters while revenue growth decelerates, the math usually says channel stuffing: more product was pushed to distributors who haven't yet sold it through to end customers. The receivable swelled because real demand didn't.

Two other recognition red flags worth knowing: (1) deferred revenue *falling* faster than revenue at a subscription business — the backlog of future-period bookings is draining, which means the renewal pipeline is weaker than the reported top line; (2) "bill-and-hold" disclosures in the 10-K — a customer is invoiced and revenue is booked but goods haven't shipped. This is occasionally legitimate, but is one of the most over-represented practices in historic restatement cases.

On Bursa Malaysia: TOPGLOV during the 2020–2021 glove cycle saw receivables and revenue both surge as buyers locked in supply. When demand normalised, receivable trends preceded the revenue reversal by a quarter — the cash side told the story first.

## The trap

The trap is asking "is revenue up?" instead of "is the receivable line growing faster than revenue, and why?" A beginner sees a beat-and-raise quarter and stops. The trap thinking: "revenue grew, EPS beat, what else is there?" The correct mental model: every dollar of recognised revenue should leave a coherent trail through receivables, deferred revenue, and operating cash flow. When AR grows materially faster than sales for two consecutive quarters and the company doesn't disclose a customer-mix shift or a one-off contract that explains it, you are looking at either deteriorating collections or aggressive recognition. Either way, the quality of the top line is degrading.

## Takeaway

Revenue is recognised; cash is collected. When receivables run ahead of revenue and operating cash flow doesn't follow, the top line is borrowing credibility it hasn't earned.
