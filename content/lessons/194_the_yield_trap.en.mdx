# The yield trap — when high yield is a warning, not an opportunity

Lesson 043 introduced the yield trap as one of the three categories a high yield falls into. This lesson goes deep on the mechanics of how a yield gets to "trap" levels and why the screen that sorts a watchlist by dividend yield is one of the most reliably-losing approaches in retail investing. By the end of this lesson you should be able to identify a yield trap in under two minutes from the data feed, name the three specific patterns that produce one, and apply a falsifiable rule for distinguishing a sustainable high yield from a cut waiting to happen.

## How this plays out in real markets

A dividend yield rises in two ways: the dividend goes up, or the price goes down. When the dividend goes up, the company has signalled confidence; when the price goes down and the dividend hasn't been cut yet, the yield rises mechanically and the market is signalling that a cut is coming.

Concrete example: T (AT&T) in early 2022 traded near $24 with an annual dividend of $2.08, yielding 8.7%. The historical average yield on T had been roughly 5%. The yield was elevated because the stock had been falling, and the underlying fundamentals — declining wireless margins, the Time Warner spin-off, the debt load — pointed to a payout that wasn't being earned. T cut the dividend by ~50% in February 2022. The investors who bought the 8.7% yield ended up holding a sub-5% yield on a stock that was meaningfully lower than where they bought.

A second example: Kraft Heinz (KHC) in early 2019. The yield reached 7% on a falling price and well-publicised free-cash-flow issues. The company cut the dividend by 36% in February 2019; the stock fell further on the announcement. The yield-chasers absorbed both the cut and the price drop.

Compare a high yield that is real. ENB (Enbridge), a Canadian energy infrastructure name, trades on NYSE with a yield typically in the 6–7% range and a payout funded by predictable, regulated cash flows from pipeline tariffs. The yield is high not because the price is falling but because the business model produces high cash distributions per dollar of revenue. Payout-on-distributable-cash-flow is in the 60–70% range — not a stress signal.

The pattern that distinguishes trap from opportunity: in a trap, the high yield comes with payout-on-FCF above 100%, FCF declining, debt rising, and a falling stock price preceding the yield rise. In a real high-yield opportunity, the yield reflects structural cash returns from a stable, regulated, or asset-heavy business with payout-on-FCF below 90%, a stable price, and either flat or rising FCF.

On Bursa Malaysia: a chronic example was BAT Malaysia (British American Tobacco) in the late 2010s — yields reached 9–10% as the stock fell on regulatory pressure (excise hikes, plain packaging). The dividend was eventually cut materially. MAYBANK with its 5–6% yield is the opposite case: high yield, stable price, well-covered payout.

Falsifiable claim: a dividend yield above 7% on a non-REIT, non-utility, non-MLP equity that has fallen more than 25% over the trailing 12 months is a yield trap candidate that requires the full safety-scoring framework before any income-driven buy. The signal invalidates if the stock fell on a one-time event (a lawsuit settlement, a divestiture) where the underlying cash-generating business is unimpaired — but those cases are far rarer than yield-chasers assume.

## The trap

The trap is the math illusion. A 10% yield "pays you to wait", a 12% yield "covers a 50% drawdown in five years", and so on. The math is correct but conditional on the dividend being paid. Once the dividend is cut, both the income stream and the price collapse — total return is often dramatically negative even from a moderate purchase price, because the yield-chaser bought at the cusp of the cut. The correct mental model: a stock is yielding 10% because someone is selling it. Ask who is selling and why before joining the buyers. The seller usually has a thesis you don't have. What invalidates the trap reading: a structural reason for the high yield — REIT mandate, regulated utility distribution, pipeline tariff base, or BDC/MLP structure — turns the same headline into a different category of investment, governed by different sustainability rules.

## Takeaway

A high yield is information about expectations, not a free lunch. Sort a watchlist by yield and you'll find traps; sort by payout-on-FCF coverage and you'll find durable income.
