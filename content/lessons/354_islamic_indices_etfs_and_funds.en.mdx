# Islamic indices, ETFs & funds

You already know how to evaluate any fund — expense ratio, tracking error, passive vs active (<Lesson id="310_etfs_vs_mutual_funds"/>-<Lesson id="312_passive_vs_active_bogle"/>). A Sharia-compliant ETF adds exactly one more layer on top: which index methodology it tracks, and how often that index rebalances to drop stocks that drift out of compliance (<Lesson id="350_standards_differ_why_the_same_stock_flips"/>'s "compliance drift"). The fund-selection skills you already have transfer directly; the new judgment is reading an Islamic ETF's prospectus for its named benchmark. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name real Islamic ETFs and explain what to check beyond the usual expense-ratio comparison.

## Example

**SPUS** (SP Funds S&P 500 Sharia Industry Exclusions ETF) tracks the S&P 500 Sharia Industry Exclusions Index — S&P's own methodology applied to the S&P 500 universe. **HLAL** (Wahed FTSE USA Shariah ETF) tracks a FTSE Shariah index. **iShares MSCI World Islamic UCITS ETF** and similar names track MSCI's Islamic Index Series. Each of these applies a *different* named standard (<Lesson id="350_standards_differ_why_the_same_stock_flips"/>'s disagreement, now showing up in a fund wrapper) — so two "Islamic ETFs" covering similar large-cap universes can hold a meaningfully different stock list, because S&P's screening methodology and FTSE's don't produce identical pass/fail outcomes on every borderline name. On Bursa Malaysia, the exchange itself designates a **Shariah-compliant securities list** (Bursa-i), refreshed twice yearly by the SAC — an investor building a Bursa-only Islamic portfolio checks that list directly rather than relying on a US-domiciled ETF's index. The expense-ratio and tracking-error checks from <Lesson id="311_expense_ratio_tracking_error"/> still apply in full — a Sharia-compliant fund with a bloated expense ratio is still a bad fund on cost grounds, compliance aside.

## The trap

Beginners assume "it says Islamic/Shariah in the fund name" is itself the due diligence — pick any fund with that label and move on. That skips exactly the check <Lesson id="350_standards_differ_why_the_same_stock_flips"/> already taught: **which standard** does this specific fund's index track, and does that match the standard the investor actually wants applied? A fund tracking a more lenient methodology can hold names a stricter-standard investor would exclude. The correct mental model: an Islamic ETF's name tells you it applies *some* Sharia screen; the prospectus's stated index methodology tells you *which one* — read that before assuming two "Islamic" funds are interchangeable.

## Try it

Open the Fundamentals Analyst chat with a real Islamic ETF ticker (e.g. SPUS or HLAL) and ask which index methodology it tracks and how its expense ratio compares to a conventional broad-market ETF covering a similar universe. This lesson teaches how to read published screening methodologies and understand what an ETF's label implies; any real fund selection should factor in a qualified advisor's guidance on compliance.

## Takeaway

An Islamic ETF's name confirms a Sharia screen is applied; the prospectus's named index methodology tells you which standard — and the ordinary cost checks from <Lesson id="311_expense_ratio_tracking_error"/> still apply in full. These are published methodologies taught here for training; consult a qualified Islamic finance expert on any actual fund selection.

*Where this comes from: the published S&P Shariah, FTSE Shariah, MSCI Islamic, and Dow Jones Islamic index methodologies, each of which discloses its own screening standard.*
