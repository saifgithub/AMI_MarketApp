# Comparable companies

Comparable-company analysis ("comps") prices one business by reference to other similar businesses. The idea is simple — if five peers trade at 22x earnings on average and the company you're studying trades at 14x, you have a starting point for asking why. The discipline lies entirely in choosing the right peer set; with the wrong comps, you can prove almost anything. By the end of this lesson you should know how to assemble a defensible peer group, which multiples are appropriate for which kinds of business, and the specific failure mode that turns comparable analysis into confirmation bias.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take JPM. If you want to value JPM, you don't compare it to MSFT — they're both in the S&P 500, but their business models share almost nothing. The correct peer set is the other large US universal banks: BAC, WFC, C, and Goldman/Morgan Stanley for the capital-markets exposure. On price-to-book (P/B), the multiple banks live and die by, JPM trades around 1.8x book as of mid-2026, against a peer median closer to 1.2x. The 0.6-turn premium reflects JPM's higher and more stable return on equity (~17% vs ~12% for the peer median). Same multiple, very different conclusions depending on the comp set: against tech, JPM looks cheap; against banks, JPM looks expensively-priced for its quality.

For a growth company, P/B is irrelevant — book value doesn't capture the brand or the network. NVDA, AMD, and AVGO are reasonable design-led semiconductor comps; you'd value them on forward P/E or EV/EBITDA. In mid-2026 NVDA's forward P/E sits at a premium to AMD's, reflecting NVDA's higher growth and data-centre dominance. Strip out the data-centre segment and the multiples look closer — which tells you most of NVDA's premium is one segment's pricing power.

A bad comp set example: in 2024, several investors compared TSLA to traditional automakers (F, GM, STLA) and concluded it was wildly overvalued at 60x+ earnings against their 5x–8x. Others compared it to high-growth software companies and concluded it was reasonable. Neither comparison was fully right — TSLA is a hybrid of car company, energy storage business, and software-licence aspirations. The honest comp set has to combine perspectives, and the resulting valuation range is wide.

On Bursa Malaysia: comparing MAYBANK to CIMB and PUBLIC Bank is a defensible peer set (similar size, similar mix of consumer and corporate lending, same regulatory regime). Comparing MAYBANK to a fintech start-up isn't — different business model, different risk profile.

The falsifiable claim: a comparable analysis is only as useful as its peer set. If you can defend each peer choice with a one-sentence "same business model, same customer base, same regulatory environment" reason, the comp is fair. If you can't, the comp is window-dressing.

## The trap

The trap is reverse-engineering the comp set to support a conclusion you've already reached. A beginner who wants to argue a stock is cheap will quietly include high-multiple peers and exclude low-multiple ones; if they want to argue it's expensive, they do the reverse. The correct mental model: define the peer-set rules *before* you look at the multiples — "any company in the same primary industry with revenue between X and Y, the same geography, and a similar mix of products." Then accept whatever the median multiple tells you. The other version of the trap: anchoring on a sector median that itself is distorted (e.g., comparing to peers in the middle of a bubble or a crash). A median multiple is a relative number — it doesn't tell you whether the entire sector is mispriced together.

In the <Term id="ami_decision_journal" />, write down whether you'd add or remove anyone — and why, in one sentence each. ## Takeaway

Comparables are only as honest as the peer set — define the rules before you look at the numbers, and let the median (not the mean) speak when outliers are in the picture.
