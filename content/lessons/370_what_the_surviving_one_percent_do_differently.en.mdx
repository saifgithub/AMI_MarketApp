# What the surviving 1% do differently

<Lesson id="366_what_the_data_actually_says_about_day_trading"/> gave you the headline: under 1% of Taiwan's day traders were reliably profitable net of fees. This lesson asks the question everyone skips — reliably *how*? Not by mindset. The same dataset that found the 1% also found the single best predictor of whether someone would be in it, and it's a number you can start computing on yourself today, for free, in a simulator.

## How this plays out in real markets

Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean ranked every day trader in a given year by the Sharpe ratio of their day-trading returns — mean net daily return divided by its own standard deviation, the same risk-adjusted measure a fund would report. Then they simply watched what that same group did the following year. The top 500, ranked purely on the prior year's Sharpe ratio, went on to earn 61.3 basis points a day before fees (37.9bps after fees) in the next year. The bottom-ranked 500 went on to earn -11.5bps before fees (-28.9bps after) — reliably negative, not unlucky once. The researchers' own conclusion, stated plainly: past performance, whether measured as returns or dollar profits, is by a large margin the best predictor of future performance they tested.

Two things follow from that finding, and together they're what actually separates the roughly one-in-a-hundred from everyone else.

First, skill is measurable before it's provable. You don't need ten years to start telling signal from noise — you need a Sharpe ratio computed the way theirs was: net of costs, over enough trades that one lucky week can't dominate it, tracked consistently rather than checked selectively.

Second, the ranking only works as a test if it's run *before* you know the answer. The paper's persistence result holds because traders were sorted on one full year of data and graded on the next year's results — out of sample. A trader who only checks their record after a good stretch, or only when they feel confident, isn't running this test. They're running a search for confirmation.

## The trap

The trap is treating "what separates them" as a personality question — grit, discipline, nerve — instead of the unglamorous, falsifiable thing the data actually points to: a continuously kept, honestly scored record, checked against itself out of sample, over a run long enough that one hot week can't hide inside it. That's not inspiring copy. It's also the only thing in this entire track the research actually measured as predictive, as opposed to merely plausible.

The second trap is specific to this lesson: reading "past performance predicts future performance" as a green light to size up the moment your own numbers turn positive. The paper's top group was ranked on a full prior year, not a hot week — and <Lesson id="368_overconfidence_is_the_mechanism"/> already covered what a short winning streak does to confidence. A month of good <Term id="simulated_trade"/>s is data. It is not yet a Sharpe ratio you can trust.
