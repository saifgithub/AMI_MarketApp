# The floor diversification cannot go below

Diversification removes one kind of risk completely and the other kind not at all. After this lesson you will be able to say where your own book sits relative to the floor that no amount of further diversification can cross, and — more usefully — you will know when adding names has stopped buying you anything and the work should move somewhere else.

## Example

Take an openly hypothetical book of identical holdings: each name carries **30% annualised volatility**, every pair correlates at **0.35** — an ordinary figure for large-cap equities inside one market — and the money is spread equally. Nothing is a real company; the pattern is the lesson. Adding names one at a time and recomputing:

| names held | portfolio volatility | gained over the previous row |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **30.00%** | — |
| 2 | **24.65%** | −5.35 pp |
| 5 | **20.78%** | −3.87 pp |
| 10 | **19.33%** | −1.45 pp |
| 20 | **18.55%** | −0.78 pp |
| 30 | **18.29%** | −0.26 pp |
| 50 | **18.07%** | −0.22 pp |
| 100 | **17.91%** | −0.16 pp |

The column on the right is the whole lesson. The second name is worth 5.35 percentage points. Names 51 through 100 are worth 0.16 between them.

And there is a hard floor underneath. As the number of names grows, portfolio volatility converges on √ρ × σ = √0.35 × 30% = **17.75%**, and it never goes below. At 30 names you are already within 0.54 percentage points of a floor you could not cross with ten thousand names.

That floor has a name: **systematic risk** — the part every holding shares, which is why it survives averaging. Everything above it is **idiosyncratic** — the part specific to one company, which averages away because one firm's bad quarter is uncorrelated with another's. Diversification is a machine that removes the second and is completely powerless against the first.

The academic estimates of "enough" land in this region rather than at the old rule of thumb. Evans and Archer (1968) put the number near ten; Statman (1987) reworked the cost–benefit and concluded a well-diversified portfolio of randomly chosen stocks needs **at least 30 names for a borrowing investor and 40 for a lending one** — which is a statement about where the right-hand column stops being worth the trouble, not about a point where risk disappears. <Lesson id="365_your_risk_limits_in_ami"/> covers how this maps onto the position-count limit AMI derived for you.

## The trap

The trap is reading "diversification reduces risk" as "enough diversification removes risk", and then sizing a book as though it did. It feels right because the early rows reward you so heavily — going from one name to five genuinely cuts volatility by nine points, which is a large, visible win, and the natural inference is that the trend continues. It does not: the curve flattens hard, and it flattens onto a floor that is a property of the market, not of your effort.

The correct mental model is two separate quantities. Idiosyncratic risk is a problem you can solve, cheaply, and you are mostly done by twenty or thirty names. Systematic risk is not a problem diversification addresses at all — it is addressed, if at all, by *how much* you hold rather than *how many*, which is the exposure decision in <Lesson id="373_strategic_allocation_vs_tactical_drift"/>. A book of 200 names is fully exposed to a market-wide decline, and a beginner who diversified their way to 200 names in the belief that they had de-risked is holding the same market risk they started with, plus 200 sets of costs.

## The steelman

The strongest case against the "stop at thirty" reading: ρ is not a constant, and a *deliberately* chosen set of 60 names spanning different economies, currencies and rate sensitivities has a lower average pairwise correlation than a randomly chosen 30 from one index — so the extra names lower the floor itself rather than chasing the flattening curve, which is a different and much better argument for breadth. That is correct, and it is why the table's assumption is stated explicitly as "every pair correlates at 0.35": the floor moves when ρ moves. The falsifier is measurable rather than rhetorical. Compute the average pairwise correlation of the book you actually hold. If adding names has been lowering it, breadth is buying you a lower floor and is worth continuing. If it has been flat or rising — the usual outcome when names are added from whatever is currently working — the extra names are chasing the flat part of the curve, and the table's verdict stands.

## Try it

Open your Portfolio and count your open holdings, then place yourself on the table above. If you hold fewer than five names, the next addition is worth several percentage points of volatility and is the highest-value work available to you. If you hold more than twenty-five, further additions are worth fractions of a point — so ask the Chief Investment Officer in a 1-on-1 what would reduce the *shared* risk in your book instead, and evaluate the answer rather than acting on it. Remember: AMI is a training simulator, and everything it produces is a practice artifact.

## Takeaway

Diversification solves company-specific risk and is roughly finished by thirty names; the market risk underneath is untouched by counting, and only changing your exposure or its drivers moves it.

*Where this comes from: Markowitz's decomposition of portfolio variance into shared and specific components — Portfolio Selection (1952) — with the "how many names" question as Statman (1987) reworked it.*
