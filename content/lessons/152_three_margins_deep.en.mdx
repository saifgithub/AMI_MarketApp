# Three margins, three different stories

Lesson 033 introduced gross, operating, and net margin. This lesson zooms in: each tells you about a *different part* of the cost stack, and which one is moving is more diagnostic than the absolute level. Gross margin reveals product economics and pricing power. Operating margin reveals how efficiently the company runs. Net margin reveals everything else — interest, tax structure, one-time items. By the end of this lesson you should be able to read a margin compression and immediately know *where* it's happening, and what each location implies for the business.

## How this plays out in real markets

Look at NVDA in fiscal 2025. Revenue around $130B, gross margin around 75%, operating margin around 62%, net margin around 56%. That set of numbers tells you three things at once: (1) the gross margin says NVDA sells something with extreme pricing power — the COGS per unit is roughly a quarter of what customers pay; (2) the gap between gross and operating margin (13 percentage points) is R&D, sales, G&A — relatively small at this scale because the operating expense base doesn't scale with units sold; (3) the gap between operating and net margin (6 points) is tax and net interest — modest. The company converts dollars top-to-bottom remarkably efficiently.

Now contrast with WMT in fiscal 2025. Revenue around $681B, gross margin around 24%, operating margin around 4.3%, net margin around 2.6%. Each margin tells a story specific to retail: gross at 24% reflects the markup over wholesale cost; operating at 4.3% reflects how thin the store/logistics operating overhead has to be squeezed for the model to work; net at 2.6% is what's left after interest on $44B of debt and tax. A 100bps shift in gross margin (24% → 23%) wipes out roughly 25% of operating profit because the operating buffer is already so thin.

Here's the diagnostic: imagine WMT's gross margin drops to 22% next quarter while operating margin stays at 4.3%. That would mean the company offset gross compression with cost cuts elsewhere — usually labour or G&A — which is sustainable in the short term but not indefinitely. If instead gross margin holds and operating margin drops, the issue is operating expense growth, not product economics. Same headline EPS miss; very different diagnoses.

On Bursa Malaysia: PUBLIC Bank historically runs net interest margin (the bank-specific analog of gross margin) around 2.2%–2.5%. A 10bps move at that level is meaningful — net interest income shifts roughly 4% on the same loan book.

The falsifiable claim: when gross margin compresses 200+ bps in a single quarter and operating margin holds flat or expands, the company is funding a product-economics issue with overhead cuts. That posture is sustainable for 2–3 quarters at most before something breaks at the operating line.

## The trap

The trap is reading a "margin miss" without identifying *which* margin missed. A beginner sees the headline EPS shortfall and concludes "margins compressed". They miss that a gross-margin miss (pricing pressure, input costs, product mix) is a different disease than an operating-margin miss (overhead growth outrunning revenue) which is a different disease than a net-margin miss (one-time tax or interest event). The correct mental model: every margin compression has an address. Gross = product economics. The gap to operating = overhead efficiency. The gap to net = capital structure and tax. When you see the miss, ask "which of the three lines moved?" before reaching any conclusion about the business.

Highlight any quarter where one margin moved more than 200bps while another stayed flat. Tell me what the divergence suggests." ## Takeaway

A margin miss has an address. Find it before you form an opinion — product, overhead, or capital structure each demand a different response.
