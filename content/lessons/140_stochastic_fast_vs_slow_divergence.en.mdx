# Fast vs slow Stochastic and divergence reads

<Lesson id="139_stochastic_k_d_crosses"/> used the standard %K (14, 3, 3) Stochastic — a 14-bar lookback with %D as a 3-SMA and an additional 3-bar smoothing on %K itself. That "slow" version dampens the raw oscillator. The "fast" Stochastic (14, 3) skips the extra smoothing on %K, producing a jagged line that flips often. Divergence — the same concept <Lesson id="128_rsi_hidden_divergence"/> taught for RSI — works on both, but the fast Stochastic gives earlier, noisier divergence calls and the slow Stochastic gives later, cleaner ones. The trade-off mirrors the SMA-vs-EMA choice from <Lesson id="124_sma_vs_ema_vs_wma"/>: speed costs you in whipsaws.

## How this plays out in real markets

Take a stock printing a new closing high. The slow Stochastic %K reached 84 — barely above the 80 overbought line. At the prior closing high, slow %K had topped at 91. So slow Stochastic showed bearish divergence: higher price, lower oscillator high (84 vs 91).

The fast Stochastic told a louder story. At the prior high, fast %K printed 96. At the new high, fast %K topped at 79 — a 17-point gap, double the slow Stochastic's 7-point divergence. The fast version had been signalling momentum loss for two weeks before the slow version flagged anything.

The stock fell over the next 18 sessions. The slow Stochastic's divergence call was correct; the fast Stochastic's divergence call was the same direction, two weeks earlier, but accompanied by three false divergences in the prior six months that the slow version did not give.

Falsifiable claim: fast Stochastic divergences produce more signals per period but a lower hit rate; slow Stochastic divergences produce fewer signals but a higher hit rate. The optimal use is to read both — fast as an early-warning radar, slow as the confirmation. A divergence visible on both is materially higher conviction than one visible on only the fast.

Invalidation: a fast-only divergence (slow Stochastic still confirming the price high) is not a tradeable signal yet — it's a watchlist trigger. If the slow Stochastic confirms within the next 5 sessions, then the setup is live. If the slow recovers and price keeps trending, the fast divergence was a false alarm.

## The trap

The trap is using only one of the two. Beginners pick fast Stochastic for "responsiveness" and end up trading every wiggle, or pick slow Stochastic for "smoothness" and miss the early warning. Either choice on its own discards information.

The correct mental model: read both in parallel. Fast Stochastic identifies divergence candidates; slow Stochastic confirms or rejects them. The fast version is the lookout calling "ship on the horizon"; the slow version is the captain confirming the heading. Trading the lookout's first call is reckless; ignoring the captain's confirmation is wasteful. Use the pair, not either alone.

## Try it

In the Market Analyst <Term id="ami_one_on_one" />, paste a ticker that's making new highs in a trend and ask: "Compare fast Stochastic (14, 3) and slow Stochastic (14, 3, 3) at the last two swing highs — is there divergence on either, and if so how deep is each?" Then build the habit of checking both whenever you scan for divergence; the pair-read is the edge.

## Takeaway

Fast Stochastic spots divergences earlier and noisier; slow Stochastic confirms them later and cleaner. Use the pair — fast as radar, slow as trigger — and trade only when both agree.
