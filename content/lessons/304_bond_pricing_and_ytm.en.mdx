# Bond pricing & yield-to-maturity

A bond's price moves every day even though its coupon and face value never change — because the market is constantly repricing what that fixed cash flow is worth against current interest rates. Yield-to-maturity (YTM) is the single number that answers "what am I actually earning if I buy at this price and hold to maturity, coupons and all?" After this lesson you'll be able to explain why bond prices and yields move in opposite directions and read a bond price as a verdict on where rates are headed.

## Example

Take a hypothetical corporate bond like ones from JPM or MSFT might carry: face value $1,000, 4% annual coupon (paid semiannually), 10 years to maturity. Run it through two rate environments. If the market's required yield on comparable debt is 5%, this bond's fixed 4% coupon looks stingy — nobody pays full face value for a below-market coupon, so the price falls to **$922.05**. If the market's required yield drops to 3%, this same bond's 4% coupon now looks generous — investors bid the price up to **$1,085.84** to capture it. Run it the other direction: if you observe this bond trading at a market price of $950, the implied YTM is **4.630%** (the discount to par is compensating you for the below-market coupon). If it trades at $1,050, the implied YTM is **3.406%**. This is exactly why the 10-year US Treasury yield moves every time the market reprices Fed policy expectations — the yield and the price are two views of the same number, and every basis point on one is a whole recalculation of the other.

## The trap

Beginners hear "bond prices fell" and assume something went wrong with the issuer — like a stock selling off on bad earnings. That's usually not it. A bond's price falls when *market interest rates rise*, because a fixed 4% coupon becomes less attractive next to a fresh bond paying 5%, full stop — even if the issuer's credit is flawless. The correct mental model: bond price and yield are inversely linked by definition, not by sentiment. When you see a bond price drop, ask "did the issuer's ability to pay change, or did the general level of rates change?" — those are two entirely different explanations for the same price move, and only one of them says anything about credit risk.

## The steelman

A skeptic might argue: "If price and yield always move opposite, then YTM tells me nothing new beyond price — it's a purely mechanical inverse." That's a fair simplification for a single bond, but it breaks down as a portfolio tool. The falsifier: compare two bonds trading at the *same* price but different coupons and maturities — their YTMs will differ. YTM standardizes bonds with different coupon rates and maturities onto one comparable return figure, which raw price cannot do. If YTM were purely redundant with price, professional bond desks wouldn't quote yield as the primary number and back into price — but they do exactly that, because yield is the portable unit across a portfolio of dissimilar bonds.

## Takeaway

Bond price and yield move in lockstep opposite directions for a simple mechanical reason — a fixed coupon has to compete with whatever the market currently pays — so a bond price move is a verdict on rates, not automatically a verdict on credit.

*Where this comes from: standard fixed-income present-value pricing, the same discounting logic used across corporate finance to price any fixed cash-flow stream.*
