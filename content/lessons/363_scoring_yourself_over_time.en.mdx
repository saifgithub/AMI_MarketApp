# Scoring yourself over time

A hit rate is a raw number, and raw numbers lie at small sample sizes. Six correct calls out of ten feels like proof; a fair coin produces that result or better 38% of the time. Before your record establishes anything, it has to clear two bars: it must beat the base rate for the same set of decisions, and it must rest on enough decisions that luck cannot be the explanation. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to state what your own record does and does not establish, and to score calibration across many decisions rather than the last few.

## Example

Suppose your journal holds ten resolved decisions and six went the way you predicted — a 60% hit rate. Ask what a fair coin does with ten flips. Of the 1,024 equally likely sequences, 386 contain six or more heads: 37.7%. More than a third of people flipping coins post a record at least as good as yours. The 95% interval around 6-of-10 runs from roughly 30% to 90%, and it comfortably contains 50%. This record cannot separate you from chance.

Now keep logging. At 100 resolved decisions with the same 60% hit rate, the interval narrows to roughly 50.4% to 69.6%. Identical headline number, completely different claim: the interval width shrinks with the square root of the sample, so 100 decisions just clears the coin where 10 never came close.

Then apply the second bar. Say that over the same period the naive benchmark — every candidate you screened, held over the same horizon, no selection applied — resolved favourably 64% of the time. Your 60% sits below the number you'd have got by not selecting at all. The sample is finally large enough to say something, and what it says is that the point estimate has your selection giving back about four points. That is a useful finding. It is not the finding the raw 60% advertised.

## The trap

The trap is resulting on your own record: you go 6-for-10, conclude the approach works, and size up. It feels like evidence because the outcomes are real — they just aren't numerous enough to carry the weight put on them. The twin error is the same mistake inverted: a 4-for-10 stretch is not proof the approach is broken, and abandoning a process on that basis discards a method you never tested. The correct mental model is that a hit rate is an estimate with an error bar attached, and only decisions shrink it. Hit rate alone also hides calibration — being right 60% of the time while claiming 90% confidence is a different failure from being right 60% while claiming 60%, which is why <Lesson id="358_what_a_confidence_number_means"/> scores the number you stated, not just the outcome. Forecasting research identifies its best forecasters over hundreds of scored predictions, never a hot week.

## Try it

Open the Decision Journal and count how many entries have actually resolved — not how many you have logged, how many have outcomes. Write that number at the top of your review notes and treat every rate computed from it as provisional until it grows. Then, in a 1-on-1 with the Research Manager, ask AMI what benchmark your hit rate should be measured against for the horizon you work in. Remember: AMI is a training simulator, and its output is a practice artifact, not investment advice.

## Takeaway

A hit rate is an estimate with an error bar: 6-or-better in 10 is what a coin does 38% of the time. Score yourself against a base rate, across enough decisions that luck cannot be the explanation — and until you have them, "I don't know yet" is the accurate answer.
