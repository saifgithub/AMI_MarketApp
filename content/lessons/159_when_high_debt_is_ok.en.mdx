# When high debt is OK

<Lesson id="034_debt_and_the_balance_sheet"/> set debt-to-EBITDA >4x and interest coverage <2x as a stress configuration. This lesson is the necessary counterweight: certain business models *should* carry that level of leverage, and judging them by industrial-company thresholds will reject the entire sector. Regulated utilities, REITs, banks, and infrastructure operators run on contractual or regulated revenue with low operating volatility — making predictable cash flow available to service a much higher debt load than a cyclical or growth business could safely carry. By the end of this lesson you should know which sectors break the <Lesson id="034_debt_and_the_balance_sheet"/> thresholds by design, what the adjusted thresholds look like for each, and the falsifiable signal that even these "safe" leveraged businesses are turning stressed.

## How this plays out in real markets

**Regulated utilities** are the cleanest example. SO (Southern Company), DUK (Duke Energy), and AEP (American Electric Power) all carry debt-to-EBITDA in the 5–6x range — well above the industrial stress threshold — and have done so for decades without incident. The reason: utilities recover their cost of capital through regulated rate cases, which means the regulator effectively guarantees that the utility can earn a return on its rate base sufficient to service its debt. Revenue is contractually predictable, demand is inelastic, and the regulator has a structural incentive to keep the utility solvent.

Industry adjusted thresholds: debt-to-EBITDA up to 6x is normal at integrated utilities; interest coverage of 2.5–3x is fine (vs the 2x stress floor for industrials).

**REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)** routinely run debt-to-EBITDA in the 5–8x range. REITs are required by tax law to distribute 90% of taxable income as dividends, leaving them with limited retained cash. They fund growth through a mix of debt and equity issuance. The relevant ratios are debt/total assets (typically 35–55%) and debt/gross book value, not industrial-style leverage. Look at the loan-to-value ratio (LTV) on the underlying property portfolio — that's the equivalent risk metric. An LTV above 60% in commercial real estate is the stress flag.

**Banks** are the extreme case. Banks are structurally leveraged 10–15x equity (assets/equity) because their business is intermediating capital. Conventional debt-to-EBITDA does not apply; the relevant ratios are CET1 capital ratio (regulators require ≥4.5%, most large banks hold 11–14%), tangible common equity / tangible assets, and the supplementary leverage ratio.

**Infrastructure / pipelines / regulated transport** sit in the 4–6x debt-to-EBITDA range as a structural norm. Long-life assets, regulated or contracted revenue, and predictable cash flow let them carry leverage that would crush a software or retail business.

Real numerical example: TENAGA on Bursa Malaysia, as a regulated utility, has historically run debt-to-EBITDA in the 4–5x range with interest coverage near 3x. That same configuration at, say, MRDIY (variety retail) would be a flashing red light; at TENAGA it's a sector-typical structure for a regulated electricity transmission and distribution business with periodically reset tariffs.

The falsifiable claim: regulated utilities, REITs, and infrastructure operators tolerate debt-to-EBITDA up to ~6x as a baseline without stress, provided (a) cash-flow volatility is contractually contained, (b) the regulatory or contractual framework that supports the cash flow has not been altered, and (c) interest coverage stays above 2.5x. When any one of those three conditions breaks — deregulation, contract renegotiation, or coverage falling toward 2x — the sector premium on leverage disappears and the company should be evaluated by industrial-company rules.

## The trap

The trap is rejecting an entire sector because its leverage looks "high" by industrial standards. A beginner sees a utility at 5.5x debt-to-EBITDA and concludes "too levered". They miss that the same 5.5x is the sector median and that the company would underperform peers — and underfund growth — if it tried to operate at industrial-style 2x leverage. The mirror trap is more dangerous: assuming any leveraged sector is "safe" because the regulator or contract is presumed permanent. The regulatory framework that supports utility leverage can change (rate-case denials, structural deregulation), and contracted pipeline revenue can be renegotiated. The correct mental model: structural leverage is sector-appropriate when the cash-flow predictability that supports it is intact. Always verify the contractual or regulatory framework that justifies the leverage, and watch for the warning signs that the framework is weakening.

## Takeaway

High debt is OK when the cash flow supporting it is predictable — but only as long as that's true. Read the regulatory or contractual framework before you read the leverage ratio, and watch for the conditions that make the framework break.
