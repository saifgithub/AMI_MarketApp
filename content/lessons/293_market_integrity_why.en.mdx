# Why market integrity matters

A market is a machine for turning honest information into prices. Every quote you see assumes the filings are real, the volume is real, and nobody traded on what you couldn't know. When that assumption breaks, the machine doesn't just misprice one stock — investors start charging every stock for the risk of being cheated. After this lesson you'll be able to explain how integrity failures show up as losses, and read integrity risk the way you read any other position risk.

## How this plays out in real markets

Enron traded at $90.75 in August 2000, a top-ten US company by market value. Its reported profits ran through off-balance-sheet entities designed so shareholders couldn't see the debt. When the structure surfaced, the stock went to under $1 by late November 2001 and the company filed for bankruptcy that December. Employee retirement accounts holding Enron stock were wiped out alongside outside shareholders — tens of billions of dollars of market value gone, not because a business cycle turned, but because the numbers were never real.

The damage didn't stop at one ticker. Auditor Arthur Andersen collapsed, Congress passed Sarbanes-Oxley, and for years investors applied a "trust discount" to any company with complex reporting. On Bursa Malaysia, the 1MDB scandal (the US Department of Justice traced roughly $4.5 billion misappropriated) weighed on foreign participation in Malaysian markets for years. Integrity failures are systemic: honest companies pay higher costs of capital because a dishonest one poisoned the well.

## The trap

Beginners file ethics under "compliance paperwork" — rules for professionals that don't touch a retail P&L. It feels right because integrity failures are invisible until they detonate; nothing in a candlestick chart shows you the books are fake. The correct mental model: **integrity risk is position risk.** A stock whose numbers can't be trusted can gap down 90% on a single disclosure, straight through any stop-loss. You can't diversify away a fraud you're holding, and you can't manage a risk you've defined as someone else's problem.

## The steelman

A serious counterargument exists: some economists have argued that insider trading makes markets *more* efficient — insiders trading on real information moves prices toward truth faster than waiting for disclosure. The falsifier is measurable: if that were the whole story, markets with weak enforcement would trade at normal valuations. They don't. Markets where outsiders expect to be picked off show wider bid-ask spreads, thinner participation, and persistent valuation discounts — investors price the cheating risk and pay less for everything. Efficiency for the insider is a tax on everyone else.

## Try it

In 1-on-1 with the PM, ask: "What does the safety floor check before a simulated trade passes?" Map each check the PM names to a form of trust — that positions are sized honestly against the mandate, that constraints can't be talked around. AMI's safety floor is a working model of what a market integrity function does: it doesn't trust claims, it verifies them.

## Takeaway

Price discovery only works when the inputs are honest. When integrity goes, the discount arrives before the headlines do — so price integrity risk like any other risk you hold.

*Where this comes from: the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (2014) — the profession's baseline case that market integrity is what makes investing investable.*
