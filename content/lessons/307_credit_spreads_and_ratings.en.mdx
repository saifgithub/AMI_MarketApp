# Credit spreads & ratings

Two bonds with the same maturity can pay very different yields, and the gap between them — the credit spread — is the market's real-time price on default risk. Credit rating agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) grade issuers from safest to riskiest, and the spread between a risky issuer's yield and a same-maturity government bond widens or narrows as the market's confidence in getting repaid changes. After this lesson you'll be able to read a credit spread as a risk gauge and explain why spreads widen exactly when you'd least want to be caught holding risky debt.

## Example

S&P, Moody's, and Fitch rate bonds on a letter scale — AAA/Aaa down through BBB-/Baa3 is "investment grade," and anything below that is "high yield" or "junk," compensating investors for materially higher default risk with a materially higher coupon. The credit spread is the extra yield a risky issuer pays over a same-maturity Treasury: a stable investment-grade corporate might trade 100-150 basis points over Treasuries in calm markets, while high-yield issuers can trade several hundred basis points over. The clearest real-world illustration is 2008: credit spreads on corporate debt — especially financial-sector and high-yield bonds — widened dramatically as the market repriced default risk in real time, well before most rating agency downgrades caught up; spreads are a faster-moving, more market-driven signal than ratings, which update on a slower committee cycle. The same logic applies on Bursa Malaysia and the broader Islamic finance market: a sukuk issued by a lower-rated corporate will price at a spread over Malaysian Government Securities or an equivalent sovereign benchmark, for exactly the same default-risk reason, even though the sukuk structure avoids conventional interest.

## The trap

Beginners treat a credit rating as a fixed, trustworthy label — "it's rated A, so it's safe" — and stop there. Two problems: ratings change slower than reality (agencies review periodically, not continuously, and 2008 showed AAA-rated structured products could be catastrophically mispriced), and a rating is a category, not a number you can compare precisely across issuers within the same tier. The correct mental model: use the rating as a starting filter, but read the *spread* as the market's live, continuously updated verdict on default risk — if a spread is widening while the rating hasn't moved yet, the market is telling you something the rating agency hasn't caught up to.

## The steelman

A skeptic might argue: "Spreads are noisy and can widen for reasons that have nothing to do with the specific issuer's default risk — a broad market liquidity crunch pushes even solid credits' spreads wider, so treating spread-widening as a pure default-risk signal is overreading it." That's a fair and important qualifier. The falsifier: compare the widening across issuers of different quality during the same stress period. If spreads widen roughly proportionally across investment-grade and high-yield alike, that's a liquidity/risk-appetite story, not an issuer-specific default story. If low-rated issuers' spreads widen dramatically more than high-rated issuers' during the same window — which is exactly what happened in 2008 — that differential is the genuine default-risk signal, distinct from the market-wide liquidity effect layered on top of it.

## Takeaway

A credit rating is a slow-moving category; a credit spread is the market's live, continuously repricing verdict on default risk — read the spread first, and treat a rating as a starting filter, not the final word.
