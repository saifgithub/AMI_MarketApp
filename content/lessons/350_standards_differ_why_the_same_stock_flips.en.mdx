# Standards differ — why the same stock flips

The same company can be Sharia-compliant under one standard and non-compliant under another, at the exact same moment, with nothing about the company having changed. AAOIFI, Bursa Malaysia's Shariah Advisory Council (SAC), and the major index providers (Dow Jones Islamic, S&P Shariah, MSCI Islamic, FTSE Shariah) don't share one identical rulebook — they differ on thresholds, on whether the debt/cash denominator is total assets or market cap, and on whether market cap is a single-day snapshot or a trailing average. AMI's job is to name which standard produced a given verdict, never to adjudicate which standard is "more correct." By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain at least two concrete sources of this disagreement.

## Example

Bursa Malaysia's SAC methodology is widely understood to run more leniently than the AAOIFI/GCC approach on some criteria — historically screening a materially larger share of Bursa-listed stocks as Sharia-compliant than an AAOIFI-style pass would produce on the identical company set. Part of the reason: different standards specify different denominators for the same ratio — some measure interest-bearing debt against **total assets**, others against **market capitalization**, and a company with a large market cap relative to its balance sheet gets a much smaller ratio under the market-cap denominator than under total assets. Separately, the choice between a **single trading-day** market cap snapshot and a **12-month trailing average** market cap changes the outcome for any stock whose price has moved sharply — a stock priced high on the snapshot day looks safely compliant, while the same company checked against its trailing-average (lower) price during a downturn can cross the same 33% line. Neither number is "wrong" — they're two different, both-published methodologies answering a slightly different question.

## The trap

Beginners see a stock listed as "halal" on one screener and "non-compliant" on another and conclude someone made a mistake. That's the wrong frame. The correct mental model: **name the standard, don't adjudicate it.** AMI's role is to show which methodology (AAOIFI, Bursa SAC, or a named index rulebook) produced a given verdict and why — never to declare one standard the "real" one and the other wrong. Where standards genuinely disagree, present the disagreement plainly and defer the final call to the user's own qualified Sharia authority; that is the load-bearing frame this whole module operates under.

## The steelman

A skeptic might push further: "If the standards disagree this much, isn't the whole screening exercise just picking whichever number you want?" That's a real tension, not a strawman — reasonable scholars and standard-setting bodies genuinely differ on thresholds and denominators, and no single number commands universal consensus. The falsifier: despite the disagreement, every major standard converges on the *same four prohibitions* (lesson 1) and the *same three-ratio structure* (lesson 3) — debt, liquidity, income, each capped near a third or a twentieth. The disagreement is about calibration at the margin (which denominator, which snapshot), not about whether the screening framework itself is coherent. A company deeply outside every standard's range (like the debt-heavy example in lesson 3) fails under all of them; the flipping cases are concentrated near the boundary, which is exactly where "present the disagreement, don't adjudicate" earns its keep.

## Takeaway

When a stock's halal status differs across standards, the discipline is to name which methodology produced which result — never to pick a winner.

*Where this comes from: AAOIFI's and Bursa Malaysia SAC's published screening methodologies; the Dow Jones Islamic, S&P Shariah, MSCI Islamic, and FTSE Shariah index rulebooks, each of which documents its own thresholds and denominators.*
