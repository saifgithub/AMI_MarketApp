# PEG ratio — paying the right price for growth

Lesson 039 introduced PEG — P/E divided by expected earnings-growth rate — as a way to read multiples against the growth they're pricing. This lesson nails down the mechanics: when PEG = 1.0 is genuinely fair, when PEG = 0.5 isn't actually a bargain, and when PEG breaks entirely. By the end you should be able to compute PEG, choose the right growth input (this is where most readers go wrong), and recognise the specific business profile where the ratio is meaningless.

## How this plays out in real markets

NVDA mid-2026: trailing P/E 42x, consensus next-three-year EPS CAGR ~40%. PEG = 42 / 40 ≈ 1.05. Peter Lynch's rough rule of thumb — PEG around 1.0 is fair, below 0.75 is interesting, above 1.5 is rich — would call NVDA fairly priced for its growth. AAPL: P/E 32x, next-three-year EPS CAGR ~7%. PEG = 32 / 7 ≈ 4.6. By PEG, AAPL is paying steep multiples for muted growth — far less attractive than NVDA despite the lower headline P/E.

Now a counterexample. SNOW (Snowflake) trading at forward P/E 80x with expected 30% growth gives PEG = 2.7. By Lynch's rule, expensive. But Snowflake's gross margins are 70%+ and net retention runs above 130% — most of the "growth" is locked-in expansion within existing customers, not customer acquisition. The PEG ratio doesn't weight earnings quality; a dollar of high-net-retention SaaS growth is more valuable than a dollar of one-shot project revenue growth. PEG flags Snowflake as expensive when a more nuanced read might call it fairly priced — the ratio strips out everything except growth rate.

Falsifiable claim: PEG is most reliable when expected earnings growth is (a) between roughly 8% and 30% annually, AND (b) durable for at least three years. Below 8%, the ratio explodes mathematically — a 30x stock with 2% growth gives PEG = 15, which is technically true but uninformative. Above 30%, growth is rarely durable enough for the ratio to be meaningful at face value. On Bursa: TENAGA at P/E 13x with 3% expected EPS growth gives PEG ≈ 4.3 — looks bad, but utilities aren't supposed to grow earnings; the ratio simply doesn't fit the business model.

## The trap

The trap is computing PEG with the wrong growth input. Most readers grab next-year EPS growth from a finance site, divide P/E by that, and call it done. But next-year growth is noisy — a single one-off cost can make next-year growth look 40% when the underlying business compounds at 12%. The correct mental model: use a multi-year CAGR (typically three to five years of expected EPS growth), not single-year. And use *sustainable* growth, not one-shot bounces. A retailer reopening stores post-pandemic might show 60% expected EPS growth next year while the long-run rate is 8%; PEG on the 60% number says "buy", PEG on the 8% says "avoid", and the 8% is the honest signal. What invalidates the ratio entirely: businesses with very low growth (<5%) or those where earnings are not the right metric (banks where ROE matters more, REITs where AFFO matters, early-stage software where revenue growth matters before earnings exist).

## Takeaway

PEG only works when growth is durable and in the 8–30% range. Use multi-year CAGR, never single-year — and recognise that for dividend-heavy or low-growth businesses, PEG is the wrong tool entirely.
