# Paid-influencer disclosure failures

A finfluencer who is paid to promote a stock or platform must disclose that payment under most major regulators' rules. Most do not — or bury the disclosure where viewers will not see it. This lesson teaches the disclosure obligations under the SEC, FCA, ASIC, and SC Malaysia regimes, and the three structural patterns that signal an undisclosed paid promotion: a sudden ticker pivot, a platform-affiliate funnel, and a "results" video tied to a particular broker referral link. Once you recognise the pattern, you can discount the content.

## How this plays out in real markets

The SEC's anti-touting rule (Section 17(b) of the Securities Act of 1933) requires disclosure of compensation when promoting a security. The SEC has settled multiple cases since 2022 against influencers and celebrities who promoted stocks or tokens without disclosing payments. The FCA's 2024 finfluencer guidance — and parallel guidance from ASIC and SC Malaysia — extends the same disclosure principle to social-media promotion within their jurisdictions.

Patterns that recur in enforcement filings:

- An influencer with a broad market-commentary channel suddenly publishes a video focused on a single small-cap ticker, with bullish framing and a price target. No company-news catalyst justifies the pivot. The video coincides with a pump in the underlying.
- An influencer who has never mentioned a specific brokerage suddenly produces a "my broker setup" video that funnels viewers to a referral link with a bonus. The bonus typically pays the influencer a percentage of the referred deposit, sometimes a percentage of the deposited losses.
- A "trade results" video shows a screenshot of profits on a specific platform. The screenshot is from a demo account or a different account than the one being promoted. The platform pays the influencer per signup.
- Disclosure language exists but is buried at second 47 of a 6-minute video, in 8-point grey-on-grey text, or in a description users do not expand. Regulators treat hidden disclosure as no disclosure.

The SEC's investor.gov page on "celebrity-backed investments" and the FCA's ScamSmart influencer guidance both publish examples and the specific rule citations.

## The trap

The trap is parasocial trust. The viewer has watched the influencer for months, the influencer feels like a friend, and the brain treats their recommendation as a friend's opinion rather than a paid advertisement. The mistake is that parasocial duration is unrelated to content veracity — the influencer can be honest for 100 videos and paid on the 101st, and the viewer has no way to distinguish them without a disclosure. The fix is to assume a single-ticker focus video or a broker referral video carries a payment unless the disclosure is plainly stated, on screen, in the first 30 seconds, and the influencer can show the contract terms on request. Most cannot.

## Takeaway

If a single-ticker video, a broker referral funnel, and a platform-tied profit screenshot cluster together without clear first-minute disclosure, assume a paid promotion. Parasocial trust is not a substitute for the disclosure regulators require.
