# The 'explain to a 6-year-old' framework

Most trade rationalisations collapse the moment you try to explain them in plain language. The 'explain to a 6-year-old' framework is a single-sentence test: before any entry, you must be able to say *what the trade is, why it should work, and what would prove it wrong* using vocabulary a six-year-old would understand. If you cannot, the trade is not a thesis — it is a feeling with industry jargon attached. After this lesson you will be able to apply the test to your own pending entries, recognise the specific phrasings that fail it, and use the failure as a pre-entry circuit breaker.

## How this plays out in real markets

A trader pre-entry test for NVDA, October 2023: "NVDA makes the chips that AI computers need. Companies are buying lots of these chips. I think the stock will go up because more people want these chips than the company can make. I'll know I'm wrong if the price falls below $100 because that means the chart says the buyers have stopped." Plain language, three parts: what, why, what-invalidates. Trade executed. Win.

Same trader pre-entry test, three weeks later, on a different ticker: "This is a position in a company that has good momentum in a sector with structural tailwinds and the multiple is reasonable given the growth profile and the technicals look constructive with the recent breakout above the prior consolidation range." That sentence is 38 words of professional vocabulary and zero plain-language thesis. He could not pass the six-year-old test. He took the trade anyway. The trade closed -7%. The post-trade journal entry, rewritten in plain language, reads: "I bought a stock that had been going up, because I wanted to be in a stock that had been going up." That is not a thesis; that is FOMO with vocabulary.

The falsifiable test: can you write the entry rationale in three sentences using only words a six-year-old knows? Sentence 1 — what the company does. Sentence 2 — why the price should go up. Sentence 3 — what would prove the trade wrong. If any of the three sentences requires more than 25 words or any term you would not use with a child, the thesis is incomplete or you are rationalising.

## The trap

The trap is the belief that complexity equals sophistication. It does not. Most real edges are simple — a structural pattern, a clear catalyst, a placeable invalidation. The complexity in most failed trades is rhetorical, not analytical: it lets the trader feel like they have done research when they have actually accumulated jargon. The correct mental model: simplicity is the audit. If you cannot say the trade plainly, you have not finished thinking — or you are concealing from yourself that the thinking did not reach a thesis. The Portfolio Manager's <Term id="ami_mandate" /> enforcement runs on simple rules for exactly this reason: every <Term id="ami_pm_safety_floor" /> rule can be stated in one plain-language sentence. The traders who do best long-term apply the same standard to their entries.

## Try it

Before your next entry, write three sentences in plain language: (1) what the company does, (2) why the price should move in your direction, (3) what specific price level or event would prove the trade wrong. Read them out loud. If any sentence runs over 25 words or uses terminology you would not use with a child, rewrite it; if you cannot rewrite it without losing the argument, the argument was not there. Then open a <Term id="ami_one_on_one" /> with the Portfolio Manager and ask: "Given this three-sentence thesis, does my proposed position size match the clarity of the invalidation?" If the invalidation is vague, the position size must be small or the trade must wait.

## Takeaway

Simplicity is the audit. If you cannot explain the trade in three plain sentences — what, why, what-invalidates — you have not finished thinking, and the trade is rationalisation, not thesis.
