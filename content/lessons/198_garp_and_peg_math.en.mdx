# GARP and the PEG ratio — growth at a reasonable price

<Lesson id="044_earnings_growth"/> introduced earnings growth and mentioned PEG (P/E divided by growth rate) as the bridge between the multiple and the growth that has to justify it. This lesson goes deep on the Growth-At-a-Reasonable-Price (GARP) framework popularised by Peter Lynch and refined by countless practitioners. It's a discipline that rejects both the deep-value trap (cheap multiple, no growth) and the growth-at-any-price trap (high multiple, growth that won't compound for long enough to matter). By the end of this lesson you should be able to compute PEG, apply the GARP triple-filter, and name the precise failure mode of PEG that requires layering on the rest of the financial-ratio toolkit.

## How this plays out in real markets

The basic math: PEG = (P/E) / (expected earnings growth rate in percent). A PEG of 1.0 is "fairly priced for growth", below 1.0 is "the bet", above 2.0 is "the warning".

Apply it to NVDA at the end of 2024. Forward P/E roughly 35x. Consensus forward 3-year EPS growth roughly 30% annually. PEG = 35 / 30 ≈ 1.17. The math says "moderately expensive but defensible if growth holds". The falsifiability comes from the growth rate — if NVDA delivers 30%, PEG was approximately right; if growth decelerates to 15%, the implied PEG was 2.3 and the market over-paid.

Apply it to META in early 2023, when the stock had collapsed to roughly $90 with a forward P/E near 14x. Consensus growth was being aggressively cut, but META's own EPS growth subsequently exceeded 30% in 2023 and 2024 as the cost-cutting and the AI infrastructure ramp paid off. The PEG at the trough was effectively below 0.5 looking back. That's the GARP buy signal — a P/E that has overshot to the downside relative to the actual growth that the business delivers.

Apply it to a deep-value name. GM trades at a P/E around 5-6x with expected EPS growth in the low single digits. PEG is around 2-3x — high despite the cheap absolute multiple, because the growth simply isn't there. Cheap and stagnant is not GARP; it's value-trap territory. The opposite mistake from over-paying for growth.

The GARP triple-filter as practised by serious investors:
1. **PEG below 1.5x** (with PEG below 1.0x being the strong-buy zone).
2. **Earnings growth above the inflation rate + risk-free rate** (a minimum bar — anything growing slower than money in T-bills is liquidating in real terms).
3. **ROE or ROIC above the cost of capital** so that the growth is value-creating (a 20% growth rate at 5% ROIC destroys value; same growth at 25% ROIC creates it).

On Bursa Malaysia: MAYBANK trades at a P/E around 12x with mid-single-digit growth, PEG around 2-3x — closer to value than to GARP. IHH Healthcare with double-digit growth at a similar multiple often has a more favourable PEG, depending on the cycle.

Falsifiable claim: a stock with PEG below 1.0x AND ROE above 15% AND consensus forward growth above 10% has historically outperformed broad indices on a forward 3-year basis with statistical significance, in studies covering both US and developed-market equities. The signal invalidates when the growth rate is forecast-driven rather than evidence-driven — a company guiding to 25% growth that has never delivered above 8% has a PEG that's fictional.

## The trap

The trap is treating PEG as a magic formula. A PEG of 0.8x looks like a buy until you ask: "where did the growth rate in the denominator come from?" Sell-side consensus growth rates are notorious for being too high in early forecasting and getting cut as deadlines approach. A backward-looking PEG using trailing growth is conservative; a forward PEG using sell-side estimates is often wishful. The correct mental model: PEG is a screen, the growth-rate input is the assumption, and the discipline is to use the lower of (a) trailing 3-year actual growth, (b) consensus forward growth, and (c) management's own guidance, then apply a margin of safety. What invalidates PEG as a standalone metric: GARP fails for companies with one or two years of unusual growth that won't repeat (a post-pandemic snapback, a cyclical commodity year) — always check that the growth rate plugged in is sustainable, not a one-year artefact.

## Takeaway

GARP is the discipline of not over-paying for growth and not under-paying for stagnation. PEG is the screen; trailing-growth input plus the ROE-vs-cost-of-capital quality check is what makes it honest.
